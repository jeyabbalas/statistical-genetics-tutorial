Measuring phenotypes

In an analysis, a phenotype is one column y with one value per participant. Every value in that column was produced by a measurement process: a question on a touchscreen, a blood pressure monitor, a laboratory analyzer running on a sample frozen years earlier, or a diagnosis code entered in a hospital record. This page follows those processes in UK Biobank and shows what each one does to the numbers you analyze.

Mental model

Every phenotype column is the output of a measurement process

Every phenotype column is the output of a measurement process. Before you analyze it, ask who was measured, when, how, and which errors, gaps and selection the process added.

  • Five channels. UK Biobank phenotypes come from self-report, physical measurements, assays of stored samples, linked health records, and imaging and wearables. Each has its own timing and typical problems.
  • Decode before you analyze. Special codes such as −3 (“Prefer not to answer”) are not numbers, questions can be skipped by design, and medication changes measured values such as LDL cholesterol and blood pressure.
  • Disease status is constructed. A case definition chooses codes, record sources, a time window and a control group. Each choice changes who counts as a case and how large a genetic association looks.
  • Time is part of the phenotype. Baseline versus follow-up, prevalent versus incident cases, and censoring when follow-up ends.
  • Errors, gaps and selection have predictable effects. Depending on where they occur, they cost power or bias estimates.

On the overview page, the genotype–phenotype data matrix has a phenotype column y and covariate columns next to the genotype matrix. The previous page showed how the genotype columns are made from DNA. This page shows how the phenotype columns are made, and the next page shows the files and tables that hold them.

Kinds of phenotype variables Core

The genotype-to-phenotype page introduced quantitative and binary traits. Real phenotype columns come in a few more types, and the type decides which regression model an association test uses.

An ordinal variable has categories with a natural order but no fixed distance between them; a categorical variable has categories with no order. A time-to-event outcome records how long each person was followed and whether the event happened during that time. Censoring means that follow-up ended before the event was seen: the person died of something else, left the study, or reached the last date for which records are complete. A censored time is a lower bound on the time to the event, not a missing value. Cox regression models the hazard, the rate at which the event occurs among people still at risk, and reports a hazard ratio: the factor by which one extra copy of an allele multiplies that rate.

In the data Declaring the phenotype type in REGENIE

REGENIE, the association program used in Session 2, asks you to declare the type of y: --qt for quantitative traits (the default), --bt for binary traits coded 0 = control, 1 = case, NA = missing, and --t2e for time-to-event traits with an event column coded 0 = censored, 1 = event. With --apply-rint it applies a rank-based inverse normal transformation to quantitative traits: each value is replaced by the standard normal quantile of its rank, which removes skew and limits the pull of outliers1. Large lipid and protein studies used this kind of transformation2,3.

Self-report: questionnaires and interviews Core

Self-reported data are answers that participants give about themselves. At the baseline visit (2006–2010) each participant completed a touchscreen questionnaire and then a short interview, in which trained staff recorded illnesses, operations and medicines5. Online questionnaires followed in later years. The UK Biobank page describes the visit itself. Some typical self-report fields:

Three features of these fields matter for analysis.

  • Special codes. Negative values are answers, not quantities: −1 means “Do not know” and −3 “Prefer not to answer” in many fields, and some fields add others, such as −7 “None of the above”9,10. They must become missing values (NA), or a category of their own, before any arithmetic. A mean of alcohol codes that includes −3 has no meaning.
  • Skip logic. The touchscreen showed some questions only after certain answers. Field 1249 (past tobacco smoking) was not asked of people who said they smoke on most or all days, so an empty 1249 for them means “not asked”, not “unknown”6.
  • Derived fields. UK Biobank combines raw answers into summary fields. Smoking status (Field 20116) is derived from Fields 1239 and 1249 by a fixed rule, which the figure below writes as a lookup table.

Recall. Self-report depends on memory and on what people were told by their doctors. Dates are approximate: Field 20008 stores the year a participant gave, placed at mid-year (a reply of 1970 is stored as 1970.5), or the mid-point of the age they gave8. Reported diagnoses can be wrong in both directions. For MI found by self-report alone, UK Biobank describes the positive predictive value (PPV), the share of identified cases that truly have the disease, as uncertain but likely lower than for MI confirmed in health records11.

Common confusion “Prefer not to answer” is not “No”

Treating −3 as a number, or merging it with 0 (“No”), puts people who declined into a category they did not choose. Recode special values to NA, or keep them as an explicit category, and report how many there were. Free text needs care too: in Field 20003, negative codes mark medicines entered as free text9.

In the data One field, many columns

A data-field is one question or measurement, identified by its Field ID. It can fill several columns: one per instance (the visit: 0 = baseline, 1 = first repeat visit, 2 = first imaging visit) and one per array index (several answers at the same visit, such as the up to 37 illnesses of Field 20002). On the Research Analysis Platform, Field 1558 at the baseline visit is the column p1558_i0; files from earlier years call it 1558-0.012. Each coded field points to a data-coding, the lookup table from codes to meanings: Field 1558 uses one in which −3 means “Prefer not to answer”7. The next page gives the naming rules, including how fields with several answers per visit are stored.

Physical measurements Core

Physical measurements are taken by staff with instruments, following a written protocol. At the baseline visit everyone had, among other measurements, standing height (Field 50, measured with a Seca 202 device), weight (Field 21002) and two automated blood pressure readings5,13. Body mass index, BMI = weight (kg) / height (m)², is a derived field (21001), and it is empty if either input was omitted13.

Blood pressure shows several measurement issues at once. Field 4080 (systolic blood pressure, automated reading) holds two readings taken a few moments apart with an Omron device, stored as array index 0 and 1 of the same instance. A manual sphygmomanometer was used if the automated device could not be used, and those readings are in a separate field (93)13. A common approach averages the available readings and then adds a fixed amount for people who report taking blood pressure medication, because treatment lowers the measured value. A blood pressure GWAS of over one million people, including UK Biobank, averaged the two readings and added 15 mmHg to systolic and 10 mmHg to diastolic pressure for the 94,289 UK Biobank participants taking such medication14. A simulation study of this problem found that adding a sensible constant to treated people’s values works well, whereas the common alternatives (ignoring treatment, adding treatment as a covariate, or excluding treated people) are flawed15.

In the data Two readings, one value

The columns p4080_i0_a0 and p4080_i0_a1 hold the two baseline readings, and p4080_i1_a0 is the first reading at the repeat visit. Average within an instance, never across instances: the visits are years apart. Blood pressure medication is code 2 in Field 6177 (men) or 6153 (women). Both allow several answers, which are spread over array columns, so check all of them9.

Biomarkers from stored samples Core

A biomarker is a measurable substance or property, here in blood or urine, that reflects a biological state: cholesterol, glucose or a protein, for example. In UK Biobank, blood was drawn at the visit and kept cool; at a central laboratory it was spun, split into small tubes (aliquots) and frozen, on average 24 ± 2.5 hours after the blood draw16. Most assays were run years later on thawed aliquots. The UK Biobank page describes the tubes and their handling, and the samples-to-genotypes page follows the same samples to DNA.

Each step adds issues of its own:

  • Fasting. Blood was not taken fasting; Field 74 records the hours since the participant last ate or drank (median 3 hours at baseline)18,21. After a usual meal, average triglycerides rise by up to about 0.3 mmol/L and LDL cholesterol falls by up to about 0.2 mmol/L22, so fasting time is a candidate covariate for markers that change after meals.
  • Handling. Some serum samples were accidentally diluted while aliquots were made, probably by water from the equipment. UK Biobank estimates that 92% of results are within about 1% of an undiluted sample and 8.3% are diluted by up to 10%, and it corrected the results for the aliquot used16.
  • Limits of the assay. Each assay measures reliably only within a range. For LDL the reportable range is 0.26–10.3 mmol/L. A result above it was diluted and re-analyzed when enough sample remained; otherwise, and for results below the range, the value is left missing and quality-control fields record why (Fields 30785 and 30786)18,23. Protein assays have a limit of detection, the lowest level that can be told apart from a blank sample. In the UK Biobank Olink data, 73.2% of protein assays had fewer than 30% of samples below the limit, so the other 26.8% had 30% or more below it3.
  • Batches and plates. Samples are run in batches over months or years, on several machines, and for Olink on plates of 88 participant samples. A shift between batches can look like biology. UK Biobank records the assay date and aliquot in quality-control fields, and the protein study reported little variation due to batch or plate after its quality control3,18.
In the data LDL and its quality-control fields

LDL cholesterol is Field 30780, in mmol/L. To convert to mg/dL, the unit used in the United States, multiply by 38.67 (the molar mass of cholesterol, 386.7 g/mol, divided by 10)24. Fields 30781–30786 give the assay date, the aliquot, any correction applied, a missing reason and reportability. A missing LDL value usually comes with a reason: Field 30785 has one for about 24,800 participants, coded for example 1 “No data returned” or 2 “Original value above or below reportable limit”18.

Medication changes the measured value: statins and LDL

Statins lower LDL cholesterol. People with high LDL are the ones most likely to be prescribed a statin, so the measured LDL of statin users is lower than their untreated LDL would be, and the reduction is concentrated among people with high values. Statin use can be found in Fields 6177 and 6153 (code 1, “Cholesterol lowering medication”) or in the medicine codes of Field 20003, for example 1140861958 simvastatin and 1141146234 atorvastatin9.

The figure below simulates 40,000 people and one LDL-raising allele with a true effect of 0.15 mmol/L per copy; about 19% of the people take a statin that lowers LDL by 30% on average. It compares five ways of handling statin users. Ignoring treatment shrinks the estimate by more than a third. Dropping statin users, or adding statin use as a covariate, also gives a biased answer, because who takes a statin depends on LDL itself. Dividing statin users’ measured LDL by 0.7, the approach of a large global lipid GWAS2, nearly recovers the true effect. It works so well here partly because the simulated statins lower LDL by 30% on average; real effects differ by drug, dose and adherence.

Lecture 1 Slides 15–16 · Two discoveries that rest on LDL measurements

In Lecture 1 (slides 15–16) you will see two discoveries that rest on LDL measurements. Going from phenotype to genotype, researchers found a healthy person with extremely low LDL whose three siblings also had very low LDL; sequencing showed that the siblings carried two copies of a rare loss-of-function variant in ANGPTL3. Going from genotype to phenotype, researchers who knew that variation in PCSK9 influences LDL screened for new variants and found a healthy person with extremely low LDL who was homozygous for an extremely rare loss-of-function variant. In the published cases closest to these slides, the siblings and the healthy person each carried two different loss-of-function variants, one on each copy of the gene, rather than two copies of one variant45,46. It means that both discoveries depend on a measured phenotype, and a measured value reflects treatment as well as biology: a statin user also has low measured LDL. Listen for how LDL was measured and whether treatment was taken into account. The genotype-to-phenotype page tells both stories.

Lecture 1 Slides 31–35 · Margarine, butter, LDL and MI

In Lecture 1 (slides 31–35) you will see that, in UK Biobank, alleles at APOE that raise LDL and statin use, and alleles at LPA that raise MI risk, are also associated with more margarine use and less butter use. The slides give the likely reason: people who learn they have high cholesterol or a family history of heart disease are more likely to switch to margarine. It means that a self-reported behavior measured after a diagnosis can carry the genetic signal of the diagnosis. The spread question (Field 1428, “What type of spread do you mainly use?”) was asked at the baseline visit, after many such diagnoses, and it even offers the brand names “Flora Pro-Active/Benecol” as a separate answer25. Listen for “social context matters”, and ask when a behavior was measured relative to diagnosis and treatment.

Health records and clinical codes Core

Record linkage connects each participant to national health records: hospital inpatient records (Hospital Episode Statistics in England and the equivalent systems in Scotland and Wales), primary care (general practitioner, GP) records for a subset of participants, cancer registries and death registries. The UK Biobank page describes how the linkage works; this section is about what the records do to a phenotype. These records were not collected for research. Clinical staff and trained coders create them for patient care and administration, using standard coding systems:

  • ICD-10, the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, has about 18,000 codes in 22 chapters26. It codes hospital diagnoses, causes of death and cancers. Scottish hospital records up to 1996 use the previous revision, ICD-927.
  • OPCS-4 is the UK classification of operations and procedures, used in hospital records28.
  • Read codes (version 2 and CTV3) are the coded vocabularies of UK primary care records. Neither is updated any more (the last updates were in April 2016 and April 2018); from April 2018, primary care moved in phases to a newer vocabulary, SNOMED CT29.

ICD-10 codes form a hierarchy. A 3-character category is a letter and two digits, such as I21; a dot and one more digit give a 4-character subcategory, such as I21.4. Categories are grouped into blocks and blocks into chapters30.

Hospital records are organized by episodes. An episode is a continuous period of care under one consultant, and one hospital stay can contain several episodes. Each episode has one main (primary) diagnosis, the main reason for the admission, and secondary diagnoses for other conditions. Records from England can hold up to 20 diagnosis codes per episode, from Wales 12 to 14, and from Scotland 6, so a condition that is not the reason for an admission is more likely to be coded in England28. The date of a diagnosis is usually taken from the episode start or admission date28: the date it was recorded, not the date the disease began.

Hospital records arrive as long tables with one row per episode (hesin), per diagnosis code (hesin_diag) or per procedure (hesin_oper); the next page shows them, with a query that turns them into a phenotype. They are placeholders, not events29,31.

From records to a case definition Core

A case definition is the rule that decides, from the available data, who counts as a case of a disease, who counts as a control, and who is left out. For myocardial infarction (MI) in UK Biobank, such a rule answers four questions:

  1. Which codes? For example ICD-10 I21 (acute MI) and I22 (subsequent MI), perhaps I25.2 (old MI), the matching ICD-9 and Read codes, and self-report code 1075.
  2. Which sources? Self-report, hospital records (main diagnosis only, or any position), primary care, death records.
  3. Which time window? A prevalent case had the disease before the baseline visit; an incident case was first diagnosed after it. A study of what predicts new disease uses incident cases.
  4. Who is a control? Everyone without a case record, or only people with no related record anywhere.

UK Biobank provides two ready-made sets of outcomes. The first-occurrence fields give, for about 1,200 3-character ICD-10 codes, the earliest date on which the code, or a code mapped to it, appears in primary care, hospital, death or self-report data, plus the source of that earliest record26. Algorithmically defined outcomes (ADOs) combine self-report, hospital and death records with fixed code lists for a few major diseases, aiming for a high positive predictive value11. The two give different counts for “the same” disease. Field 131298 (Date I21 first reported) has a date for 26,191 participants, while the ADO Field 42000 (Date of myocardial infarction) has 31,19631. The first-occurrence field covers I21 and the codes mapped to it, from all four sources; the ADO list adds I22, I23, I24.1 and I25.2 (with the matching ICD-9 codes), and does not use primary care11. For MI, UK Biobank cites a PPV of 75–100% for its hospital codes, and a somewhat lower one, around 70–75%, for MI found only in death records11.

Try itBuild a case definition (simulated cohort of 100,000 people)

What to notice. The numbers below are for the first simulated cohort; “New simulated cohort” changes them a little. In this simulation, each copy of one SNP allele multiplies the rate of a first MI by 1.30, and about 6.7% of the 100,000 people have an MI by 2023. With the default definition (self-report or hospital, any time) about 6,900 people are cases, but only about 88% of them truly had an MI, because some people report an MI they never had. Untick self-report: the cases drop to about 5,600, 97% of them true, and the odds ratio moves toward the value obtained with true MI status, even though about 1,300 people with a real MI now sit among the controls. Choose self-report alone with “Incident only”: there are no cases, because self-report happens at the baseline visit and can find only past events. Choose primary care alone: only about 1,500 cases, because only 45% of people have primary care data and those data stop in 2016–2017, and over 5,000 people with a real MI become controls. Ticking “Exclude people with an MI record in a source you do not count” removes most of them. In every version, the odds ratio is pulled toward 1 (no effect) when non-cases enter the case group or cases enter the control group, as expected when the errors do not depend on genotype32. Wrong cases do more damage than missed ones here. Counting self-report adds about 650 true cases and about 650 people who never had an MI, and the odds ratio falls from about 1.31 to 1.27. The added true cases had been about 0.7% of the controls, too few to change the controls’ allele frequencies much, whereas people without an MI now make up about 12% of the cases. The odds ratios here come from a logistic regression on the genotype counts; for incident disease, a time-to-event model is the more natural choice.

Common confusion No record is not the same as no disease

A control in a record-based definition is a person with no qualifying record, not a person known to be free of the disease. People without primary care data, people whose MI happened before hospital records began (1997 in England), and people treated in a nation whose records hold fewer diagnosis codes per episode can have had the disease and still look like controls. Exclusion rules for controls, and sensitivity analyses that change the definition, show how much this matters.

Time-to-event outcomes and censoring

For incident disease, the natural phenotype is a pair (time, event): the time from the baseline visit to the first event or to censoring, and an indicator that is 1 if the event was seen. A person’s follow-up ends at the earliest of the event, death from another cause, loss to follow-up, or the censoring date of the records for their nation. UK Biobank defines a data provider’s censoring date as the date up to which it estimates the data to be mostly complete, and it does not cut the data at that date: later records may exist but be incomplete27.

Imaging and wearables Optional

From 2014 UK Biobank invited participants back for brain, heart and body MRI, DXA (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, a low-dose X-ray scan of bone and body composition) and ultrasound of the neck arteries; by July 2025 it had scanned 100,000 people33,34. The Showcase records the first imaging visit (instance 2) for 102,521 participants35. Automated processing pipelines turn each scan into thousands of imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs), numbers such as the volume of a brain region33; for example, Field 25010 (“Volume of brain, grey+white matter”) is available for 82,012 participants35.

Between 2013 and 2015, participants with an email address were invited to wear a wrist accelerometer, a motion sensor, for 7 days; 103,712 data sets came back36. Algorithms summarize the raw signal into fields such as overall acceleration average (Field 90012, 103,567 participants)35.

These data come with three cautions. They were measured years after the baseline visit, so they describe the participant at an older age than the baseline fields do. They exist for a subset, and the people who came back for imaging were on average healthier than those who did not37. And each value depends on a processing pipeline whose version and quality flags belong in the analysis.

Phenotype quality: error, missing data and selection Core

Measurement error and misclassification

Measurement error is the difference between a recorded value and the true value. Random error changes from one measurement to the next: two blood pressure readings taken moments apart differ. Systematic error shifts values in one direction: an analyzer that reads high, or the measured LDL of statin users. For a binary phenotype, error is called misclassification: a true case recorded as a control, or the reverse.

Where the error sits decides what it does. Random error in the outcome y leaves a regression slope unbiased but widens its confidence interval; random error in a predictor x flattens the slope toward zero, which is called regression dilution38. In a GWAS the predictor is the genotype, which is measured with little error, so random error in a quantitative phenotype mainly costs power. When a phenotype serves as an exposure, as in observational epidemiology, its error biases the estimate. UK Biobank’s repeat visit in 2012–2013 was designed in part to measure this within-person variation so that analyses can correct for regression dilution5.

Misclassification of a binary outcome that is equally likely in all genotype groups (non-differential) biases the odds ratio toward 1. Misclassification that differs between the groups compared (differential), such as cases recalling past exposures more completely than controls, can bias it in either direction32. The case-definition widget above shows the non-differential pattern.

Lecture 1 Slide 23 · CRP, HDL and coronary heart disease

In Lecture 1 (slide 23) you will see that Mendelian randomization studies suggest CRP and HDL levels are not causally associated with coronary heart disease, consistent with clinical trials (the slide’s “HLD” means HDL). Listen for how each one was measured: an observational slope for an exposure with random measurement error is biased toward zero (regression dilution)38, whereas the genotypes that Mendelian randomization uses are measured with little error.

Missing data

Missing data are entries with no value. Why a value is missing matters more than how many values are missing. Values are missing completely at random if the missing values do not differ systematically from the observed ones; missing at random if any difference is explained by other observed data; and missing not at random otherwise, for example if heavy drinkers more often choose “Prefer not to answer”. Analyzing only complete cases loses power and, unless values are missing completely at random, can be biased39.

In practice: recode special values to NA first; check whether a field was asked of everyone or only after certain answers; check whether a measurement was made in a subset by design; compare people with and without data on variables you do have, such as age, sex and deprivation; and never fill a missing phenotype with 0.

Selection: who is in the data

Selection bias arises when the people in an analysis differ from the population of interest in a way that is related to the question. UK Biobank invited about 9.2 million people, and about 5.5% took part41. Participants were healthier than the general population of the same age: at ages 70–74, all-cause mortality was 46.2% lower in men and 55.5% lower in women41. This is healthy volunteer bias, which the UK Biobank page describes in detail. It does not necessarily distort associations: the hazard ratio for cardiovascular death in ever versus never smokers was 2.04 in UK Biobank and 1.99 in population surveys with conventional response rates42.

Selection also happens inside the cohort. Only some participants have primary care data, proteomics, imaging or accelerometer data; for the first Olink project, 6,376 of the 54,219 participants were chosen by the funding consortium rather than at random3, and imaging participants are on average healthier than the rest37. People with a severe disease before 2006 could join only if they survived to the baseline visit, so prevalent cases are survivors.

Codes and coverage change over time

Coding systems and data sources change. Scottish hospital records switched from ICD-9 to ICD-10 in 1996; primary care moved from Read codes to SNOMED CT from 2018; England allows more diagnosis codes per episode than Wales or Scotland; and primary care data stop in 2016–201727,28,29. A rule that looks simple, such as “any I21 code”, therefore captures different things for different people and periods. Mapping between systems can lose detail: the first-occurrence fields leave out Read codes that map to more than one 3-character ICD-10 code26.

Harmonizing codes: phecodes

To test thousands of diseases at once, in a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS), researchers group diagnosis codes into phecodes. Phecode version 1.2 condenses roughly 15,500 ICD-9-CM and 90,000 ICD-10-CM codes (the US clinical modifications) into 1,867 phecodes. Each phecode has an exclude range of related codes, and controls are people with no code in that range, which keeps people with closely related diagnoses out of the control group43. UK Biobank uses the WHO version of ICD-10, for which a beta map to phecodes is available44.

Key points
  • Five channels, one question. Every phenotype column is the output of a measurement process. Ask who was measured, when, how, and with which errors, gaps and selection.
  • Decode before you analyze. Recode special values such as −1, −3 and −7, respect skip logic, read the rule behind derived fields, and handle medication with a principled adjustment (for example LDL ÷ 0.7 for statin users, +15 mmHg systolic pressure for blood pressure drugs) rather than ignoring treatment, excluding treated people or adding treatment as a covariate.
  • A case definition is a set of choices. Codes, sources, time window and control rule decide the positive predictive value, the number of hidden cases among controls, and the estimate.
  • Time matters. Prevalent and incident cases answer different questions; incident disease is a (time, event) pair, with censoring at death, loss to follow-up or the source’s censoring date.
  • Errors, gaps and selection have predictable effects. Random error in y costs power, random error in x biases slopes toward zero, non-differential misclassification pulls odds ratios toward 1, and missing data and selection can bias results unless you know why values are missing and who was measured.

Check your understanding

A participant’s value in Field 1558 (alcohol intake frequency) is −3. What should you do with it before you compute the mean frequency?

−3 means “Prefer not to answer”. It is an answer, not a frequency, so recode it to NA (or keep it as a separate category) and report how many such values there were. Including it in a mean, or merging it with “Never”, gives a meaningless or biased result.

Field 1249 (past tobacco smoking) is empty for everyone who said they smoke on most or all days (39,207 people at baseline). Is that a data error?

No. Because of skip logic, the question was not asked of people who said they smoke on most or all days now. The empty value means “not asked”. The derived Field 20116 already classifies these people as current smokers.

You regress measured LDL cholesterol on genotype and ignore statins. In which direction is the estimate biased, and what is one standard fix?

Toward zero. Statins are given mainly to people with high LDL, including carriers of LDL-raising alleles, and they lower measured LDL, so the difference between genotype groups shrinks. A standard fix is to estimate untreated LDL, for example by dividing statin users’ LDL by 0.7. Excluding statin users or adding statin use as a covariate does not fix the bias.

In the widget, why does “self-report only, incident only” give no cases at all?

Self-report was collected at the baseline visit and asks about past illness, so every self-reported MI happened before baseline: all are prevalent. An incident-only definition excludes them, and self-report cannot find events after the visit.

A quantitative phenotype is measured with random error. Is the per-allele effect estimate in a GWAS biased?

Not by random error in y: the slope stays unbiased, but its standard error grows, so power falls. Random error in the predictor x would bias the slope toward zero (regression dilution); in a GWAS the predictor is the genotype, which is measured with little error.

Field 131298 (Date I21 first reported) and the ADO Field 42000 (Date of myocardial infarction) give different numbers of people with MI. Give two reasons.

They use different code lists: the ADO adds I22, I23, I24.1 and I25.2 (with the matching ICD-9 codes), while the first-occurrence field covers I21 and the codes mapped to it. They also use different sources: the first-occurrence field includes primary care, the ADO does not.
