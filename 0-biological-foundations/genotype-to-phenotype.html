From genotype to phenotype

Earlier pages described how genomes differ and how variants are inherited. This page connects those variants to measurable traits: one variant at a time (a regression slope), all variants together (genetic architecture and heritability), and the question of when an association means causation. It is the biology behind most of Lecture 1.

Mental model

Every trait-associated variant has a frequency and an effect size

A trait is shaped by many genetic variants plus environment, life history and chance. Each variant has a position on a chart of allele frequency against effect size: rare variants with large effects are found in families and by sequencing, common variants with small effects are found by GWAS, and common variants with large effects are few.

  • One variant’s effect is a regression slope, \(\beta\): the change in mean phenotype per copy of the effect allele (one variant’s effect).
  • Frequency and effect size together set how much of the trait’s variance a variant explains, \(R^2 = 2p(1 - p)\beta^2\) for effect-allele frequency \(p\) and \(\beta\) in SD units, and therefore how many people a study needs to detect it (try it).
  • Most traits are polygenic: thousands of small effects add up to a bell-shaped distribution, and heritability measures their combined share of the variance in one population (architecture, heritability).
  • An association is not automatically causal. Linkage disequilibrium, ancestry and social pathways can all produce one; Mendelian randomization uses the random inheritance of alleles to ask causal questions (causation, Mendelian randomization).
  • A GWAS fits one regression per variant. Its summary statistics are used in biology, drug-target discovery, Mendelian randomization, TWAS and polygenic scores (GWAS, polygenic scores).

In the genotype–phenotype data matrix from the overview page, each variant is one column of 0/1/2 entries. Its allele frequency is the column mean divided by 2, and its effect size is the slope from regressing the phenotype column \(\mathbf{y}\) on that column.

Phenotypes and traits Core

A phenotype is any observable or measurable characteristic of a person: a blood test result, a body measurement, a diagnosis, a behavior. A trait is a characteristic that varies among people; in genetics the two words are used almost interchangeably. Statistical genetics treats a phenotype as a variable \(y\) measured on each participant; its values form the phenotype column \(\mathbf{y}\) of the data matrix.

Three kinds of phenotype need different models:

  • Quantitative traits are measured on a continuous scale, such as height (cm), body mass index (BMI, kg/m²) or LDL cholesterol (mmol/L). The usual model is linear regression.
  • Binary traits are yes/no, such as whether a person has coronary artery disease. People with the disease are cases and the others are controls. The usual model is logistic regression.
  • Time-to-event outcomes record when something happened, such as age at a first heart attack, for people followed over time. They are covered on the phenotypes page.

Lesk summarizes what shapes a phenotype as “genotype + environment + life history + epigenetics”:3 the alleles a person carries, their surroundings, everything they have experienced since conception, and chemical marks on DNA that change how genes are used (genes page). The “+” is shorthand. The parts can interact, as the section on genes and environment shows.

The running example trait on this page is LDL cholesterol. Cholesterol does not dissolve in blood, so it travels inside lipoproteins: particles made of fats and proteins. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particles are the main carriers of cholesterol in the blood,4 and “LDL cholesterol” is the amount of cholesterol they carry. High LDL cholesterol causes atherosclerosis, the build-up of fatty plaque in artery walls.5 Plaque in the arteries that supply the heart is coronary artery disease (CAD); when a plaque breaks open, a blood clot can form on it and block the artery, and the heart muscle beyond it dies: a myocardial infarction, or heart attack.6 Statins are drugs that lower LDL cholesterol by blocking HMG-CoA reductase, an enzyme the liver uses to make cholesterol.7

One variant’s effect is a regression slope Core

Take one biallelic SNP and code each person’s genotype \(g\) as the number of copies of the effect allele: 0, 1 or 2 (the allele dosage from the variation page). Plot the phenotype against \(g\) and draw a line through the three group means. The standard model is the additive model:

\[ y_i = \alpha + \beta g_i + \boldsymbol{\gamma}^\top \mathbf{c}_i + \varepsilon_i \]
\(y_i\)
Phenotype of person \(i\), one of the \(n\) people in the study, in the units of the trait.
\(g_i\)
Genotype of person \(i\): the number of copies of the effect allele, 0, 1 or 2 (an imputed dosage can lie in between).
\(\alpha\)
Intercept: the expected phenotype when \(g_i = 0\) and every covariate is 0. Without covariates, it is the mean phenotype of people who carry no copy of the effect allele.
\(\beta\)
The per-allele effect: the change in mean phenotype for each extra copy of the effect allele. It is an effect size in the units of the trait, such as mmol/L per allele for LDL cholesterol or cm per allele for height. If \(y\) is standardized to mean 0 and standard deviation (SD) 1, \(\beta\) is in SD units, which makes effects on different traits comparable. \(\beta\) is a property of the population; a study estimates it as \(\hat{\beta}\).
\(\mathbf{c}_i\)
Covariates of person \(i\), as a vector: for example age, sex and genetic principal components.
\(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\)
Covariate effects, one per covariate. \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^\top \mathbf{c}_i = \gamma_1 c_{i1} + \gamma_2 c_{i2} + \cdots\) is the part of the phenotype that the model attributes to person \(i\)’s covariates.
\(\varepsilon_i\)
Residual: everything the model does not explain for person \(i\) (other variants, environment, measurement error). The model assumes that it has mean 0 at every genotype and covariate value.

Averaged over people with the same genotype and covariates, the residual is 0, so the mean phenotype is a straight line in \(g_i\): \(\alpha\), \(\alpha + \beta\) and \(\alpha + 2\beta\) for 0, 1 and 2 copies, plus the same covariate term \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^\top \mathbf{c}_i\) in all three groups. Every copy adds the same amount \(\beta\); this is what “additive” means, and it places the heterozygotes’ mean exactly halfway between the two homozygotes’ means. Because the covariates are in the same regression, \(\beta\) is estimated after adjusting for them: it compares people with the same covariate values. Without covariates, the least-squares estimate is the slope \(\hat{\beta} = \widehat{\Cov}(g, y)/\widehat{\Var}(g)\), the sample covariance of genotype and phenotype divided by the sample variance of the genotype. If \(\beta = 0\), the three genotype groups have the same mean phenotype, so a test of association is a test of \(\beta = 0\). Counting the other allele replaces \(g_i\) by \(2 - g_i\): the fitted means stay the same, \(\beta\) becomes \(-\beta\) and \(\alpha\) becomes \(\alpha + 2\beta\).

For a binary trait, the same idea uses logistic regression, which fits the straight line to the log odds of being a case:

\[ \begin{gathered} \ln \frac{\pi_i}{1 - \pi_i} = \alpha + \beta g_i + \boldsymbol{\gamma}^\top \mathbf{c}_i \\[6pt] \mathrm{OR} = e^{\beta} \end{gathered} \]
\(\pi_i\)
Probability that person \(i\) is a case, given their genotype and covariates; between 0 and 1.
\(\pi_i/(1 - \pi_i)\)
Odds of being a case: the probability of being a case divided by the probability of not being one. Odds run from 0 to infinity; a probability of 0.2 gives odds of 0.25. Their natural logarithm, the log odds or \(\logit(\pi_i)\), can take any real value.
\(\alpha, \beta, \boldsymbol{\gamma}\)
Intercept, per-allele effect and covariate effects, as in the linear model but on the log-odds scale: \(\beta\) is the change in log odds per extra copy of the effect allele, the log odds ratio.
\(g_i, \mathbf{c}_i\)
Genotype and covariates of person \(i\), as above.
\(\mathrm{OR}\)
Odds ratio per allele: the factor by which each extra copy of the effect allele multiplies the odds, for people with the same covariates.

A probability lies between 0 and 1, so a straight line in \(g_i\) could predict impossible values; the log odds can take any real value, so a straight line on that scale never does. Taking the exponential of both sides gives the odds, \(e^{\alpha + \boldsymbol{\gamma}^\top \mathbf{c}_i} (e^{\beta})^{g_i}\): each extra copy multiplies the odds by \(e^{\beta}\), so for people with the same covariates the odds with 0, 1 and 2 copies are in the ratio \(1 : \mathrm{OR} : \mathrm{OR}^2\). \(\beta = 0\) gives \(\mathrm{OR} = 1\), which means no effect; \(\beta > 0\) gives \(\mathrm{OR} > 1\), and \(\beta < 0\) gives \(\mathrm{OR} < 1\). When \(\pi_i\) is small, the odds \(\pi_i/(1 - \pi_i)\) are close to \(\pi_i\) itself, so for a rare disease the odds ratio is close to the ratio of the two probabilities, the relative risk.

For example, in clinic- and autopsy-based studies of white participants (labeled “Caucasian” in the paper, an outdated term), the odds of Alzheimer’s disease were 3.2 times higher with one APOE ε4 allele (genotype ε3/ε4) than with ε3/ε3, and 14.9 times higher with two (ε4/ε4).11 A purely multiplicative per-allele model would predict \(3.2^2 \approx 10\) for two copies, so real effects need not follow the additive coding exactly.

How much variance one variant explains

How much does one variant matter for the whole population? The variance explained by the variant is the share of the trait’s variance across people that its genotype accounts for:

\[ \begin{aligned} R^2 &= \frac{\beta^2 \Var(g)}{\Var(y)} \\[4pt] &= 2p(1 - p)\,\beta^2 \end{aligned} \]
\(R^2\)
Variance explained: the fraction of the variance of \(y\) among the people of a population that the variant’s genotype accounts for, from 0 to 1 (often given as a percentage). It equals the population \(R^2\) of the regression of \(y\) on \(g\), which is also the squared correlation between \(g\) and \(y\). The \(R^2\) of a regression fitted to a sample estimates it.
\(\beta\)
Per-allele effect from the additive model. On the second line it is in SD units: the effect in trait units divided by the standard deviation of \(y\).
\(\Var(g)\)
Variance of the genotype \(g_i\) across people. If the effect allele has frequency \(p\) and the population is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, \(g_i\) follows a \(\Binomial(2, p)\) distribution, whose variance is \(2p(1 - p)\) (allele frequencies page).
\(\Var(y)\)
Variance of the phenotype across people, in squared trait units. It is 1 when \(y\) is in SD units, which gives the second line.
\(p\)
Frequency of the effect allele in the population.

Without covariates the model is \(y_i = \alpha + \beta g_i + \varepsilon_i\). If the residual is uncorrelated with the genotype, the variance of \(y\) splits into two parts, \(\Var(y) = \beta^2 \Var(g) + \Var(\varepsilon)\): the constant \(\alpha\) adds no variance, and multiplying \(g_i\) by \(\beta\) multiplies its variance by \(\beta^2\). \(R^2\) is the first part’s share. Because \(\Cov(g, y) = \beta \Var(g)\), the same number is the squared correlation \(\Cov(g, y)^2/(\Var(g)\Var(y))\). \(R^2\) grows with the square of the effect, so doubling \(\beta\) quadruples it. The factor \(2p(1 - p)\) is largest, 0.5, at \(p = 0.5\), and falls to 0 as \(p\) approaches 0 or 1, where nearly everyone has the same genotype. Counting the other allele leaves \(R^2\) unchanged, because \(p\) becomes \(1 - p\) and \(\beta\) becomes \(-\beta\).

Two consequences follow. A rare variant can have a large \(\beta\) and still explain little variance, because \(2p(1 - p)\) is small. And a common variant with a small \(\beta\) explains very little: with \(p = 0.3\) and \(\beta = 0.02\) SD, \(R^2 = 2 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 \times 0.02^2 \approx 0.017\%\) of the variance.

Variance explained also sets the sample size a study needs. The test of \(\beta = 0\) in \(n\) people uses a chi-square statistic with 1 degree of freedom, and a study of a variant with a given \(R^2\) has 80% power if that statistic reaches a p-value below 5 × 10⁻⁸ (the genome-wide significance threshold, explained in the GWAS section) with probability 0.8. The number of people this takes follows from the statistic’s noncentrality \(\lambda\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda \approx \frac{nR^2}{1 - R^2} \\[6pt] n \approx \frac{39.6\,(1 - R^2)}{R^2} \approx \frac{40}{R^2} \end{gathered} \]
\(\lambda\)
Noncentrality of the chi-square statistic for the test of \(\beta = 0\): its expected value minus 1. It is 0 when \(\beta = 0\), because the statistic then follows a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom, whose mean is 1.
\(n\)
Number of unrelated people in the study.
\(R^2\)
Variance explained by the variant, as above.
\(39.6\)
The noncentrality that gives 80% power at 5 × 10⁻⁸: \((5.45 + 0.84)^2\). A standard normal variable exceeds 5.45 in absolute value with probability 5 × 10⁻⁸ (a two-sided test), and it falls below 0.84 with probability 0.8.

Take \(\beta > 0\); the other case is symmetric. The chi-square statistic is the square of \(Z = \hat{\beta}/\SE(\hat{\beta})\), which is approximately normal with standard deviation 1 and mean \(\sqrt{\lambda}\). The least-squares slope has \(\SE(\hat{\beta})^2 \approx \Var(\varepsilon)/(n\Var(g))\), and \(\Var(\varepsilon) = (1 - R^2)\Var(y)\), so \(\lambda = \beta^2/\SE(\hat{\beta})^2 \approx n\beta^2\Var(g)/((1 - R^2)\Var(y)) = nR^2/(1 - R^2)\), the first line: \(\lambda\) grows in proportion to the number of people and to \(R^2/(1 - R^2)\), the variance the variant explains relative to the variance it leaves unexplained. Significance needs \(|Z| > 5.45\), and \(Z\) exceeds 5.45 with probability 0.8 when its mean is \(5.45 + 0.84\) (the chance that \(Z < -5.45\) is negligible), so \(\lambda = 39.6\). Solving the first line for \(n\) at that value gives the second line. A single variant explains a small fraction of the variance, so \(1 - R^2 \approx 1\) and \(n \approx 40/R^2\): the sample size needed is inversely proportional to the variance explained, and halving \(\beta\) quadruples it.

The common variant above needs about 236,000 people. PCSK9 R46L (rs11591147), a low-frequency variant with a larger effect, needs far fewer. Its T allele has frequency about 1.6% in gnomAD’s non-Finnish European samples,12 and carriers have about 15% lower LDL cholesterol,13 roughly 0.5 mmol/L, or a little over half of the SD of LDL cholesterol in UK Biobank (0.87 mmol/L).9 Large GWAS estimate about 0.4–0.5 SD per copy of the T allele.59 Taking \(\beta \approx 0.5\) SD gives \(R^2 \approx 2 \times 0.016 \times 0.984 \times 0.5^2 \approx 0.8\%\) and \(n \approx 5{,}000\).

Common confusion A tiny p-value is not a large effect

In 500,000 people, a variant that explains only 0.02% of the variance has noncentrality \(\lambda \approx nR^2 = 500{,}000 \times 0.0002 = 100\), so its expected chi-square statistic is about 100, a p-value around 10⁻²³. Significance says an effect is unlikely to be exactly zero; \(\beta\) and the variance explained say how large it is. Report and read both.

In the data BETA, SE and p in GWAS output

GWAS software writes one row per variant. REGENIE, used in Session 2, writes the columns CHROM GENPOS ID ALLELE0 ALLELE1 A1FREQ INFO N TEST BETA SE CHISQ LOG10P: ALLELE1 is the allele whose copies are counted, A1FREQ its frequency, BETA its estimated effect \(\hat{\beta}\) “on the original scale” of the trait (mmol/L per allele if \(y\) is LDL cholesterol in mmol/L), SE the standard error of BETA, and LOG10P = −log₁₀(p-value).10 For binary traits REGENIE fits logistic regression, so BETA is a log odds ratio.14 The sign of BETA flips if the other allele is counted, so check which allele is the effect allele before comparing studies.

Genetic architecture: one gene or thousands Core

The genetic architecture of a trait is the number of variants that affect it, their allele frequencies, their effect sizes and how their effects combine. Traits range from monogenic to polygenic:

  • A monogenic trait is caused mainly by variants in one gene, usually with large effects and simple inheritance patterns (inheritance page). Example: familial hypercholesterolemia, very high LDL cholesterol from birth, caused by variants in LDLR, APOB, LDLRAP1 or PCSK9 and affecting about 1 in 200 to 1 in 250 people.4
  • A polygenic trait is influenced by many variants, each with a small effect. A complex trait (or multifactorial trait) is polygenic and also shaped by environment. Height, BMI, coronary artery disease and LDL cholesterol in people without familial hypercholesterolemia are complex traits.

Height shows how far polygenicity goes. A GWAS of about 5.4 million people found 12,111 independent SNPs associated with height, clustered in 7,209 genomic segments that cover about 21% of the genome. Together these SNPs explain about 40% of the variance of height in samples the study labels European ancestry but only about 10–20% in samples with other ancestry labels,15 a gap discussed again in the polygenic-score section.

Why are there so few common variants with large effects, the nearly empty top-right corner of the chart? Natural selection removes alleles that reduce survival or reproduction. This negative (purifying) selection keeps large-effect alleles in important genes rare, so the heritability carried by common variants is spread in small amounts across thousands of regions.16 Early GWAS results show the pattern: across the associations from GWAS published through 2008, the median frequency of the risk allele was 36% and the median odds ratio was 1.33.17

When many independent variants each add a small amount, their sum is approximately normally distributed (the central limit theorem). Fisher showed in 1918 that many Mendelian factors with small effects reproduce both the continuous, bell-shaped distributions of traits such as height and the correlations between relatives.18 Diseases are recorded as yes/no, but the same idea applies through the liability threshold model:19 each person has an unobserved, continuous liability (genetic contribution plus everything else, approximately normal), and a person becomes a case when liability passes a threshold. The threshold is set by the prevalence; for a disease that affects 5% of people it lies 1.645 SD above the mean.

Heritability Core

Heritability (\(h^2\)) is the share of the variance of a trait, in a given population and environment, that is due to genetic differences between people: \(h^2 = \Var(\text{genetic})/\Var(\text{phenotype})\), where \(\Var(\text{phenotype})\) is the variance of the trait among the people of that population and \(\Var(\text{genetic})\) is the variance of the trait values that their genotypes alone would predict.20 Narrow-sense heritability counts only additive genetic effects, the part that a regression on allele counts can capture; broad-sense heritability, written \(H^2\) when the two are distinguished, also counts dominance and interactions between variants. Heritability describes a population, not a person.

A twin study estimates heritability by comparing two kinds of twin pairs. Monozygotic (identical) twins carry the same DNA sequence; dizygotic (fraternal) twins inherit the same parental copy at about half of their genome on average, like ordinary siblings (inheritance page). If both kinds of pairs share their environments to the same degree, the extra similarity of identical pairs reflects genes, and Falconer’s formula splits the variance of the trait into three shares:

\[ \begin{aligned} h^2 &\approx 2\,(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} - r_{\mathrm{DZ}}) \\ c^2 &\approx r_{\mathrm{MZ}} - h^2 \\ e^2 &\approx 1 - r_{\mathrm{MZ}} \end{aligned} \]
\(r_{\mathrm{MZ}}\)
Correlation of the trait between the two twins of a pair, computed across identical (monozygotic) pairs; from −1 to 1.
\(r_{\mathrm{DZ}}\)
The same correlation, computed across fraternal (dizygotic) pairs.
\(h^2\)
Heritability, here narrow-sense: the share of the trait’s variance due to additive genetic effects.
\(c^2\)
Share of the variance due to environment that the two twins of a pair share, such as their family home.
\(e^2\)
Share due to environment unique to each twin, plus measurement error.

The model writes the trait as the sum of three uncorrelated parts, additive genetic, shared environment and unique environment, whose variances are the fractions \(h^2\), \(c^2\) and \(e^2\) of the total, so \(h^2 + c^2 + e^2 = 1\). The correlation between the two twins of a pair is the fraction of the variance that comes from the parts they have in common. Identical twins have the whole genetic part and the shared environment in common, so \(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} = h^2 + c^2\). Fraternal twins inherit the same parental copy at half of the genome on average, so they have half of the additive genetic part in common, plus the shared environment: \(r_{\mathrm{DZ}} = \tfrac{1}{2}h^2 + c^2\). Subtracting gives \(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} - r_{\mathrm{DZ}} = \tfrac{1}{2}h^2\), the first line; \(c^2\) is the rest of \(r_{\mathrm{MZ}}\), and \(e^2\) is the share that identical twins do not have in common. If identical pairs are no more alike than fraternal pairs, \(h^2 = 0\); if \(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} = 2r_{\mathrm{DZ}}\), \(c^2 = 0\). In the simulated pairs below, \(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} = 0.80\) and \(r_{\mathrm{DZ}} = 0.45\) give \(h^2 = 0.70\), \(c^2 = 0.10\) and \(e^2 = 0.20\). The \(\approx\) signs mark that the shares hold only under the model’s assumptions: more similar environments in identical than in fraternal pairs, or dominance effects (shared fully by identical twins but on average only a quarter by fraternal twins), make \(2(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} - r_{\mathrm{DZ}})\) larger than \(h^2\).

For adult height, twin cohorts in eight countries gave heritability estimates of 0.87–0.93 in men and 0.68–0.84 in women.21 Across 17,804 traits studied in fifty years of twin research, the average reported heritability was 49%.22

SNP-heritability is the variance explained by all the common SNPs on a genotyping array together, estimated from unrelated people without identifying individual SNPs. In 2010, 294,831 SNPs measured in 3,925 unrelated people explained about 45% of the variance of height.23 A year earlier, the roughly 40 genome-wide significant loci then known explained only about 5%, against a twin heritability of about 80%.2 That gap is the missing heritability. Yang et al. concluded that most of it is not missing: it comes from many SNPs whose individual effects are too small to pass a genome-wide threshold in the samples of the time, plus causal variants that array SNPs tag only partly, often because they are rarer.23 By 2022, with 5.4 million people, the 12,111 significant SNPs explained about 40% of the variance in samples labeled European ancestry, nearly all of the SNP-heritability.15

Common confusion What heritability is not
  • Not the share of one person’s trait caused by genes. A heritability of 0.7 means 70% of the variation in a population is due to genetic differences, not that a trait is 70% genetic in each person.24
  • Not a fixed constant. An estimate is specific to one population in one environment and can change as circumstances change.24
  • Not the same as familial. Traits cluster in families for non-genetic reasons too; the language a person speaks runs in families but has no genetic contribution.24
  • Not a measure of how changeable a trait is. Phenylketonuria is caused by variants in one gene, PAH, and is highly heritable, yet a low-phenylalanine diet started after newborn screening prevents its severe signs.24,25 Lecture 1 slide 19 lists it among the conditions screened in newborns.

Genes and environment act together Core

A gene–environment interaction (G×E) means that the effect of a genotype depends on an environmental exposure, or equivalently that the effect of the exposure depends on the genotype. In a regression it is a product term:

\[ \begin{aligned} y_i = \alpha &+ \beta_{\mathrm{G}}\, g_i + \beta_{\mathrm{E}}\, E_i \\ &+ \beta_{\mathrm{GE}}\, g_i E_i + \varepsilon_i \end{aligned} \]
\(y_i, g_i, \varepsilon_i, \alpha\)
Phenotype, genotype (copies of the effect allele) and residual of person \(i\), and the intercept, as in the additive model; covariates can be added in the same way.
\(E_i\)
Exposure of person \(i\): 0 or 1 (for example, does not drink or drinks) or an amount (for example, drinks per week).
\(\beta_{\mathrm{G}}\)
Per-allele effect of the genotype in people with \(E_i = 0\).
\(\beta_{\mathrm{E}}\)
Effect of one unit of exposure in people with \(g_i = 0\).
\(g_i E_i\)
The product term, or interaction term: genotype times exposure, entered as one more column of the regression.
\(\beta_{\mathrm{GE}}\)
Interaction effect: the change in the per-allele effect for each unit of exposure. \(\beta_{\mathrm{GE}} \ne 0\) is the interaction.

Collecting the terms that contain \(g_i\) gives \(y_i = \alpha + \beta_{\mathrm{E}} E_i + (\beta_{\mathrm{G}} + \beta_{\mathrm{GE}} E_i)\, g_i + \varepsilon_i\), so the per-allele effect is \(\beta_{\mathrm{G}} + \beta_{\mathrm{GE}} E_i\): with a 0/1 exposure it is \(\beta_{\mathrm{G}}\) in unexposed people and \(\beta_{\mathrm{G}} + \beta_{\mathrm{GE}}\) in exposed people. Collecting the terms that contain \(E_i\) instead shows that the effect of one unit of exposure, \(\beta_{\mathrm{E}} + \beta_{\mathrm{GE}}\, g_i\), depends on the genotype through the same coefficient, which is why the two descriptions in the definition above are equivalent. If \(\beta_{\mathrm{GE}} = 0\), the genotype has the same effect at every level of exposure, the effects of genotype and exposure add, and the lines of mean phenotype against exposure for the three genotypes are parallel. For a binary trait the same terms make up the log odds of a logistic model, so \(\beta_{\mathrm{GE}}\) is the change in the per-allele log odds ratio per unit of exposure, and \(e^{\beta_{\mathrm{GE}}}\) is the factor by which each unit of exposure multiplies the per-allele odds ratio.

The running example rs671 shows a strong interaction. Its A allele (ALDH2*2) produces a nearly inactive form of the enzyme ALDH2, so acetaldehyde, a toxic product of alcohol breakdown, builds up after drinking and causes facial flushing.26 The allele has a frequency of about 17–23% in East Asian reference samples and is nearly absent in European samples.12 In Japanese and Taiwanese case–control studies, heterozygous carriers who drank had odds ratios of 3.7 to 18.1 for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, and most studies found odds ratios above 10 for heavy-drinking carriers.26 The genotype’s effect on this cancer depends on how much a person drinks.

Lesk gives a second example. α1-antitrypsin, the protein made from the gene SERPINA1, inhibits elastase, an enzyme released by white blood cells that damages the lining of the lungs’ air sacs unless it is inhibited. Its Z allele leaves too little working inhibitor, which predisposes to emphysema, lung damage that makes breathing difficult, and smoking makes the effect worse.3,27

A different pattern is one variant affecting several traits, called pleiotropy. APOE is the standard example. Its ε4 allele (marked by the C allele of the SNP rs429358) raises LDL cholesterol; across APOE genotypes, LDL cholesterol rises roughly linearly from ε2/ε2 to ε4/ε4.28 The same ε4 allele raises the risk of Alzheimer’s disease,11 while the ε2 allele (marked by the T allele of rs7412) lowers LDL cholesterol. Pleiotropy matters for Mendelian randomization: a variant chosen as an instrument for one trait may also affect the outcome through another.

Association is not causation Core

A genetic association means that the average phenotype differs between genotype groups, \(\beta \ne 0\). It does not by itself show that the tested variant causes the difference. Three explanations come up again and again:

  1. Linkage disequilibrium. Nearby variants are inherited together, so their genotype columns are correlated (haplotypes and LD page). The tested SNP may only be correlated with the causal variant. Finding the causal variant within an associated region is fine-mapping (Session 4).
  2. Confounding by ancestry. If allele frequencies and the trait both differ between ancestry groups for unrelated reasons (diet, healthcare, other environments), SNP and trait are associated without any causal link. This is population stratification (ancestry page); GWAS adjust for genetic principal components or use mixed models.
  3. Indirect and social pathways. A variant can truly change a trait through a long chain that runs through medicine and behavior, as the margarine example shows.

UK Biobank Data-Field 1428 records the answer to the touchscreen question “What type of spread do you mainly use?”.29 A GWAS of margarine versus butter use finds genome-wide significant peaks at APOE and LPA (Lecture 1 slides 31–33). Both are genes for lipoproteins, not for taste. Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), is an LDL-like particle with an extra protein, apolipoprotein(a), attached; its blood level is more than 90% determined by variation at LPA, and high levels cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.30 The LPA variant rs10455872 has an odds ratio for coronary disease of 1.70.31 Slide 32 lists the evidence: at APOE, the allele that raises LDL levels and statin use also raises margarine use; at LPA, the allele that raises heart-attack risk does too. The likely chain runs through diagnosis, treatment and advice to eat less saturated fat.

One problem genetic studies largely avoid is reverse causation, where the outcome changes the supposed cause. For example, if illness makes some people stop drinking, non-drinkers will include more sick people, and drinking will look protective. A person’s germline genotype is fixed at conception, so a disease cannot change it. That fact is the basis of Mendelian randomization.

Mendelian randomization Core

Observational studies struggle to tell whether an exposure causes an outcome. People with high HDL cholesterol (the cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein particles) have fewer heart attacks, but they also differ in diet, exercise and many other ways. Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to test such causal questions.33 An instrumental variable is a variable that is associated with the exposure and affects the outcome only through the exposure. Genotypes are good candidates: at conception each parent passes on one of their two alleles at random (Mendel’s law of segregation, inheritance page), so which allele a child receives does not depend on the child’s later lifestyle.

A genetic variant \(G\) is a valid instrument for the effect of an exposure \(X\) on an outcome \(Y\) under three conditions (Lecture 1 slide 22, Assumptions 1–3):33

  1. Relevance: \(G\) is associated with \(X\).
  2. Independence: \(G\) shares no common cause with \(Y\). Sanderson et al. call this exchangeability; population stratification can break it.
  3. Exclusion restriction: \(G\) affects \(Y\) only through \(X\). Pleiotropy can break it.

Only the first condition can be tested directly from data.33 If all three hold, the causal effect of \(X\) on \(Y\) is the ratio of two regression slopes, the Wald ratio:

\[ \beta_{XY} = \frac{\beta_{GY}}{\beta_{GX}} \]
\(\beta_{GX}\)
Per-allele effect of the variant \(G\) on the exposure \(X\): the slope of the regression of \(X\) on the genotype, in units of \(X\) per allele (for example mmol/L of HDL cholesterol per allele).
\(\beta_{GY}\)
Per-allele effect of \(G\) on the outcome \(Y\): the slope of the regression of \(Y\) on the genotype; a log odds ratio when \(Y\) is a disease.
\(\beta_{XY}\)
Causal effect of \(X\) on \(Y\): the change in \(Y\), or in its log odds, caused by raising \(X\) by one unit.

Under the three conditions, the only path from \(G\) to \(Y\) runs through \(X\). Each extra copy of the allele changes \(X\) by \(\beta_{GX}\), and each unit of \(X\) changes \(Y\) by \(\beta_{XY}\), so each copy changes \(Y\) by \(\beta_{GY} = \beta_{GX}\beta_{XY}\); dividing by \(\beta_{GX}\) gives the ratio. Confounders \(U\), factors that affect both \(X\) and \(Y\), do not enter, because the genotype is unrelated to them (independence), whereas the slope of \(Y\) on \(X\) in observational data also contains their effects. The ratio needs \(\beta_{GX} \ne 0\) (relevance), and when \(\beta_{GX}\) is close to 0, small errors in it change the ratio a lot. If the variant also affects \(Y\) through another path (pleiotropy), \(\beta_{GY}\) includes that path and the ratio is biased. A variant that clearly changes \(X\) but leaves \(Y\) unchanged, \(\beta_{GY} \approx 0\), implies \(\beta_{XY} \approx 0\), which is the logic of the HDL cholesterol example below. With data, the estimates take the place of the slopes: \(\hat{\beta}_{XY} = \hat{\beta}_{GY}/\hat{\beta}_{GX}\).

Both slopes can be taken from GWAS summary statistics, which is one reason MR is a major use of GWAS results.

Two results from Lecture 1 slide 23 show what MR can do. The 396Ser allele of the gene LIPG (frequency 2.6%) raises HDL cholesterol by 0.14 mmol/L. Observational data predict that such a rise should lower the odds of myocardial infarction to about 0.87, yet carriers had an odds ratio of 0.99 (95% CI 0.88–1.11). A score of 14 HDL-raising variants gave the same answer, while a score of LDL-raising variants, used as a positive control, strongly raised risk (odds ratio 2.13 per SD of LDL cholesterol).34 Similarly, variants that lower C-reactive protein (CRP), a blood marker of inflammation, did not lower the risk of coronary heart disease (odds ratio 1.00 per 20% lower CRP).35 So raising HDL cholesterol or lowering CRP by themselves is unlikely to prevent heart attacks, in line with clinical trials, as the slide notes.

The running example rs671 has been used the same way. In the China Kadoorie Biobank, genotypes at rs671 and at a second alcohol-metabolism variant predicted how much men drank. Genotype-predicted drinking raised stroke risk steadily, with no protective effect of moderate drinking, unlike the pattern seen with self-reported drinking.36

From a variant to a medicine: forward and reverse genetics Core

Genetic studies find drug targets in two directions. Forward genetics starts from a phenotype, such as a family with unusually low LDL cholesterol, and searches for the responsible variant. Reverse genetics starts from a gene and asks what happens to people whose copies of it do not work. Loss-of-function variants (variation page) are especially informative: if people who carry them are healthy and have less disease, a drug that blocks the same protein is more likely to be safe and effective (Lecture 1 slides 15–16).

PCSK9: from families to a class of drugs

The liver removes LDL particles from the blood with LDL receptors on the surface of liver cells. A receptor binds an LDL particle, and the cell takes both inside in a small membrane sac, an endosome. The LDL is then broken down in the lysosome, the cell compartment that digests molecules, while the receptor normally returns to the surface for another round. The PCSK9 protein, which the liver makes and releases into the blood, binds LDL receptors at the cell surface; a bound receptor is sent to the lysosome and destroyed instead of returning.38 More PCSK9 means fewer receptors and more LDL cholesterol in the blood; less PCSK9 means the opposite.

Research on PCSK9 moved from families to populations to drugs:

  1. 2003, families. Missense variants in PCSK9 that increase its activity were found in families with autosomal dominant hypercholesterolemia (very high LDL cholesterol).39,38
  2. 2005, people with low LDL cholesterol. Sequencing PCSK9 in people with low LDL cholesterol found two nonsense variants, Y142X and C679X. Together they were carried by about 2% of African Americans and were associated with 40% lower LDL cholesterol.40
  3. 2006, a population cohort. Over 15 years in the ARIC study, Black participants with one of these nonsense variants (2.6%) had 28% lower LDL cholesterol and an 88% lower risk of coronary heart disease. White participants carrying R46L (rs11591147; 3.2%) had 15% lower LDL cholesterol and a 47% lower risk.13
  4. Healthy people without working PCSK9. A woman with two inactivating variants, one on each copy of the gene, had no detectable PCSK9 in her blood and LDL cholesterol of 14 mg/dL (about 0.36 mmol/L), and was healthy.41,42 A woman homozygous for C679X had LDL cholesterol of 0.4 mmol/L.43
  5. Drugs. Antibodies that block PCSK9 were approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2015: alirocumab in July and evolocumab in August. Inclisiran, a small interfering RNA (siRNA) that makes liver cells destroy the PCSK9 messenger RNA so that less protein is made, followed in 2021.44 In a trial of 27,564 patients with cardiovascular disease, evolocumab lowered LDL cholesterol by 59% and the rate of major cardiovascular events by 15%.45

ANGPTL3 and BCL11A: forward genetics and GWAS

Forward genetics (slide 15): exome sequencing of two siblings with very low LDL cholesterol and very low triglycerides (another blood fat) found that both carried two different nonsense variants in ANGPTL3, one on each copy (S17X and E129X). Family members with both variants had mean LDL cholesterol of 33 mg/dL against 109 mg/dL in non-carriers, and triglycerides of 21 against 130 mg/dL.46 Later, people with one loss-of-function copy (about 1 in 237 people labeled European ancestry in the DiscovEHR study) had 27% lower triglycerides, 9% lower LDL cholesterol and 41% lower odds of coronary artery disease.47 An antibody against ANGPTL3, evinacumab, was approved by the FDA in February 2021 for homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia,48 the severe form in which both copies of a familial hypercholesterolemia gene carry a variant.4

Genome-wide association (slide 17): fetal hemoglobin (HbF) is the form of hemoglobin made before birth, and adults who keep making more of it have milder sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia (genes page). A GWAS of HbF levels in 4,305 Sardinians found a strong association at BCL11A (p < 10⁻³⁵).49 The associated variants, including the running example rs1427407, lie in an enhancer that increases BCL11A expression in red-blood-cell precursors. The high-HbF allele weakens a transcription-factor binding site, lowers BCL11A expression, and so raises HbF,50 because the BCL11A protein represses fetal hemoglobin.51 Editing that enhancer with CRISPR-Cas9 in patients’ own blood stem cells raised HbF in the first two treated patients, one with β-thalassemia and one with sickle cell disease: both stopped needing transfusions, and the patient with sickle cell disease had no further pain crises (vaso-occlusive episodes).51 The therapy, exagamglogene autotemcel (exa-cel, sold as Casgevy), was approved by the FDA on 8 December 2023 for sickle cell disease in people aged 12 and older.52

What a GWAS does Core

A genome-wide association study (GWAS) repeats the single-variant regression of the earlier section for every variant in the genome, one at a time. For variant \(j\) it fits \(y_i = \alpha + \beta_j g_{ij} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}^\top \mathbf{c}_i + \varepsilon_i\), where \(g_{ij}\) is the number of copies of variant \(j\)’s effect allele that person \(i\) carries, and records \(\hat{\beta}_j\), its standard error and its p-value; the intercept and the covariate effects are estimated again in each regression. With genotyped plus imputed variants that is millions of regressions; UK Biobank’s imputed data hold about 96 million variants.32

Because so many tests are run, a GWAS uses a strict threshold, p < 5 × 10⁻⁸, called genome-wide significance. It is a Bonferroni correction of 0.05 for about one million independent tests, the number estimated for common variants in genomes labeled European ancestry; linkage disequilibrium makes neighboring tests correlated, so the effective number of tests is far below the number of variants. Genomes labeled African ancestry have shorter-range LD and about twice as many independent tests.53

The output, one row per variant with its position, alleles, effect-allele frequency, \(\hat{\beta}\), standard error, p-value and sample size, is called GWAS summary statistics. Summary statistics contain no individual-level data, so they are widely shared; the GWAS Catalog collects published associations (1,198,394 top associations from 7,845 publications as of October 2026).54 A Manhattan plot shows a whole GWAS at once: position along the genome on the x-axis and −log₁₀(p-value) on the y-axis.

What GWAS results are used for:

  • Biology. A peak points to a region, not to a gene. About 88% of early GWAS hits were in introns or between genes,17 and about 93% of disease- and trait-associated variants lie in non-coding sequence,55 so linking a peak to its gene usually needs functional data such as eQTLs (genes page; Session 5).
  • Drug targets. HMGCR and PCSK9 both appear in GWAS of LDL cholesterol, and both encode drug targets.
  • Mendelian randomization. The GWAS slopes \(\hat{\beta}_{GX}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{GY}\) are the inputs of the Wald ratio.
  • TWAS. A transcriptome-wide association study predicts each person’s gene expression from their genotypes, using models trained on eQTL data, and tests the predicted expression against the trait (Lecture 1 slide 25).
  • Polygenic scores, next.

Polygenic scores Core

A polygenic score (PRS, polygenic risk score; also PGS) adds up a person’s effect alleles, weighted by GWAS effect sizes:

\[ \mathrm{PRS}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{M} \hat{\beta}_j \, g_{ij} \]
\(\mathrm{PRS}_i\)
Polygenic score of person \(i\), in the units of the weights: trait units for a quantitative trait, log odds for a binary trait.
\(j, M\)
Index of the variants in the score, \(j = 1, \ldots, M\). The \(M\) variants are a chosen set, from hundreds to millions.
\(\hat{\beta}_j\)
Weight of variant \(j\): its estimated per-allele effect. The weights come from a GWAS in other people; the score is then computed for new people from their genotypes alone.
\(g_{ij}\)
Number of copies of variant \(j\)’s effect allele that person \(i\) carries: 0, 1 or 2, or an imputed dosage.

Under the additive model each copy of variant \(j\)’s effect allele changes the expected trait by \(\beta_j\), so \(\hat{\beta}_j g_{ij}\) estimates the difference in expected trait between person \(i\) and a person with no copy at variant \(j\), and the score adds these differences over the \(M\) variants. The sum assumes that the variants’ effects add up and that each effect is counted once; variants in strong linkage disequilibrium carry largely the same association, so score methods keep one variant per correlated group or adjust the weights for LD. In the figure below, the eight products add up to 0.35 for person \(i\). Counting the other allele of variant \(j\) turns \(g_{ij}\) into \(2 - g_{ij}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_j\) into \(-\hat{\beta}_j\), which changes every person’s score by the same amount, \(-2\hat{\beta}_j\); only differences between people carry information, so scores are usually standardized across people to mean 0 and SD 1. Weights estimated in the same people would also fit the random noise of that sample, and the score would look more accurate there than in new people.

Polygenic scores are used to stratify risk, usually together with other risk factors. Lecture 1 slide 20 shows BOADICEA, a breast cancer risk model that combines questionnaire risk factors, mammographic density, a 313-SNP polygenic score, family history and rare variants in genes such as CHEK2.56 For coronary artery disease, a genome-wide score identified 8.0% of the population with more than three times the risk of the rest, about 20 times as many people as carry rare monogenic variants with similar risk.57

Scores work best in people similar to the GWAS samples. About 79% of GWAS participants were described as “of European descent”, against 16% of the world’s population. In UK Biobank, scores for 17 traits built from GWAS of samples labeled European were on average 1.6-fold less accurate in the groups Martin et al. label Hispanic/Latino American and South Asian, 2.0-fold in East Asian and 4.5-fold in African than in the European group.58 Allele frequencies and LD patterns differ between populations (ancestry page), so the same weights tag the causal variants differently.

Key points
  • One variant: the additive model \(y_i = \alpha + \beta g_i + \boldsymbol{\gamma}^\top \mathbf{c}_i + \varepsilon_i\). \(\beta\) is the per-allele effect in trait units, or a log odds ratio for binary traits.
  • Frequency × effect: a variant explains \(R^2 = 2p(1 - p)\beta^2\) of the variance (\(\beta\) in SD units), and a GWAS needs about \(40/R^2\) people for 80% power at p < 5 × 10⁻⁸. Common, small effects need hundreds of thousands of people.
  • Polygenic traits and heritability: many small effects sum to a near-normal distribution, and diseases are modeled with a liability threshold. Heritability is a population-specific share of variance, estimated from twins (\(h^2\)) or from SNPs (SNP-heritability).
  • Causation: associations can come from LD, ancestry confounding or social pathways (margarine). Mendelian randomization uses germline genotype as an instrument under three conditions: relevance, independence and exclusion restriction.
  • From GWAS to use: a GWAS is one regression per variant. Its summary statistics are used for fine-mapping, drug-target discovery (PCSK9, ANGPTL3, BCL11A), Mendelian randomization, TWAS and polygenic scores, which are less accurate in groups under-represented in GWAS.

Check your understanding

A SNP has effect-allele frequency 0.3 and a per-allele effect of 0.05 SD on a quantitative trait. What share of the variance does it explain, and roughly how many people does a GWAS need to detect it at p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ with 80% power?

Variance explained: \(R^2 = 2 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 \times 0.05^2 = 0.00105\), about 0.1%. The sample size is about \(40/R^2 = 40/0.00105 \approx 38{,}000\) people. Check it in the widget by setting \(p = 0.3\) and \(\beta = 0.05\).

Why are common variants with large effects on serious diseases so rare?

Negative (purifying) selection: alleles that strongly reduce survival or reproduction are removed from the population before they can become common. Large-effect alleles therefore stay rare, and the common variants that remain mostly have small effects.

Twin correlations for a trait are \(r_{\mathrm{MZ}} = 0.6\) and \(r_{\mathrm{DZ}} = 0.4\). Estimate \(h^2\), the shared-environment share and the unique-environment share, and name the assumptions. Does \(h^2 = 0.4\) mean that 40% of each person’s trait value comes from their genes?

\(h^2 \approx 2 \times (0.6 - 0.4) = 0.4\); shared environment \(c^2 \approx 0.6 - 0.4 = 0.2\); unique environment plus measurement error \(e^2 \approx 1 - 0.6 = 0.4\). The calculation assumes that identical and fraternal pairs share their environments to the same degree and that genetic effects are additive. No: heritability is a share of the variation among people in one population and environment, not a split of one person’s trait value. It can differ between populations and over time, and it says nothing about how changeable the trait is.

A GWAS of margarine use finds a genome-wide significant peak at APOE. Give three explanations other than “this gene affects taste”, and say which one Lecture 1 favors.

(1) The tested SNPs are in LD with a causal variant that affects something else; (2) confounding by ancestry; (3) a real but indirect social pathway: alleles that raise LDL cholesterol lead to diagnoses, statins and dietary advice, which change the spread people buy. Lecture 1 (slides 32–35) favors the social pathway, supported by the matching signals for LDL levels, statin use and heart-attack risk.

Why is an APOE variant a risky instrument for the effect of LDL cholesterol on heart attacks in Mendelian randomization?

Because of pleiotropy: APOE variants also affect other traits (for example, the risk of Alzheimer’s disease), so the variant may influence the outcome through paths other than LDL cholesterol. That would break the exclusion restriction (condition 3).

A polygenic score for coronary artery disease was built from a GWAS whose participants were genetically similar to the 1000 Genomes EUR samples. Why might it predict less accurately in people genetically similar to the AFR samples?

Allele frequencies and LD patterns differ between groups, so the SNPs in the score tag the causal variants less well, and some causal variants common in that group may be rare or missing in the GWAS. Martin et al. found accuracy about 4.5-fold lower in the group they label African, on average across 17 UK Biobank traits.
