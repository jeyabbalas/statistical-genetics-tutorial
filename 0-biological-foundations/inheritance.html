Inheritance: from parents to children

You receive one copy of each chromosome from each parent. This page shows how meiosis builds those copies, why alleles at nearby positions tend to be inherited together, how single-gene traits run in families, and how relatives come to share long stretches of identical DNA. These are the facts behind the relatedness checks, linkage studies and Mendelian randomization you will meet in Lecture 1.

Core for Lecture 1 ≈ 40 min Shows up in the data as: kinship tables, PLINK PI_HAT, the cM column of a .bim file
Mental model

Each transmitted chromosome is built from segments of the parent's two copies

A parent passes on one copy of each chromosome, and that copy switches between the parent's own two copies at a few crossover points. So a child carries long, unbroken segments from each grandparent, and relatives share long segments copied from the same ancestor.

  • Segregation. At any one position, which of a parent's two alleles a child receives is a random draw: each allele with probability ½, independently for every child.
  • Few crossovers. A sperm carries on average about 27 crossovers across the 22 autosomes and an egg about 421, between about 0.5 and 3.3 per chromosome2. So nearby positions are usually inherited together (linkage), while distant positions and different chromosomes are inherited independently.
  • Shared segments. Relatives carry copies of the same ancestral segments: they are identical by descent (IBD). A parent and child share exactly one copy everywhere; full siblings share 0, 1 or 2 copies with probabilities ¼, ½ and ¼.
  • In the data matrix. Relatedness appears as pairs of rows (participants) whose genotypes agree more often than chance allows. Linkage appears as neighboring columns (variants) whose alleles are inherited together within families.
  • Fixed at conception. The germline genotype is set when sperm and egg combine and is not changed by later behavior or disease. This is why genotypes can serve as naturally randomized variables (Mendelian randomization).

Two kinds of cell division Core

Before a cell divides, it copies its DNA (DNA replication, described on the DNA page). For a while each chromosome then consists of two identical DNA molecules, called sister chromatids, held together at the centromere. Human cells use two kinds of division.

  • Mitosis is the division used for growth and repair. A cell with 46 chromosomes copies them and divides once, giving two cells with the same 46 chromosomes. Every cell of the body descends from the zygote through rounds of mitosis, which is why (almost) every cell carries the same genome.
  • Meiosis happens only in germline cells of the testes and ovaries and makes gametes (sperm or eggs). The cell copies its DNA once and then divides twice, giving four cells with 23 chromosomes each: one copy of each chromosome, so the cells are haploid3. Each gamete gets only one of the two copies of each chromosome, and that copy is a new combination of the parent's two copies.

In males, one meiosis yields four sperm. In females, one meiosis yields one egg; the other products are small polar bodies that do not become eggs. Female meiosis starts before birth, pauses until the egg matures after puberty, and finishes only if the egg is fertilized4.

Meiosis step by step Core

  1. Copy. Each of the 46 chromosomes is replicated into two sister chromatids.
  2. Pair. The two homologous chromosomes of each pair (one inherited from the person's mother, one from the father) line up along their whole length.
  3. Cross over. At a few places, the paired chromosomes break and rejoin, swapping matching segments. This exchange is called crossing over or recombination, and each exchange point is a crossover. Each pair needs at least one crossover to separate correctly3.
  4. Division I. The two homologous chromosomes of each pair move to different daughter cells. Which copy goes to which cell is random and independent for each pair.
  5. Division II. The sister chromatids separate, giving four haploid cells.

How many crossovers? Crossovers are counted in families. Compare a parent's genotypes with a child's along a chromosome: each switch between the parent's two copies marks one crossover. In more than 4,200 families, a transmitted set of autosomes carried on average 41.6 crossovers per egg and 26.6 per sperm1. Female crossover rates are thus about 1.6 times those of males1, and the number in eggs rises with the mother's age1. Per chromosome, the average ranges from about 0.5 (chromosome 21 in a sperm) to about 3.3 (chromosome 1 in an egg)2. These counts are per transmitted copy; the meiosis itself contains about twice as many exchanges, because each product carries a given exchange with probability ½ (figure above).

When a chromosome pair fails to separate correctly, a gamete ends up with an extra or a missing chromosome. This kind of aneuploidy occurs in at least 5% of clinically recognized pregnancies5; an extra chromosome 21 (trisomy 21, the cause of Down syndrome) is the best-known example that survives to birth.

Mendel's laws in modern terms Core

Gregor Mendel described two rules of inheritance from crosses of pea plants, published in 18666, decades before chromosomes and DNA were understood. Both Mendel's laws follow directly from meiosis.

The law of segregation. A person has two alleles at each autosomal locus, one on each homologous chromosome. Each gamete receives exactly one of them, chosen with probability ½, independently for every child. In data-science terms: if \(T = 1\) when a heterozygous parent transmits allele a and \(T = 0\) when it transmits the other allele, then \(T \sim \Bernoulli(\tfrac{1}{2})\), independently across children and across the two parents.

A Punnett square lists every combination of one allele from the sperm and one from the egg. Take two parents who each carry one sickle allele S at rs334 in HBB and one typical allele A (genotype A/S; see the variation page). Here A and S name the hemoglobin alleles (HbA and HbS), not DNA bases. A child's number of S alleles is the sum of two independent \(\Bernoulli(\tfrac{1}{2})\) draws, one from each parent:

\[ G = T_{\mathrm{egg}} + T_{\mathrm{sperm}} \sim \Binomial\bigl(2, \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr) \]
\(T_{\mathrm{egg}}, T_{\mathrm{sperm}}\)
1 if the egg (or the sperm) carries S, 0 if it carries A. Both parents are A/S, so each is \(\Bernoulli(\tfrac{1}{2})\).
\(G\)
The child's genotype, coded as the number of copies of S: 0, 1 or 2.

The egg and the sperm come from meioses in two different people, so the two draws are independent and each of the four boxes of the Punnett square has probability \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{4}\). A child has \(G = 0\) only with A from both parents and \(G = 2\) only with S from both, one box each, while \(G = 1\) arises in two ways (S in the egg and A in the sperm, or the reverse). So \(P(G = 0) = \tfrac{1}{4}\), \(P(G = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(P(G = 2) = \tfrac{1}{4}\): the classic 1 : 2 : 1 ratio, with a mean of \(2 \times \tfrac{1}{2} = 1\) copy. Every child is a new pair of draws, so given the parents' genotypes, siblings' genotypes are independent and each child has the same chance of two copies, \(\tfrac{1}{4}\). If one parent were A/A instead, its draw would always be 0, and a child would have 0 or 1 copy with probability \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) each.

The law of independent assortment. Alleles at loci on different chromosomes are transmitted independently of each other, because each chromosome pair separates independently in division I.

Linkage is the exception. Two loci close together on the same chromosome are not transmitted independently: unless a crossover falls between them, a gamete carries both alleles from the same parental copy. The next section makes this precise.

Linkage, recombination and the genetic map Core

Take two loci on the same chromosome. One of a parent's copies carries alleles A and B; the other carries a and b. A gamete is recombinant for the two loci if it carries A with b, or a with B. That happens when an odd number of crossovers falls between the loci.

The recombination fraction \(\theta\) is the probability that a gamete is recombinant for the two loci. It ranges from 0 (loci so close that crossovers almost never separate them) to 0.5 (loci far apart, or on different chromosomes, which are inherited independently). Two loci with \(\theta < 0.5\) are linked, and this tendency of nearby alleles to be inherited together is genetic linkage.

Genetic distance. Distance along a genetic map is measured in morgans: one morgan is the length of chromosome over which a transmitted copy carries, on average, one crossover. One centimorgan (cM) is 0.01 morgan, which corresponds to a 1% chance that a crossover separates two loci in one generation8. If crossovers along a transmitted copy occur independently at random positions (a Poisson process, Haldane's model), the recombination fraction depends only on the genetic distance between the loci7:

\[ \begin{gathered} N \sim \Poisson(d) \\[4pt] \theta = P(N \text{ odd}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1 - e^{-2d}\bigr) \end{gathered} \]
\(d\)
Genetic distance between the two loci in morgans (\(d = 0.01\) for 1 cM): the expected number of crossovers between them on one transmitted copy.
\(N\)
Number of crossovers between the two loci on one transmitted copy, that is, in one gamete. In Haldane's model it is Poisson with mean \(d\).
\(\theta\)
Recombination fraction: the probability that the gamete is recombinant for the two loci.

Each crossover between the loci switches the gamete from one of the parent's copies to the other, and a second crossover switches it back, so the gamete is recombinant exactly when \(N\) is odd. For a Poisson count, \(P(N \text{ even}) - P(N \text{ odd}) = \sum_n e^{-d}(-d)^n/n! = e^{-d}e^{-d} = e^{-2d}\), and the two probabilities add up to 1; solving for \(P(N \text{ odd})\) gives the formula. For small \(d\), \(e^{-2d} \approx 1 - 2d\), so \(\theta \approx d\): two or more crossovers between the loci are rare, and \(\theta\) is close to the probability of one (1 cM gives \(\theta = 0.0099\)). As \(d\) grows, odd and even counts become equally likely and \(\theta\) approaches ½ (\(\theta = 0.32\) at 50 cM and 0.43 at 100 cM), but it never exceeds ½, because \(e^{-2d} > 0\).

Real crossovers tend to keep their distance from each other (crossover interference), so this formula is an approximation, but the cap at ½ holds.

The genetic map. A genetic map gives each position its distance in cM from the start of the chromosome. Averaged over the genome, 1 cM corresponds to roughly 1 Mb8 (1.13 cM per Mb in the deCODE map built from 1,257 meioses9). Locally the rate varies enormously. Most crossovers fall in narrow recombination hotspots: more than 25,000 hotspots were found in a 2005 map11, and in Phase II data of the International HapMap Project (a catalog of common variants genotyped in samples from several populations) 32,996 hotspots accounted for about 60% of recombination in about 6% of the sequence10. A DNA-binding protein, PRDM9, is a major determinant of where hotspots lie in humans and mice: it binds specific DNA sequences and so marks where recombination starts12. The female map is longer than the male map (4,281 cM versus 2,590 cM for the autosomes in the deCODE map9).

In the data The cM column of a PLINK .bim file

PLINK is a widely used command-line program for genotype data, and its .bim file lists one variant per line. The third column holds the variant's genetic-map position in morgans or centimorgans; PLINK accepts 0 when it is unknown13, and many files leave it at 0. Genetic maps matter for tools that model recombination: phasing (working out which alleles sit together on each chromosome copy; see the haplotypes and LD page), genotype imputation (predicting genotypes at variants that were not measured; see From a blood sample to genotypes), and the detection of shared segments between relatives, whose lengths are reported in cM.

Linkage analysis in families

Before genome-wide association studies (GWAS), which test millions of variants for association with a trait in large samples of mostly unrelated people, most disease genes were located by linkage analysis. Researchers genotyped markers spread across the genome in families with several affected members and asked whether the disease and a marker allele are passed down together more often than \(\theta = 0.5\) allows. The evidence is a LOD score: the base-10 logarithm of the likelihood ratio comparing \(\theta < 0.5\) (linked) with \(\theta = 0.5\) (not linked):

\[ \mathrm{LOD}(\theta) = \log_{10} \frac{L(\theta)}{L(\tfrac{1}{2})} \]
\(\theta\)
Recombination fraction between the disease locus and the marker: the unknown parameter, below 0.5 if the two are linked.
\(L(\theta)\)
Likelihood: the probability of the observed marker genotypes and disease statuses of the family members, computed for a given \(\theta\) under an assumed mode of inheritance and penetrance.
\(L(\tfrac{1}{2})\)
The same probability for \(\theta = 0.5\), when the disease locus and the marker are not linked.
\(\mathrm{LOD}(\theta)\)
LOD score at \(\theta\). Positive values favor linkage and negative values favor no linkage; studies usually report its maximum over \(\theta\).

The ratio says how many times more probable the family data are if the loci are linked at \(\theta\) than if they are unlinked, and the base-10 logarithm turns a ratio of 1000 into 3. Families are independent, so their likelihoods multiply and their LOD scores add. In the simplest case every meiosis can be scored as recombinant or not: \(r\) recombinants among \(n\) scored meioses give \(L(\theta) = \theta^r(1 - \theta)^{n - r}\) and \(L(\tfrac{1}{2}) = (\tfrac{1}{2})^n\). With no recombinants the maximum is at \(\theta = 0\), where \(\mathrm{LOD} = n \log_{10} 2 = 0.301n\), so 10 such meioses give a LOD score of 3.01.

A LOD score of 3 or more (a likelihood ratio of 1000 : 1) was the classic threshold for declaring linkage14. Huntington disease was linked to a marker on chromosome 4 in 198315, and early-onset familial breast cancer to chromosome 17q21 in 1990 (LOD 5.98)16; that region contains the BRCA1 gene, at band 17q21.3148.

Linkage works best for rare variants with large effects that nearly always cause disease, because the disease must follow the marker through each family. For common traits shaped by many variants of small effect, GWAS are more powerful.

Single-gene (Mendelian) inheritance Core

A phenotype is an observable trait or disease status. A Mendelian disease is one in which the genotype at a single gene largely determines the phenotype, so the disease follows Mendel's laws in families. Most traits studied by GWAS are different: many variants, each with a small effect (see From genotype to phenotype). But the Mendelian vocabulary is how geneticists describe the genotype-to-phenotype map at one locus. Write the genotype as the number of copies of an allele: 0, 1 or 2.

  • Dominant: one copy is enough to produce the phenotype, so genotypes 1 and 2 both show it. Huntington disease is dominant. It is caused by an expanded run of the three-base sequence CAG, repeated many times in a row in the HTT gene; people with 40 or more repeats almost always develop it18. Familial hypercholesterolemia (inherited very high blood cholesterol) caused by variants in LDLR, APOB or PCSK9 is dominant too, and two altered copies cause a more severe form19.
  • Recessive: only genotype 2 shows the phenotype. People with genotype 1 are carriers: usually unaffected, but they can pass the allele on. Cystic fibrosis and sickle cell disease are recessive17; two carrier parents have a ¼ chance per child of an affected child (Punnett square above).
  • Codominant: a heterozygote shows the products of both alleles. In the ABO blood group, A and B are codominant (genotype A/B gives blood group AB) and O is recessive17.
  • Additive: each copy shifts a quantitative trait by the same amount, so heterozygotes are halfway between the two homozygotes. The additive model with genotype coded 0, 1, 2 is the default in GWAS.
  • X-linked: the gene is on the X chromosome. Males have one X, so one copy of an X-linked recessive allele is enough to affect a male, while females usually need two. A father passes his X only to his daughters, so there is no father-to-son transmission17. Hemophilia is an example.
Common confusion Dominant and recessive are defined for one phenotype at a time

The same allele can be recessive for one phenotype and dominant for another. The sickle allele is recessive for sickle cell disease, yet one copy already gives strong protection against severe malaria: in one study of children, carriers were almost 90% protected against severe or complicated malaria20. The ANGPTL3 family below is another example. Always say "recessive for this phenotype".

Penetrance. Penetrance is the probability that a person with a given genotype shows the phenotype, \(P(\text{phenotype} \mid \text{genotype})\). When some people with the genotype never develop the disease, penetrance is called incomplete or reduced21. Women carrying a disease-causing BRCA1 variant have about a 72% cumulative risk of breast cancer by age 80, and BRCA2 carriers about 69%22: high, but not 100%. Penetrance is often reported by age, and it can depend on other genes and on environment.

In the data The X chromosome needs its own handling

Outside the pseudoautosomal regions a male has one X, so he cannot be heterozygous there. PLINK logs such "heterozygous haploid" calls (most often caused by a pseudoautosomal region that was not coded separately, or by a sample whose sex is recorded wrongly) and treats them as missing in analyses; it codes the pseudoautosomal region as a separate chromosome, XY (code 25), so that this region, where males carry two copies, can be analyzed with autosomal methods13. PLINK's relatedness command (--genome, below) uses autosomal variants only13. In UK Biobank, genetic sex is in field 22001 (see the cells and chromosomes page).

A family story: ANGPTL3 and very low LDL cholesterol

LDL cholesterol is the cholesterol carried in the blood by low-density lipoprotein particles. People who inherit variants that keep their LDL lower throughout life have less coronary heart disease (disease caused by narrowing of the arteries that supply the heart)47. Lecture 1 (slide 15) tells a "forward genetics" story, which goes from an unusual phenotype to the gene. Researchers studied a family in which four siblings had combined hypolipidemia: very low levels of LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol and triglycerides (blood fats). They read the protein-coding parts of the genome (exome sequencing) of two affected siblings. Both carried two different nonsense variants in ANGPTL3, S17X and E129X (the notation gives the original amino acid, its position in the protein, and X for a premature stop), one on each copy of the gene: they were compound heterozygotes, with no working copy. Among the 38 family members tested (the two siblings included), 4 carried both variants, 13 carried one and 21 carried neither23.

Lecture 1 Slide 15 · Forward genetics with ANGPTL3

In Lecture 1 (slide 15) you will see: "a healthy individual with extremely low levels of LDL cholesterol. Three siblings of this proband also had very low LDL levels … all carried two copies of a rare loss-of-function variant in the ANGPTL3 gene." It means: the proband is the family member through whom the family came to the researchers' attention; "two copies" means both copies of the gene are knocked out (in this family by two different variants), the recessive pattern above; "loss-of-function" means the variant destroys the protein's activity. The slide's "Forward Genetics: Phenotype → Genotype" names the direction of the search. The slide ends: "Drugs that mimic the effect of this mutation are more likely to be safe and effective." In 58,335 sequenced people, carriers of one loss-of-function copy had lower triglycerides, HDL cholesterol and LDL cholesterol, and lower odds of coronary artery disease (odds ratio 0.59)24. The antibody drug evinacumab, which binds and blocks the ANGPTL3 protein, lowered LDL cholesterol by 47.1% from baseline (versus a 1.9% rise with placebo) in a trial of 65 people with homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, the severe form with two altered copies25, and was approved in the US in February 202126. Listen for the logic: a healthy person who naturally lacks a protein suggests that a drug blocking it may be safe.

Pedigrees Optional

A pedigree is a standardized family diagram. A filled symbol means affected; a horizontal line joins two partners, and lines below it connect them to their children; an arrow marks the proband; Roman numerals number the generations. Squares represent men and boys and circles women and girls49. Since the 2022 update of the standard, symbols follow a person's gender, a diamond is used for people who are non-binary or whose sex or gender is not known, and sex assigned at birth is written below the symbol when it differs (for example AFAB, assigned female at birth), because it can matter for genetic risk27. Carriers are drawn here half-filled, a common textbook convention; check the key of any published pedigree, because the 2022 update also revised how carrier status is shown27. A double line between partners marks consanguinity: the partners are blood relatives (for example first cousins)49, which raises the chance that a child receives two copies of the same rare recessive allele from a shared ancestor.

Relatedness and identity by descent Core

Two alleles are identical by state (IBS) if they have the same DNA sequence; for a SNP, the same base. They are identical by descent (IBD) if they are copies of one allele carried by a recent common ancestor and passed down through the family tree. IBD alleles are almost always IBS (unless a new mutation or a genotyping error intervenes). IBS alleles need not be IBD: two unrelated people often carry the same common allele by chance.

At each position, a pair of people shares 0, 1 or 2 copies IBD. Averaging over the genome gives three fractions that sum to 1, and two summaries of them are in common use:

\[ \begin{gathered} \pi = \frac{k_1}{2} + k_2 \\[6pt] \varphi = \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{k_1}{4} + \frac{k_2}{2} \end{gathered} \]
\(k_0, k_1, k_2\)
Fractions of the genome at which the pair shares 0, 1 or 2 copies IBD, for two people who are not inbred; \(k_0 + k_1 + k_2 = 1\). PLINK's estimates of them are called Z0, Z1 and Z2; the table below lists their expected values as IBD0, IBD1 and IBD2.
\(\pi\)
Proportion IBD: the fraction of the pair's genomes shared identical by descent, from 0 to 1. PLINK reports its estimate \(\hat{\pi}\) as PI_HAT. Here \(\pi\) is a proportion, not the circle constant.
\(\varphi\)
Kinship coefficient: the probability that an allele drawn at random from one person and an allele drawn at random from the other, at the same locus, are IBD; from 0 to ½.

Each person carries two copies at a position. Where the pair shares 2 copies IBD, all of each person's copies are shared; where it shares 1, half of them; where it shares 0, none. Averaging over positions gives \(\pi = 0 \cdot k_0 + \tfrac{1}{2} k_1 + 1 \cdot k_2\). For \(\varphi\), draw one copy from each person. Where 2 copies are shared, the copy drawn from the second person is the IBD partner of the copy drawn from the first with probability ½; where 1 is shared, both draws must pick the shared copies, with probability \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{4}\). So \(\varphi = \tfrac{1}{4} k_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} k_2 = \pi/2\). A parent and child (\(k_1 = 1\)) and full siblings (\(k_0 = \tfrac{1}{4}\), \(k_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}\), \(k_2 = \tfrac{1}{4}\)) both have \(\pi = \tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(\varphi = \tfrac{1}{4}\), so these summaries cannot tell the two relationships apart, but \(k_0\) can. Identical twins (\(k_2 = 1\)) have the largest values, \(\pi = 1\) and \(\varphi = \tfrac{1}{2}\).

Relatives are grouped by degree of relationship. First-degree relatives (parents, children, full siblings) have expected kinship ¼; second-degree relatives (half siblings, grandparents and grandchildren, aunts or uncles and nieces or nephews) ⅛; third-degree relatives (first cousins, for example) 1/16. Each additional degree halves the expected kinship. The table lists the expected values: kinship and IBD0 come from Table 1 of the paper describing KING, a widely used program that infers relationships from genotypes28, and IBD1 and IBD2 follow from \(\pi = \mathrm{IBD1}/2 + \mathrm{IBD2}\) and \(\mathrm{IBD0} + \mathrm{IBD1} + \mathrm{IBD2} = 1\).

Sharing in 500 simulated pairs

What to notice. Each sperm or egg switches between the parent's two copies only a few times, so the children inherit long blocks. Full siblings share 2 copies on some blocks, 1 on others and 0 on the rest; averaged over 500 pairs the fractions are close to ¼, ½ and ¼, and the proportion shared is close to ½. On one chromosome the proportion varies widely from pair to pair (on chromosome 21 the model's standard deviation is about 0.25); across all 22 autosomes it stays near 0.5 with a standard deviation of about 0.04. Choose "Parent and child": every pair shares exactly one copy everywhere, so the histogram collapses to a single bar at 0.5. Half siblings share about 0.25; on a short chromosome such as 21 their histogram piles up at 0 and 0.5, because a sperm often carries no crossover there.

Model. Grandparents and parents are unrelated. The number of crossovers in a sperm or egg is Poisson with mean equal to the male or female map length of the chromosome (sex-specific maps of Bhérer et al.2; chromosome lengths from GRCh38), and crossover positions are uniform along the chromosome. The model ignores crossover interference, so its spread is slightly larger than in real siblings (standard deviation 0.036 in 4,401 sibling pairs29). "Model theory" gives the standard deviation computed analytically for this model.

Real data agree: across 4,401 sibling pairs, genome-wide IBD sharing ranged from 0.374 to 0.617 (mean 0.498, standard deviation 0.036). This natural variation can be used to estimate how much of a trait's variation is genetic using siblings alone29. The variance of realized sharing has been worked out for many types of relatives30.

Twins, trios and families

Monozygotic (identical) twins develop from one zygote that splits, so their germline genomes are identical apart from a few mutations that arise during early development (on average 5.2 such differences per pair in one study)31. In genotype data they are indistinguishable from a duplicate sample. Dizygotic (fraternal) twins come from two eggs fertilized by two sperm, so genetically they are ordinary full siblings who were born together. Comparing how similar identical and fraternal twin pairs are for a trait is the classic way to estimate heritability, the share of trait variation due to genetic differences32 (details on the genotype-to-phenotype page). A related observation is familial aggregation: a trait or disease is more common among relatives of affected people than in the general population, which suggests, but does not prove, a genetic contribution, because relatives also share environments.

A trio is two parents and their child. At most SNPs, trios reveal which allele the child received from which parent (the exception is a SNP where the child and both parents are heterozygous), and that fixes the order of alleles along each chromosome copy (phasing). The International HapMap Project used this design: it sampled 30 parent–child trios from Yoruba people in Ibadan, Nigeria, and 30 trios of Utah residents with northern and western European ancestry33. UK Biobank contains 1,066 parent–child trios; 696 of them were used to measure the accuracy of its statistical phasing34. Trios also catch errors: if both parents have genotype 0 at a SNP, the child must have 0, so a child with 1 or 2 signals a genotyping error or a sample mix-up (a Mendelian error; see the samples page).

Relatives in genotype data: PLINK, KING and UK Biobank Core

Why it matters for GWAS. A standard association test treats participants as independent observations. Relatives are not independent: they share genotypes IBD and often share households. Relatedness that the investigators do not know about, called cryptic relatedness, inflates test statistics. In well-designed studies of outbred populations the effect is usually negligible, but it can be serious when sampling favors relatives, or in founder populations that grew recently from a small number of people35. Duplicate samples and identical twins add no information and must be identified.

How it is estimated. Relatedness is estimated from genotypes at many thousands of SNPs across the genome. PLINK's --genome estimates Z0, Z1, Z2 and PI_HAT for every pair from IBS counts and population allele frequencies36. KING estimates the kinship coefficient in a way that does not rely on accurate population allele frequencies, which makes it robust to population structure (ancestry differences between people), and it also reports IBS0, the fraction of SNPs at which the pair shares no allele, such as AA versus GG28. A parent and child share one allele IBD at every position, so they are never opposite homozygotes and their IBS0 is close to 0. Full siblings share nothing IBD on about a quarter of the genome, where opposite homozygotes can occur, so their IBS0 is above 0. That is how first-degree pairs are split into parent–child and sibling pairs. KING's documentation recommends plotting kinship against IBS028.

In the data The relatedness step in the Session 1 practical

In step 3 of the practical's quality control (QC), on the PennCATH data, you will keep a subset of SNPs that are not strongly correlated with nearby SNPs, using --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.3. This is called LD pruning; LD (linkage disequilibrium) is the correlation between alleles at nearby variants, covered on the haplotypes and LD page. In step 4 you run --genome on that subset37. The output file has one row per pair of samples:

penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.LD0.3.genome (first columns)

Z0, Z1 and Z2 estimate \(P(\mathrm{IBD} = 0)\), \(P(\mathrm{IBD} = 1)\) and \(P(\mathrm{IBD} = 2)\), which are \(k_0\), \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) above, and \(\mathrm{PI\_HAT} = P(\mathrm{IBD} = 2) + 0.5 \times P(\mathrm{IBD} = 1)\) is the matching estimate of \(\pi\)13. The practical plots Z0 against Z1, the view in panel A above37. A widely used rule removes one member of each pair with PI_HAT above 0.1875, halfway between the second- and third-degree expectations, keeping the member with the better call rate (the fraction of SNPs successfully genotyped)38.

In the data Relatives in UK Biobank

UK Biobank estimated kinship with KING for all pairs of genotyped participants, after excluding 977 people whose data (for example, a high missing rate) would give unreliable estimates34. In total, 147,731 participants (30.3%) have at least one relative of third degree or closer, forming 107,162 related pairs: 179 identical-twin pairs, 6,276 parent–child pairs, 22,666 full-sibling pairs (roughly twice as many as expected in a random sample of the eligible UK population, possibly because relatives of participants were more likely to take part), 11,113 second-degree pairs and 66,928 third-degree pairs34. Where to find it:

  • Field 22021, "Genetic kinship to other participants", coded −1 (excluded from kinship inference), 0 (no kinship found), 1 (at least one relative identified) and 10 (ten or more third-degree relatives identified)39.
  • The relatedness file lists each pair related up to the third degree, with columns ID1, ID2, HetHet (fraction of SNPs where both are heterozygous), IBS0 and Kinship, all from KING40.
  • Field 22020 flags the unrelated, high-quality subset used to compute the genetic principal components (summary axes of genetic variation, used to describe ancestry; see the ancestry page): 406,654 participants in the current Showcase39; Bycroft et al. reported 407,21934.

What to do about relatives. There are two main strategies. (1) Remove one member of each related pair, for example with KING's --unrelated option, which extracts a list of unrelated people28; UK Biobank computed its principal components on an unrelated subset (field 22020, above)34. (2) Keep everyone and use a method that models relatedness, such as a linear mixed model (a regression whose error terms are correlated between people in proportion to their genetic similarity) or REGENIE, the whole-genome regression method used in Session 2, which accounts for relatedness and population structure41. For polygenic scores, close relatives shared between the GWAS used to build the score and the target sample used to test it inflate the apparent association, so first- and second-degree relatives across the two should be removed42. The same applies to the training and test sets of any machine-learning model.

Why inherited genotypes are special variables Core

Three properties make a germline genotype unusual among the variables in an epidemiological dataset.

  1. Fixed at conception. It is set when sperm and egg combine and is (almost) the same in every cell for life, so it can be measured from a blood sample at any age. Somatic mutations, which arise later in some cells, are the exception (see the cells and chromosomes page).
  2. Not changed by the outcome. Disease, treatment and behavior do not change the germline DNA. So a genotype–disease association cannot arise from reverse causation, where the outcome causes the exposure (the factor whose effect is being studied)43.
  3. Randomly assigned within families. Given the parents' genotypes, which of each parent's two copies a child receives at a locus is a \(\Bernoulli(\tfrac{1}{2})\) draw made at meiosis, independently of the family's circumstances.

These properties are the basis of Mendelian randomization (MR). Suppose a variant changes an exposure such as LDL cholesterol. Because the variant was assigned at meiosis, independently of confounders (factors that affect both the exposure and the outcome), a difference in outcome between genotype groups can be attributed to the exposure. The genotype then serves as an instrumental variable: a variable that is associated with the exposure, is independent of the confounders, and affects the outcome only through the exposure43. The assumptions and their failure modes are covered on the genotype-to-phenotype page.

Caveat. Across a whole population (rather than within a family), genotypes are not randomly assigned: allele frequencies differ with ancestry, and ancestry is linked to environment, so associations can be confounded by population structure (ancestry page). Parents' genotypes can also influence a child through the family environment. Comparisons within families restore the randomization: among 178,086 siblings, within-family GWAS estimates were smaller than population estimates for traits such as height and educational attainment, but similar for molecular traits such as LDL cholesterol44.

In Lecture 1 (slides 22–24) you will see Mendelian randomization defined as using "genetic variation as an instrumental variable to assess the causal relationship between an exposure and an outcome", with diagrams of genetic variant → exposure of interest → outcome, confounders pointing at exposure and outcome, and three assumptions. It means: the "randomization" is the \(\Bernoulli(\tfrac{1}{2})\) draw at meiosis described on this page. Assumption 1 says the variant affects the exposure. Assumption 2, drawn as a crossed-out arrow between the variant and the confounders, says the two are not associated; meiosis supports it, because confounders arising later in life cannot change \(G\) (the "no \(U \to G\)" in the figure above). Assumption 3 says the variant affects the outcome only through the exposure. Slide 23 reports that MR studies suggest CRP (C-reactive protein, a blood marker of inflammation) and HDL cholesterol (written "HLD" on the slide) "are not causally associated with coronary heart disease, consistent with clinical trials". Listen for the "pesky details" that slide 24 says its short video glosses over, such as confounding by ancestry and pleiotropy (a variant affecting the outcome through a route other than the exposure).

Key points
  • Meiosis gives each gamete one copy of each chromosome, built from segments of the parent's two copies; a sperm carries about 27 and an egg about 42 crossovers across the autosomes.
  • Segregation and assortment: each allele is transmitted with probability ½, so a child of two heterozygous parents has \(\Binomial(2, \tfrac{1}{2})\) copies; chromosomes assort independently.
  • Linkage: the recombination fraction \(\theta\) runs from 0 to 0.5; 1 cM ≈ 1% recombination ≈ 1 Mb on average, but most crossovers fall in hotspots and the female map is longer.
  • Mendelian modes (dominant, recessive, codominant, additive, X-linked) describe a genotype-to-phenotype map for a given phenotype; penetrance is \(P(\text{phenotype} \mid \text{genotype})\) and is often incomplete.
  • IBD and kinship: parent–child pairs share exactly one copy everywhere; full siblings share ¼, ½, ¼ on average but vary pair to pair; kinship \(\varphi = \pi/2\), half the proportion IBD; KING separates degrees with cut-offs at 0.354, 0.177, 0.0884 and 0.0442 and uses IBS0 to tell parent–child from siblings.
  • In data: 30% of UK Biobank participants have a third-degree or closer relative in the cohort; remove relatives or model them, and keep relatives out of separate training and test sets.
  • Special variables: germline genotypes are fixed at conception and randomly assigned within families, which underlies Mendelian randomization; ancestry remains the main confounder.

Check your understanding

Two parents both carry one copy of a recessive disease allele. They have four children. What is the probability that none is affected, and that at least one is?

Each child is affected with probability ¼, independently. \(P(\text{none affected}) = (\tfrac{3}{4})^4 \approx 0.32\), so \(P(\text{at least one affected}) = 1 - (\tfrac{3}{4})^4 \approx 0.68\). The number affected is \(\Binomial(4, \tfrac{1}{4})\): for example, \(P(\text{exactly one}) = 4 \times \tfrac{1}{4} \times (\tfrac{3}{4})^3 \approx 0.42\).

Why can the recombination fraction between two loci never exceed 0.5, however far apart they are?

A gamete is recombinant when an odd number of crossovers falls between the loci. As the distance grows, odd and even counts become equally likely, so \(\theta\) approaches ½ (Haldane: \(\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - e^{-2d})\) for loci \(d\) morgans apart). Loci on different chromosomes also have \(\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}\) because of independent assortment.

KING reports two pairs, both with kinship ≈ 0.25. Pair 1 has IBS0 ≈ 0.0007; pair 2 has IBS0 ≈ 0.016. Which is parent–child and which are siblings?

Pair 1 is parent–child: they share exactly one allele IBD at every position, so they can never be opposite homozygotes (IBS0 ≈ 0 apart from genotyping errors). Pair 2 are full siblings: on about a quarter of the genome they share nothing IBD, so some SNPs show AA versus GG.

In the simulator, why is the spread of sibling sharing much smaller for the whole genome than for one chromosome?

Each chromosome contributes only a few long segments, so the shared fraction of one chromosome averages over few independent pieces and varies a lot. The genome-wide value averages over 22 independently inherited chromosomes and the roughly 136 crossovers in the two sperm and two eggs involved (2 × 26.6 + 2 × 41.6), so it concentrates near 0.5 (standard deviation about 0.04).

Can a father pass an X-linked allele to his son? What does this imply for an X-linked recessive disease?

No. A son receives his father's Y chromosome, and his single X comes from his mother. An X-linked recessive disease therefore passes from carrier mothers to affected sons, never from father to son, and a male needs only one copy to be affected.

A study finds that a germline variant is associated with a disease. Which explanation can you rule out, and which must you still consider?

Reverse causation is ruled out: the disease cannot change the germline genotype. Still consider confounding by ancestry (population structure), the possibility that a nearby correlated variant is the cause, and chance; within-family designs and adjustment for ancestry address the first.
