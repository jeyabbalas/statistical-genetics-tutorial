QC step 3: LD pruning

Relatedness, heterozygosity and principal components treat every SNP as a separate piece of evidence, but neighboring SNPs often carry the same information. This step slides a window along each chromosome and, from every pair of SNPs whose genotypes are strongly correlated, sets one aside. It changes no data: it writes two lists, the SNPs to keep and the SNPs set aside.

Slide 85 plink --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.3 Writes: .prune.in and .prune.out ≈ 45 min
Mental model

A window slides along each chromosome; from every correlated pair inside it, the SNP with the lower minor allele frequency goes to a list

Inside a window of 50 consecutive SNPs, PLINK computes r² for every pair and, while some pair is above 0.3, sets aside the SNP of that pair with the lower minor allele frequency; then it moves the window 5 SNPs on. The data are not changed: the SNPs kept go to .prune.in, which steps 4–6 read so that each group of correlated SNPs counts about once.

  • Why prune. Relatedness, heterozygosity and PCA add up evidence over SNPs; a block of correlated SNPs adds the same evidence many times.
  • The statistic: r² of allele counts. The squared correlation of two SNPs' 0/1/2 columns across people, which needs no haplotypes and is capped by the two allele frequencies.
  • A greedy rule in a sliding window. Pairs are checked in order; the first pair above the threshold loses its lower-MAF SNP, until no pair in the window is above it.
  • Lists, not a new fileset. .prune.in and .prune.out; steps 4–6 apply .prune.in with --extract, and the association analysis keeps every SNP.
  • Settings and limits. Window, step and threshold trade the number of SNPs kept against how independent they are; SNPs further apart than the window, and long-range LD regions, are not handled by pruning alone.

Why steps 4–6 need nearly independent SNPs Core

The practical gives the reason in two sentences: "Many downstream QC analyses, including PLINK's Identity-by-Descent (IBD), heterozygosity rate, and principal component analysis (PCA), can be influenced by correlated markers in linkage disequilibrium (LD). We therefore use an approximately LD-independent marker set for these analyses so that regions of high LD do not receive disproportionate weight."1 Each of the three analyses combines information across SNPs as if every SNP were a separate piece of evidence:

  • Relatedness (step 4). --genome counts, for each pair of people, the SNPs at which they share 0, 1 or 2 alleles and turns the counts into estimates of identity by descent. The PLINK documentation warns: "These calculations are not LD-aware. It is usually a good idea to perform some form of LD-based pruning before invoking them."4
  • Heterozygosity (step 5). --het compares each person's number of homozygous genotypes with the number expected from the allele frequencies, summed over SNPs. The documentation of --het and --ibc ends: "These calculations do not take LD into account. It is usually a good idea to perform some form of LD-based pruning before invoking them."4
  • Principal components (step 6). PCA finds the directions in which many SNPs vary together across people. A long stretch of correlated SNPs is such a direction even when it reflects the haplotypes of one region rather than genome-wide ancestry, so it can take a component of its own (Population structure · regions of strong LD). In the practical's words, "regions of strong or long-range LD can disproportionately influence individual components".1

The PLINK documentation gives an example of the size of the effect. --check-sex compares each person's recorded sex with an inbreeding coefficient F computed from the X chromosome: by default, F below 0.2 gives a female call and F above 0.8 a male call. Without LD pruning, among people recorded as female in the 1000 Genomes phase 1 data, "NA19332 has an F estimate just under 0.88, and there are others with F > 0.8", values that give male calls; after --indep-pairphase 20000 2000 0.5, "the largest female F estimate drops to about 0.66".4 No genotype changed. What changed is how many times each stretch of haplotype was counted. (PennCATH has no X chromosome, so the practical cannot run this check; see the .bim file.)

Pruning is therefore a means to these three analyses, not a quality filter. The SNPs it sets aside are not faulty, and a GWAS tests every SNP that passes quality control, not the pruned set.

Slides Slide 85 · Linkage disequilibrium: what it is and why it is studied

The slide defines LD as "the correlation between genetic markers in a population", measured with r², and states the property that shapes this step: "high r² between two SNPs requires both have similar MAFs" (the statistic section works out what that means for a threshold of 0.3). Two of its reasons to study LD use the same correlation structure as pruning. Counting "the number of independent tests in the genome" needs to know how many SNPs carry separate information (Lecture 0 · effective number of tests). Finding "a minimum number of variants that retain coverage" is tag SNP selection (Lecture 0 · tag SNPs): it keeps SNPs that are correlated with the SNPs left out, so that each one has a proxy. Pruning has the reverse aim: it keeps SNPs that are not correlated with each other and does not check whether a SNP it sets aside still has a proxy. The mechanisms in the middle of the slide, recombination above all, explain why correlation usually falls with distance, which is why checking pairs of nearby SNPs catches most of it.

The statistic: r² of allele counts Core

For each pair of SNPs in the window, PLINK computes r²: the squared correlation between the two SNPs' columns of allele counts, where each person has 0, 1 or 2 copies of the counted allele at each SNP. It uses founders only and, for each pair, only the people with a call at both SNPs. The documentation says the pruning commands "are currently based on correlations between genotype allele counts; phase is not considered".2

\[ \begin{gathered} r^2 = \frac{c_{xy}^2}{c_{xx}\,c_{yy}} \\[4pt] c_{xy} = \sum_{i} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) \end{gathered} \]
\(i\)
A person: the sums run over the founders with a genotype call at both SNPs.
\(x_i\), \(y_i\)
Person \(i\)'s allele counts at the first and the second SNP: 0, 1 or 2 copies of the counted allele (A1).
\(\bar{x}\), \(\bar{y}\)
The mean allele counts over the same people.
\(c_{xy}\)
The sum of cross-products of the deviations from the means: the covariance of the two columns times the number of people.
\(c_{xx}\), \(c_{yy}\)
The same sum for one SNP with itself, \(\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2\) and \(\sum_i (y_i - \bar{y})^2\): each column's variance times the number of people.
\(r^2\)
The squared correlation, from 0 to 1.

\(r^2\) is the share of the variance of one SNP's allele counts that a straight line in the other SNP's counts explains. It is 1 when one column is an exact linear function of the other, as for two SNPs whose alleles always occur on the same haplotypes, and near 0 when a person's genotype at one SNP says nothing about the other. Counting the other allele at one SNP replaces \(x_i\) by \(2 - x_i\), which changes the sign of \(c_{xy}\) and leaves \(r^2\) unchanged, so the choice of A1 does not matter. No haplotypes are needed: under random mating, this correlation of genotype columns estimates the haplotype \(r^2\) of the LD measures (why allele counts stand in for haplotypes). A SNP with no variation has \(c_{xx} = 0\) and no defined \(r^2\); PLINK sets such monomorphic SNPs aside before it checks any pair.

Slide 85's remark that high r² "requires both have similar MAFs" puts a ceiling on r² that depends only on the two SNPs' minor allele frequencies (Lecture 0 derives it). Solving it for the threshold shows which pairs can be pruned at all:

\[ \begin{gathered} r^2_{\max} = \frac{m_1(1 - m_2)}{(1 - m_1)\,m_2} \\[4pt] r^2_{\max} > t \iff m_1 > \frac{t\,m_2}{1 - m_2 + t\,m_2} \end{gathered} \]
\(m_1\), \(m_2\)
The two SNPs' minor allele frequencies, named so that \(m_1 \le m_2 \le 0.5\).
\(r^2_{\max}\)
The largest r² that any arrangement of the alleles on haplotypes allows with these frequencies, reached when every copy of the rarer minor allele is on a haplotype that also carries the other minor allele.
\(t\)
The r² threshold of the pruning: 0.3 in the practical.

The first line divides the odds of the rarer minor allele, \(m_1/(1 - m_1)\), by the odds of the other, \(m_2/(1 - m_2)\): it is 1 only for equal frequencies and falls as they move apart. The second line solves \(r^2_{\max} > t\) for \(m_1\). With \(t = 0.3\) and a SNP at \(m_2 = 0.3\), a partner can exceed the threshold only if \(m_1 > 0.09/0.79 \approx 0.114\). A SNP with a minor allele frequency of 0.05 can exceed 0.3 only with partners whose frequency lies between about 0.016 (from the second line) and 0.149 (from the first line, with the SNP as \(m_1\)). Pruning at 0.3 therefore never sets a rare SNP aside because of a common neighbor, or a common SNP because of a rare one: whether a rare SNP stays depends only on neighbors of similar frequency.

How --indep-pairwise decides Core

The documentation describes the rule in one sentence: "at each step, pairs of variants in the current window with squared correlation greater than the threshold are noted, and variants are greedily pruned from the window until no such pairs remain".2 The PLINK 1.9 source code fills in the details:3

  1. Chromosome by chromosome, in file order. The SNPs of each chromosome are taken in the order of the .bim file, after any filters in the same command; a window never spans two chromosomes. SNPs on chromosome 0 (position unknown) are ignored and appear in neither list.
  2. Monomorphic SNPs first. A SNP with a single genotype among the founders is set aside as soon as it enters a window.
  3. Pairs in order; the lower MAF goes. The first window holds the first 50 SNPs. PLINK checks SNP 1 against each later SNP in the window, then SNP 2 against each later SNP, and so on. At the first pair with r² above 0.3 it sets aside the SNP with the lower minor allele frequency (the later of the two when the frequencies are equal) and moves on to the next SNP. It repeats these passes until no pair of remaining SNPs in the window is above 0.3. A SNP that has been set aside takes no further part.
  4. Slide by the step. The window then moves 5 SNPs along the chromosome, counting SNPs already set aside, and gains the next 5 SNPs. Only pairs that include a new SNP can be above the threshold, because all other pairs were settled in the previous window.
  5. Report. At the end of each chromosome the log says "Pruned N variants from chromosome C, leaving M."; at the end of the run, "Pruning complete. X of Y variants removed." and the names of the two lists.

Three consequences follow. The rule is greedy and local: each decision is final and depends on the order of the SNPs and on the window, so the result is one reasonable set, not the largest possible set of uncorrelated SNPs. Within each correlated group it favors the SNPs with the higher minor allele frequency. And SNPs further apart than the window are never compared, so two kept SNPs can still have r² above 0.3 if no window contained both.

The two lists and the log

--indep-pairwise writes plain text files, one SNP ID per line, without a header, chromosome by chromosome in the order of the .bim file. The documentation calls them "valid input for --extract/--exclude in a future PLINK run".2, 3

PREFIX.prune.in
The SNPs kept: the nearly independent set that steps 4–6 use.
PREFIX.prune.out
The SNPs set aside, both those removed for LD and the monomorphic ones.
PREFIX.log
One "Pruned N variants from chromosome C, leaving M." line per chromosome, the total ("Pruning complete. X of Y variants removed."), and "Marker lists written to PREFIX.prune.in and PREFIX.prune.out".

Every SNP that enters the step, apart from any on chromosome 0, is in exactly one list, so the line counts of .prune.in and .prune.out add up to the number of lines of the input .bim file.

Choose the window, step and threshold Core

The three numbers are a choice, and published protocols differ:

  • Window. A window counted in SNPs covers more or less of the genome depending on how densely the array samples it. PennCATH has one SNP every 3.3 kb on average (SNPs per chromosome), so 50 consecutive SNPs span about 160 kb on average, and less where the array is dense. A window in kilobases (250kb 1 0.3, for example) fixes the physical length instead. The units matter: the protocol of Anderson et al. describes pruning so that "no pair of SNPs within a given window (say, 50kb) is correlated (typically taken as r2>0.2)", while its command uses a window of 50 SNPs.6
  • Step. Each window starts the step's number of SNPs after the one before, so consecutive windows share all but that many SNPs. With a step of 1, every pair of SNPs less than a window apart shares some window and is compared. With a larger step, only pairs at most the window minus the step apart are sure to be compared (45 SNPs for 50 5); pairs further apart may fall on either side of every window's edge. With a step as large as the window, consecutive windows do not overlap and pairs on either side of a boundary are never compared (the widget's orange pairs). PLINK accepts a step larger than the window, but then some SNPs never enter a window at all.3
  • Threshold. A lower threshold keeps fewer SNPs that are more nearly independent; a higher one keeps more. The r² is a squared correlation: 0.3 allows correlations up to about ±0.55 between kept neighbors, 0.1 up to about ±0.32.

Long-range LD regions

A window of 50 SNPs spans about 160 kb in PennCATH, but in some regions SNPs stay correlated over millions of base pairs. Price et al. found 24 such autosomal long-range LD regions, "each spanning >2 megabases (Mb)", by asking which SNPs drove the top principal components of 327 European Americans genotyped on the Illumina 550K array. Their table includes a region on chromosome 6 (25.5–33.5 Mb) that contains the MHC, and regions on chromosomes 8 (112–115 Mb) and 11 (46–57 Mb). One known cause is an inversion, a segment that is reversed on some copies of a chromosome: a crossover inside the segment between a reversed and a standard copy produces chromosomes with missing or duplicated pieces, which are rarely passed on, so the alleles inside each orientation stay together over millions of base pairs. Price et al. note that "inversions are known to suppress viable recombination" and that an inversion polymorphism at 8–12 Mb on chromosome 8 has been shown to cause long-range LD there.5 In such a region a pruned set still holds many SNPs that are correlated with each other at distances beyond the window, and together they can form a principal component of their own (Population structure).

The toy data contain a region of this kind, modeled on the chromosome 8 inversion (the toy dataset): on chromosome ?, from ? to ? Mb, the simulation gave its ? SNPs two groups of haplotypes, one for each orientation, with no recombination between them. Of these SNPs, ? pass step 1. The practical's settings keep ? of them, and a window of 250 kb keeps ?: in this region a window of 250 kb holds only a few SNPs at a time, so it never compares SNPs that are far apart but in strong LD. The SNPs that remain matter for step 6. In a PCA of the toy SNPs without pruning (not part of the practical), component ? sorts people by their number of inverted copies of the region, with a correlation of ? in absolute value; in the practical's PCA of the pruned SNPs, the component that follows the copy number most closely is component ?, with a correlation of only ?.

Anderson et al. therefore remove such regions before pruning, with PLINK 1.07's syntax --exclude high-LD-regions.txt --range.6 In PLINK 1.9 the same exclusion is --exclude range FILE, where each line of FILE names one region: chromosome, start and end in base pairs (the end included) and a name.7

bash 
$ plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01 \
      --exclude range high_ld_regions.txt \
      --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.3 --out ${analysis_dir}/penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.nohighld
# high_ld_regions.txt: one region per line, "chromosome start end name",
# with positions in the genome build of the .bim file

Two cautions. Positions differ between genome builds: PennCATH's are on GRCh37 (positions and genome builds), and Price et al. do not state the build of their table, so a region list must come from a source that names its build, or be converted. And --exclude, like --extract, acts before the pruning, so the regions' SNPs are in neither output list; they are simply left out of the analyses that use .prune.in. The practical does not exclude long-range LD regions; its study PCA in step 6 uses the pruned list as it is.1 The lab's emulator does not support --exclude range.

Removed, flagged and recorded

Nothing is removed in this step. The fileset from step 1 is unchanged, and the SNPs in .prune.out stay in it for the association analysis. The lists are the record: .prune.in says which SNPs steps 4–6 rest on, .prune.out which were set aside, and the log how many on each chromosome. Keep the lists with the fileset they were made from. If a later step removes people or SNPs, the allele frequencies and r² behind the lists change, so a list should be remade for the fileset it is applied to; the practical's note on step 7 makes the same point for merged data.1

In R Core

The practical does not read the lists in R. The code below checks them against the .bim file and shows where pruning set aside the most SNPs: the share of SNPs kept on each chromosome, then the stretches of 50 consecutive SNPs, the length of the practical's window, with the lowest share kept (with their positions and their length in kb), and the share along the chromosome that holds the most of the 20 lowest stretches. A stretch with a low share kept is a stretch of strong LD, and several low stretches in a row mark a long region of it.

r 
library(data.table)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)

analysis_dir <- Sys.getenv("analysis_dir")
prefix <- file.path(analysis_dir, "penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01")
bim <- fread(paste0(prefix, ".bim"), header = FALSE,
             col.names = c("CHR", "SNP", "CM", "BP", "A1", "A2"))
kept_ids <- fread(paste0(prefix, ".prune.in"), header = FALSE)$V1
bim <- bim %>% mutate(kept = SNP %in% kept_ids)
bim %>% count(kept)                    # TRUE: lines of .prune.in; FALSE: lines of .prune.out

# Share of SNPs kept on each chromosome
per_chr <- bim %>%
  group_by(CHR) %>%
  summarise(snps = n(), kept_snps = sum(kept), share = kept_snps / snps)
per_chr
ggplot(per_chr, aes(x = factor(CHR), y = share)) +
  geom_col() +
  labs(x = "Chromosome", y = "Share of SNPs kept") +
  theme_classic()

# Stretches of consecutive SNPs with the lowest share kept
stretch_snps <- 50                     # the practical's window
stretches <- bim %>%
  group_by(CHR) %>%
  mutate(stretch = (row_number() - 1) %/% stretch_snps) %>%
  group_by(CHR, stretch) %>%
  summarise(start_Mb = min(BP) / 1e6, end_Mb = max(BP) / 1e6,
            span_kb = (max(BP) - min(BP)) / 1e3,
            snps = n(), share = mean(kept), .groups = "drop") %>%
  filter(snps == stretch_snps)         # full stretches only
stretches %>% arrange(share) %>% head(10)

# The chromosome with the most of the 20 lowest stretches, and the share kept along it
low_chr <- stretches %>% arrange(share) %>% head(20) %>%
  count(CHR, sort = TRUE) %>% slice(1) %>% pull(CHR)
ggplot(filter(stretches, CHR == low_chr), aes(x = start_Mb, y = share)) +
  geom_line() +
  labs(x = paste("Position on chromosome", low_chr, "(Mb, GRCh37)"),
       y = "Share of SNPs kept") +
  theme_classic()

What to look at in the real output:

  • The log. PennCATH has SNPs on chromosomes 1–22 only, so there are 22 "Pruned …" lines, then the total. The practical does not quote the numbers; read them from your log.
  • Monomorphic SNPs. The data page counts 26,153 SNPs with a single allele in the input .bim file. Every one that passes step 1 is set aside at once and counted in .prune.out together with the SNPs removed for LD. The last two commands separate the two: --freq writes each SNP's minor allele frequency, and awk counts the SNPs whose frequency is 0. The rest of .prune.out is the SNPs removed for LD.
  • Where pruning sets aside the most. The R code above lists the stretches of 50 SNPs with the lowest share kept. Several neighboring stretches with a low share make a long region of strong LD, where SNPs kept in different windows can still be correlated with each other; check its position against a published list of long-range LD regions in GRCh37 coordinates.
  • The steps that follow. Steps 4–6 read penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.prune.in with --extract; their log lines "--extract: … variants remaining." should match the line count of the list.

How the real data differ from the toy data. The real fileset has hundreds of thousands of SNPs, so pruning takes longer than in the lab, and its LD comes from real recombination history, with blocks, hotspots and long-range regions. The practical reports that GrafAnc (step 8) assigns more than 99% of PennCATH people to its European (EUR) continental group and a small number to its Middle East and North Africa (MEN) group,1 so the pruning reflects the LD of a sample of mostly European genetic ancestry. LD extends less far in samples of African ancestry (LD across populations), so the same settings would be expected to keep a larger share of SNPs there; a pruned list from one sample is not a pruned list for another.

Pitfall Mistakes that are easy to make in this step
  • Treating pruning as a quality filter. .prune.out lists correlated SNPs, not bad ones. Running the GWAS on .prune.in would throw away most of the genome's coverage for no gain.
  • Misreading the three numbers. 50 5 0.3 is window, step and threshold, in that order. The window is a number of SNPs unless it ends in kb, and the threshold is on r², not r.
  • Expecting a pruned fileset. --indep-pairwise writes lists only. Adding --make-bed to the same command writes the unpruned data, because PLINK writes the fileset before it prunes (order of operations); apply the list with --extract.
  • Reusing a list for other data. A list made from one fileset does not fit a merged or re-filtered one; the practical makes this point for the merge with 1000 Genomes.
  • Confusing --prune with pruning. PLINK's --prune "filters out all samples with missing phenotypes"; it has nothing to do with LD.7
  • Assuming kept SNPs are uncorrelated everywhere. Only pairs that shared a window were compared. SNPs further apart, and long-range LD regions such as the MHC, can still be correlated.
  • Thinning at random instead. --thin and --thin-count keep random SNPs whatever their LD; the documentation notes that LD-based pruning "is normally more useful than these three commands".7
Key points
  • Relatedness, heterozygosity and PCA add up evidence over SNPs, so correlated SNPs give a stretch of haplotype extra weight; they need a nearly independent set of SNPs.
  • plink --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.3 computes r² of allele counts for pairs of SNPs in a window of 50 SNPs; while a pair is above 0.3 it sets aside the SNP with the lower minor allele frequency; then the window moves 5 SNPs.
  • \(r^2\) cannot exceed \(m_1(1 - m_2)/[(1 - m_1)m_2]\) for minor allele frequencies \(m_1 \le m_2\), so only SNPs of similar frequency can be pruned against each other.
  • The step writes .prune.in (kept) and .prune.out (set aside, including monomorphic SNPs); it changes no data. Steps 4–6 apply .prune.in with --extract; the association analysis uses every SNP.
  • Settings trade SNPs kept against independence; pairs further apart than the window, and long-range LD regions such as the MHC, need separate handling, for example --exclude range with regions in the data's genome build.

Check your understanding

Why do steps 4–6 use .prune.in, while a GWAS tests every SNP that passes quality control?

Steps 4–6 combine evidence over SNPs: IBD estimates from shared alleles, F from homozygous genotypes, principal components from SNPs that vary together. Correlated SNPs repeat the same evidence, so a block of them weighs more than its information warrants; the PLINK documentation calls these calculations not "LD-aware". A GWAS asks a separate question at each SNP, whether that SNP is associated with the trait. Correlation between SNPs does not bias that test, and dropping SNPs would lose the chance to find a signal at them, so it uses all SNPs that pass quality control.

A window holds SNPs A, B and C, in that order, with minor allele frequencies 0.40, 0.25 and 0.35. Their r² values are 0.45 for A–B, 0.60 for A–C and 0.20 for B–C. With a threshold of 0.3, which SNPs does PLINK keep?

Only A. In the first pass PLINK checks A against B: 0.45 > 0.3, and B has the lower minor allele frequency (0.25 < 0.40), so B goes and the pass moves on to the next SNP; B is gone, and C has no later SNP. The second pass checks A against C: 0.60 > 0.3, and C has the lower frequency (0.35 < 0.40), so C goes. No pair remains. The pair B–C, below the threshold, played no part. The result keeps the most common SNP of the group.

Can a SNP with a minor allele frequency of 0.02 be set aside because of a neighbor with a minor allele frequency of 0.40, at a threshold of 0.3? Which partners could set it aside?

No. The largest possible r² for these frequencies is \(0.02 \times 0.60/(0.98 \times 0.40) = 0.012/0.392 \approx 0.031\), far below 0.3. For partners less common than the SNP, the second line of the formula gives \(m_1 > 0.3 \times 0.02/(0.98 + 0.006) \approx 0.006\); for partners more common, the first line with the SNP as \(m_1\) requires \(0.02(1 - m_2)/(0.98\,m_2) > 0.3\), that is \(m_2 < 0.02/0.314 \approx 0.064\). Only SNPs with minor allele frequencies between about 0.006 and 0.064 can set it aside.

Run it in the lab: how many toy SNPs are in .prune.in and in .prune.out, and do they add up to the input .bim? Is any SNP in both lists?

Run the first four commands of the step 3 terminal. wc -l prints the three line counts (and their total, which you can ignore): ?, ? and ?. The first two add up to the third, because every SNP that enters the step ends in exactly one list. The sort | uniq -d | wc -l line prints 0: no ID occurs twice in the joined lists.

Run it in the lab: prune the toy data at thresholds of 0.1, 0.3 and 0.8. How does the number kept change, and why does a threshold of 0.8 still set some SNPs aside?

Run the second terminal's commands, or choose the settings in the widget. The toy data keep ?, ? and ? SNPs at 0.1, 0.3 and 0.8. The number kept rises with the threshold, because fewer pairs exceed a higher r². At 0.8 a SNP is set aside only if it shares a window with a SNP of higher minor allele frequency whose allele counts predict its own almost perfectly (r² above 0.8), or if it is monomorphic, which sets it aside at every threshold.

Two SNPs in .prune.in have r² = 0.6 with each other. How can that be?

They were never in the same window: they are 50 or more SNPs apart, or more than 45 apart and placed so that no window of 50 SNPs, moved 5 at a time, contained both. Pruning compares only pairs within a window, so correlation over longer distances, as in long-range LD regions such as the MHC, survives. Excluding those regions with --exclude range, or a larger window, reduces such pairs.

You add --make-bed --out pruned to the practical's pruning command, expecting a pruned fileset. What do you get, and what should you run instead?

A copy of the unpruned data: PLINK writes the fileset before it prunes, and --indep-pairwise only writes the two lists. To get a pruned fileset, run a second command with --extract …prune.in --make-bed; the practical skips the fileset and puts --extract directly into the commands of steps 4–6.

A collaborator sends a file of long-range LD regions for --exclude range, with positions taken from a 2008 paper. What do you check before using it on PennCATH?

The genome build. PennCATH's positions are on GRCh37, and the same region has different coordinates in other builds, so a list in another build would exclude the wrong SNPs. Price et al. do not name the build of their table, so use a list whose build is stated, or convert the positions. Also check the format, one region per line with chromosome, start, end (included) and a name, and the log line of --exclude, which reports how many SNPs remain.
