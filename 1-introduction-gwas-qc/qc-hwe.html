QC step 2: Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

If each person's two alleles at a SNP were independent draws from the population's allele pool, the three genotype counts would follow from the allele frequency alone. This step tests that prediction for every SNP with an exact test, reads the result in the controls, and asks which departures are genotyping errors and which are biology.

Slide 83 plink --hardy · --hwe Writes: .hwe with rows ALL, AFF and UNAFF ≈ 50 min
Mental model

One exact-test p-value per SNP and group; quality control reads the controls

For every SNP, PLINK compares the genotype counts with the counts Hardy–Weinberg proportions predict and turns the difference into an exact-test p-value, once for everyone, once for the cases and once for the controls. A genotyping error makes every row depart; a true association can make the cases depart. Quality control therefore reads the controls and removes only SNPs with extremely small p-values.

  • What breaks Hardy–Weinberg proportions. Calling errors, deletions and duplicated sequence change the genotype counts; so do mixing populations, the disease itself in cases, and chance.
  • One exact p-value per SNP and group. --hardy counts genotypes in founders and computes the exact test for ALL, AFF (cases) and UNAFF (controls).
  • Read the distribution of p-values. When Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium holds the p-values are spread almost evenly between 0 and 1; errors add a tail of tiny values.
  • Test in controls, filter stringently. Hundreds of thousands of tests produce small p-values by chance, and a stringent threshold removes the gross errors without removing real associations.
  • The same code for toy and real data. The lab, the R analysis and the PennCATH commands differ only in the file prefix.

What the step finds Core

A SNP with two alleles, allele 1 and allele 2, has three genotypes: 11, 12 and 22. If allele 1 has frequency \(p\) and allele 2 frequency \(q = 1 - p\), and each person's two alleles are independent draws from the population's allele pool, the genotype frequencies are \(p^2\), \(2pq\) and \(q^2\). These are Hardy–Weinberg proportions, and a population whose genotypes follow them is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. The assumptions behind independent draws (random mating, a large population, no selection or migration acting on the SNP) never hold exactly, but for most SNPs in a group of people with similar ancestry they hold closely enough that the observed counts sit near the predicted ones.9 A test of HWE compares the observed genotype counts with the predicted ones; a small p-value means the observed counts would be unlikely if the proportions held.

Slides Slide 83 · What Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium is used for

The slide lists four uses of HWE and outlines the first two in red. This step is the first: testing for genotyping errors. The second, simplifying calculations, is what lets the widget below and every simulation draw genotypes from an allele frequency alone. The third, testing the assumptions, is the reason a departure is not automatically an error: population mixture, selection or allele frequencies that differ between males and females move correctly called genotypes away from HWE too. The fourth, a case-only association test, works because a risk allele that acts dominantly or recessively changes the genotype mix of cases; the widget shows why its power is low. That fourth use is also why QC must not test in cases.

With PennCATH's numbers of cases and controls, the cases (AFF) have too few heterozygotes and a p-value of about 3 × 10⁻⁶, all people (ALL) about 6 × 10⁻⁴ because they include the cases, and the controls (UNAFF) about 0.7. Choose the multiplicative model: the cases return to HWE, yet ALL still departs, because pooling cases and controls with different allele frequencies is itself a mixture of groups. Now set the relative risk to 1 and miscall 20% of heterozygotes: every row departs, and the heterozygote shortfall in the note equals the miscall rate. Even so, the controls' p-value is only about 3 × 10⁻⁵ with 470 controls; at 10% it is about 0.04. In a study of this size, the HWE test finds gross errors only. Finally set the relative risk to 1.3, a typical GWAS effect: the case-only departure disappears into noise, which is why slide 83 calls that use of HWE low-powered. Tick "mid-p" to see PLINK's midp version of each p-value, which is always a little smaller.

The convention rests on a general argument. At a SNP that affects the trait, a group selected for the trait, the cases, can depart from Hardy–Weinberg proportions; a group not selected for it should not, so a departure there points to the genotyping or to the mix of people rather than to the trait. In PennCATH the word “controls” needs care. The practical's controls are PLINK's UNAFF group, the 470 people coded 1 in column 6 of the .fam file. The clinical table that comes with Patrick Breheny's teaching copy of PennCATH records coronary artery disease for these 470 people as well as for the 463 coded 2, and what separates the two codes is not documented (what column 6 holds). This page keeps the practical's word as the name of the UNAFF group, not as a description of who these people are.

The statistic: the exact test Core

For each SNP and each group (ALL, AFF, UNAFF), PLINK counts the genotypes among the founders. In PLINK's terms a founder is a person whose father and mother IDs, columns 3 and 4 of the .fam file, are both 0 (unknown); a person with a parent ID filled in is a nonfounder even if that parent is not in the fileset.2 Counting founders only keeps a family's shared alleles from being counted twice. In PennCATH all 1,401 people are founders.10 From the three counts come the allele frequency and the heterozygote frequencies the report prints:

\[ \begin{gathered} \hat{p} = \frac{2n_{11} + n_{12}}{2n} \\[4pt] \hat{H}_{\mathrm{obs}} = \frac{n_{12}}{n} \qquad \hat{H}_{\mathrm{exp}} = 2\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p}) \end{gathered} \]
\(n_{11}\), \(n_{12}\), \(n_{22}\)
Numbers of founders in the group with genotypes 11, 12 and 22 at this SNP, among those with a call. PLINK prints them as GENO, in that order (allele 1 is PLINK's A1, usually the minor allele).
\(n\)
\(n_{11} + n_{12} + n_{22}\), the number of people with a call in the group.
\(\hat{p}\)
The estimated frequency of allele 1: its copies (two per 11 homozygote, one per heterozygote) divided by the \(2n\) allele copies; between 0 and 1.
\(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{obs}}\)
The observed share of heterozygotes in the group (O(HET) in the report).
\(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{exp}}\)
The share of heterozygotes that Hardy–Weinberg proportions predict from the group's own allele frequency (E(HET) in the report); at most 0.5, reached when \(\hat{p} = 0.5\).

Both heterozygote frequencies come from the same three counts, so they can be compared directly. Take controls with 62, 158 and 250 people of genotypes 11, 12 and 22 (\(n = 470\)): \(\hat{p} = (124 + 158)/940 = 0.30\), \(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{exp}} = 2 \times 0.30 \times 0.70 = 0.42\), which predicts about 197 heterozygotes, but \(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{obs}} = 158/470 = 0.336\). These counts are what a SNP at frequency 0.30 looks like when 20% of its heterozygotes are called homozygous, half each way: the allele frequency is unchanged, and only the heterozygotes are short.

Is a shortfall from 197 to 158 heterozygotes more than chance? The exact test answers this without the large-sample approximation of the chi-square test, which gives misleading p-values when one allele is rare.5 It fixes the number of people and the number of allele 1 copies at their observed values and asks how likely each possible number of heterozygotes would be if the \(2n\) allele copies were paired into people at random:

\[ \begin{gathered} \Pr(n_{12} \mid n, n_1) = \\ \frac{2^{n_{12}}\; n!\; n_1!\; n_2!}{n_{11}!\; n_{12}!\; n_{22}!\; (2n)!} \end{gathered} \]
\(n_1\), \(n_2\)
The numbers of allele 1 and allele 2 copies in the group: \(n_1 = 2n_{11} + n_{12}\), \(n_2 = 2n_{22} + n_{12}\), with \(n_1 + n_2 = 2n\).
\(\Pr(n_{12} \mid n, n_1)\)
The probability, under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, of seeing exactly \(n_{12}\) heterozygotes among \(n\) people who carry \(n_1\) copies of allele 1 in total. Once \(n_{12}\) is fixed, \(n_{11} = (n_1 - n_{12})/2\) and \(n_{22} = (n_2 - n_{12})/2\) follow.
\(k!\)
The factorial \(1 \times 2 \times \dots \times k\), the number of orders of \(k\) distinct items; \(0! = 1\).
\(2^{n_{12}}\)
Each heterozygote can receive its two different alleles in two orders, and each order is a different pairing of copies.

The formula counts the ways of pairing the \(2n\) allele copies into \(n\) people that give \(n_{12}\) heterozygotes, as a share of all pairings. \(n!/(n_{11}!\,n_{12}!\,n_{22}!)\) counts the ways to give the \(n\) people their genotypes, \(2^{n_{12}}\) counts the orders within heterozygotes, and \(n_1!\,n_2!/(2n)!\) turns the count into a probability. Moving from \(n_{12}\) to \(n_{12} - 2\) heterozygotes (one 11 and one 22 person more) multiplies the probability by \(n_{12}(n_{12} - 1) / (4(n_{11} + 1)(n_{22} + 1))\); PLINK computes the whole distribution with this ratio, starting from the most likely count.5 For the controls above (\(n = 470\), \(n_1 = 282\)) the most likely count is 198 heterozygotes, with probability 0.088; the observed 158 has probability 9.1 × 10⁻⁶.

The p-value adds up the probabilities of every heterozygote count that is as unlikely as the observed one or more so, on both sides of the most likely count:

\[ \sum_{k \,:\, \Pr(k) \le \Pr(n_{12})} \Pr(k \mid n, n_1) \]
\(k\)
A possible number of heterozygotes for the same \(n\) and \(n_1\): it has the same parity (odd or even) as \(n_1\) and runs from 0 or 1 up to the smaller of \(n_1\) and \(n_2\).
\(\Pr(k \mid n, n_1)\)
The probability of \(k\) heterozygotes from the previous formula.
\(\Pr(k) \le \Pr(n_{12})\)
The condition that selects the counts at least as unlikely as the observed \(n_{12}\).

This sum is the exact test's p-value (the P column of the report). It is two-sided: too few and too many heterozygotes both count as departures. In the example, counts of 158 or fewer contribute 1.48 × 10⁻⁵ and counts of 236 or more, which are as unlikely on the other side, contribute 1.07 × 10⁻⁵, so the p-value is 2.55 × 10⁻⁵. With --hardy midp or --hwe … midp, PLINK counts the observed probability (and any exactly equal one) only half, the mid-p value (2.09 × 10⁻⁵ here). The plain exact test rejects HWE slightly less often than its nominal rate when HWE holds; the mid-p version brings the rate close to nominal, and the PLINK documentation recommends it for filtering.2,6

The report does not print the size of the departure, but O(HET) and E(HET) give it. The relative shortfall of heterozygotes is the per-SNP version of the inbreeding coefficient \(F\) that step 5 computes per person:

\[ \hat{F} = 1 - \frac{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{obs}}}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{exp}}} \]
\(\hat{F}\)
The share of the expected heterozygotes that are missing at this SNP, in this group: 0 when the counts match HWE, positive when heterozygotes are too few (1 when there are none), negative when they are too many.
\(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{obs}}\), \(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{exp}}\)
O(HET) and E(HET) from the report, as defined above.

For the example, \(\hat{F} = 1 - 0.336/0.42 = 0.20\): exactly the 20% of heterozygotes that were miscalled, because symmetric miscalls lower \(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{obs}}\) by that share and leave \(\hat{p}\), and so \(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{exp}}\), unchanged. The p-value measures evidence, not size: the same \(\hat{F} = 0.20\) gives 2.6 × 10⁻⁵ with 470 controls and about 10⁻⁹ with all 933 people of the widget's study. A SNP with a large \(|\hat{F}|\) but an unremarkable p-value is often a rare SNP, where even a large relative shortfall is a few people.

The toy data contain SNPs whose genotypes the simulation miscalled on purpose (the toy dataset), and their UNAFF rows show \(\hat{F}\) near its extremes. At the ? SNPs with a heterozygote deficit (?), most heterozygotes were called as the common homozygote, so O(HET) is close to 0 and \(\hat{F}\) close to 1. At the ? SNPs with an excess (?), most common homozygotes were called heterozygous, so O(HET) is far above E(HET) and \(\hat{F}\) is strongly negative. Look at the distribution Core

If every SNP were in HWE, the p-values of a continuous test would be spread evenly between 0 and 1: 10% of SNPs below 0.1, 1% below 0.01, one in a million below 10⁻⁶. The exact test is discrete, so its p-values come in steps, and whenever the observed heterozygote count is the most likely count for that SNP, the p-value is exactly 1. The fewer counts are plausible, the more often that happens: for a SNP whose rare allele is carried by only a few people, and for any SNP in a small group. The practical's histogram of the controls' p-values therefore shows a nearly flat floor, a tall bar at 1, and, if there are errors, a pile-up near 0. A histogram cannot show how extreme the smallest values are; for that, plot \(-\log_{10}\) of each p-value against the value expected for its rank under HWE, a quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plot (Lecture 0 · QQ plots). Points on the diagonal match chance; points that rise above it at the right end are the SNPs that chance does not explain.

How many small p-values chance alone produces

\[ \E[N_t] \le M\,t \]
\(t\)
A p-value threshold, such as 10⁻⁶.
\(M\)
The number of SNPs tested.
\(N_t\)
The number of SNPs whose p-value falls below \(t\) when every SNP is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.
\(\E[N_t]\)
The expected (average) value of \(N_t\) over repeated samples.

When HWE holds, each SNP's p-value falls below \(t\) with probability \(t\) for a continuous test and at most \(t\) for the exact test, so on average at most \(Mt\) SNPs cross the threshold by chance. PennCATH has at most 861,473 SNPs: about 861 cross 10⁻³, 0.86 cross 10⁻⁶ and 0.009 cross 10⁻⁸. A threshold of 10⁻³ would therefore remove several hundred well-genotyped SNPs, while at 10⁻⁶ or below almost every SNP removed has a real departure, error or biology.

Choose a threshold and act Core

The practical counts the controls' SNPs below 10⁻⁸, applies no filter, and leaves the choice to the exercise: "a higher cutoff (e.g., 1e-3) is more aggressive and removes more markers, while a lower cutoff (e.g., 1e-6) is more permissive and retains more".1 Published protocols filter in controls with thresholds from 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻¹²:

Hardy–Weinberg thresholds in the practical and in published protocols
SourceGroup testedRemove SNPs with an exact-test p-value below

Three arguments narrow the choice. Chance: at \(t\), about \(Mt\) good SNPs go (above). Sample size: the p-value of a given departure shrinks as the number of people grows, so a fixed threshold is stricter in a large study; UK Biobank's 10⁻¹² across hundreds of thousands of people is no more lenient than 10⁻⁶ in a few thousand. Biology: real associations depart slightly, so the threshold should be far below the p-values that a true effect can produce in controls. The PLINK documentation recommends "a low threshold", because serious errors give extreme p-values that any reasonable threshold catches.2

The --hwe filter

plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 --hwe 1e-6 midp --make-bed --out ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.hwe1e-6
  1. plink — as before.
  2. --bfile … — the fileset after step 1.
  3. --hwe 1e-6 midp — remove every SNP whose exact-test p-value is below 10⁻⁶ (here the mid-p value). For case/control data PLINK tests the controls only: "cases and missing phenotypes are normally ignored"; the modifier include-nonctrl tests all founders instead.2 The number is required; there is no default threshold.
  4. --make-bed — write the remaining SNPs as a new fileset; without it the filter changes nothing on disk.
  5. --out …hwe1e-6 — a name that records the threshold.

The log then says "--hwe: N variants removed due to Hardy-Weinberg exact test." Two more lines can appear. "Warning: --hwe observation counts vary by more than 10%" means that the SNPs were tested on very different numbers of people, usually because of missing calls; a SNP with many missing calls gets a less extreme p-value for the same departure, so one threshold treats SNPs unequally. After step 1's --geno 0.01 every SNP is called in at least 99% of people, so the warning should not appear. On the X chromosome only females are tested, which triggers a second version of the warning in data with many males.2 In PLINK's fixed order of operations --hwe runs after --mind and --geno and before --maf, so a single command with all of them tests HWE on the data the missingness filters leave (order of operations).

On the toy data the default removes ? SNPs, the planted errors. include-nonctrl removes the same SNPs and also the causal SNP ?: testing everyone would delete the toy data's real association before the association analysis could find it.

Several ancestry groups in one study

The practical's discussion question points to a second reason for a departure: "in datasets with multiple ancestries, apparent HWE violations can also reflect population stratification rather than genotyping error".1 Population stratification here means that the study combines groups of people whose allele frequencies differ because of their genetic ancestry. Each group can be in HWE while the pooled controls are not (the Wahlund effect), and the departure is largest at SNPs whose frequencies differ most between groups, which are exactly the SNPs that later separate the groups in a PCA (step 6). Filtering the pooled data would remove informative, correctly called SNPs. Options, in the order pipelines usually take them: run the test separately within groups of similar genetic ancestry (--keep a list of the group's IDs, from step 7 or step 8) and remove a SNP only if it fails in a group, or in several; or use a more lenient threshold on the pooled data. Ancestry groups defined from genotypes are relative to a reference panel and are not race or ethnicity (Lecture 0 · population descriptors).

Removed, flagged and recorded

--hwe removes SNPs; it writes no list of them. As in step 1, the list is the difference between the input and output .bim files, or the SNPs below the threshold in the .hwe report: awk '$3 == "UNAFF" && $9 < 1e-6 { print $2 }' FILE.hwe > hwe_fail.txt (without midp; with it, run --hardy midp first). Many analysts keep such SNPs in a list and check them after the association analysis instead: a SNP removed for an HWE departure in controls cannot become a GWAS hit, and one that is a hit and departs in cases only is a reason to look closer, not to delete. The practical applies no HWE filter before step 3, so steps 3–8 use all SNPs that passed step 1.1

What to look at in the real output:

  • Who is in each row. PennCATH started with 463 cases, 470 controls and 468 people with a missing phenotype (codes 2, 1 and −9; see what the codes mean in this copy).10 Step 1 removed 21 people, so the ALL rows count up to 1,380 founders, the AFF rows at most 463 and the UNAFF rows at most 470. The QC test therefore rests on at most 470 controls: as the widget shows, a sample of that size detects gross errors but not subtle ones.
  • The counts. Real PLINK 1.9.0's --hardy reports 1,380 founders and 0 nonfounders: 455 cases, 463 controls and 462 people with a missing phenotype (step 1's numbers). Of the 569,723 SNPs, 162 have a p-value below 10⁻⁸ in the UNAFF rows, 157 in the AFF rows and 407 in the ALL rows; below 10⁻⁶ the counts are 212, 205 and 602. On average, chance alone puts fewer than one SNP per group below 10⁻⁶ (how many small p-values chance produces), so almost all of these SNPs have a real departure, error or biology. The ALL rows count every founder, about three times as many people as either group, so the same departure reaches a smaller p-value there.
  • The extreme SNPs. Sort the controls by P. For the most extreme ones, is it a shortfall or an excess of heterozygotes (O(HET) against E(HET))? Do the AFF and ALL rows depart as well, as an error would make them?
  • The histogram and the Q–Q plot. A flat floor, a bar at 1 and a few extreme values are expected. The bar at 1 includes the SNPs that are monomorphic, carrying one allele only: the data page counts 26,153 in the input .bim file, where PennCATH writes −9 as the missing allele's name. With one allele there is only one possible set of genotype counts, so the exact test returns 1 (0.5 with midp). A Q–Q plot that leaves the diagonal early, across many SNPs, would point to structure in the controls rather than to a few bad SNPs.
  • The --hwe log line. How many SNPs a 10⁻⁶ filter removes, compared with the \(Mt \approx 0.86\) expected by chance. With midp it removes at least the SNPs whose UNAFF p-value in the report is below 10⁻⁶, because a mid-p value is smaller than the report's p-value for the same SNP. Real PLINK 1.9.0 removes 218 SNPs.

How the real data differ from the toy data. The real file has three rows for each SNP that passed step 1, so it is several times longer than the .lmiss file; sort and count it with the shell or R, not a spreadsheet. The practical reports that GrafAnc (step 8) assigns more than 99% of PennCATH people to its European (EUR) continental group and a small number to its Middle East and North Africa (MEN) group,1 so departures caused by pooling very different ancestry groups should be rarer than in a multi-ancestry study. The toy study is similar: ? of its ? people were simulated as European-like (the toy dataset). The toy data's failing SNPs were planted with known causes; in the real data the cause of a departure has to be inferred from its direction and from which rows show it, and you will not know for certain.

Pitfall Mistakes that are easy to make in this step
  • Reading the wrong row. The first row of each SNP is ALL. Filter on TEST == "UNAFF" for QC; a filter on ALL or AFF can delete true associations.
  • Getting the direction of the threshold backwards. --hwe 1e-3 removes more SNPs than --hwe 1e-6: a larger threshold catches more p-values.
  • Counting lines instead of SNPs. wc -l on the .hwe file counts each SNP three times, plus the header.
  • Testing a mixture. Controls of several ancestries depart from HWE at SNPs whose allele frequencies differ between the groups. Test within groups of similar ancestry, or choose a lenient threshold.
  • Reading P = 1 as proof of quality. A rare SNP often has P = 1 because few heterozygote counts are possible; the test has little power there. A monomorphic SNP (one allele only) also gets 1.
  • Expecting --hardy to filter. In PLINK 1.9 it only reports; PLINK 1.07 removed SNPs below 0.001 automatically. Use --hwe with --make-bed to remove.
  • Testing before the missingness filters. SNPs with many missing calls are tested on fewer people and get less extreme p-values; run step 1 first, as the practical does.
Key points
  • Under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium the genotype counts follow from the allele frequency: \(p^2\), \(2pq\), \(q^2\). Calling errors, deletions, duplicated sequence, population mixture, the disease and chance move them away.
  • plink --hardy writes, for every SNP, the genotype counts (GENO), O(HET), E(HET) and the exact test's p-value, in three rows for case/control data: ALL (all founders), AFF (cases), UNAFF (controls).
  • The exact test fixes the number of people and allele copies and sums the probabilities of all heterozygote counts as unlikely as the observed one; the mid-p version counts the observed probability half.
  • QC reads UNAFF, because a true association can make cases (and ALL) depart. --hwe tests controls only by default (include-nonctrl changes that).
  • With \(M\) SNPs, about \(Mt\) cross a threshold \(t\) by chance: thresholds of 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻¹⁰ in controls keep the chance losses below one SNP or a few, and still catch gross errors.
  • The practical counts SNPs below 10⁻⁸ in controls and leaves the filter to the exercise; with several ancestry groups, test within groups.

Check your understanding

A SNP's controls have GENO 10/120/340. Compute \(\hat{p}\), O(HET), E(HET) and \(\hat{F}\). Too few or too many heterozygotes?

\(n = 470\) and \(n_1 = 2 \times 10 + 120 = 140\) copies of allele 1, so \(\hat{p} = 140/940 = 0.149\). O(HET) \(= 120/470 = 0.255\); E(HET) \(= 2 \times 0.149 \times 0.851 = 0.254\); \(\hat{F} = 1 - 0.255/0.254 \approx -0.006\). The counts match Hardy–Weinberg proportions almost exactly (the expected numbers are about 10.4, 119.2 and 340.4), so the exact test's p-value is close to 1.

Why does PLINK's --hwe test only the controls in a case–control study, and when would you use include-nonctrl?

Cases are selected for the disease, so if a SNP affects risk, the cases' genotype mix shifts. Under a recessive or dominant model the cases then depart from HWE, and ALL, which contains the cases, departs too; a filter on those rows could remove the very SNPs the study is looking for. Controls of a rare disease are close to a random sample of the population, so a departure there points to an error or to population structure. include-nonctrl tests all founders, which suits data without a case/control phenotype (where there are no controls to test) or a deliberate choice to test everyone, accepting the risk.

With 861,473 SNPs all in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, about how many would a filter at 10⁻³ remove, and at 10⁻⁸?

At most \(Mt\): \(861{,}473 \times 10^{-3} \approx 861\) SNPs at 10⁻³ and \(861{,}473 \times 10^{-8} \approx 0.009\) at 10⁻⁸ (so almost certainly none). The exact test is slightly conservative, so the real numbers are a little lower. This is the cost side of the practical's remark that 10⁻³ is "more aggressive": hundreds of well-genotyped SNPs would go.

Run it in the lab: how many toy SNPs have an HWE p-value below 10⁻⁸ in the controls, and how many in the cases? Explain the difference.

Run awk '$3 == "UNAFF" && $9 < 1e-8' ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.hwe | wc -l and the same with "AFF": the controls have ? such SNPs and the cases ?. The SNPs that appear in both lists are candidates for genotyping errors, which affect everyone; in the toy data they are the planted errors. A SNP that appears only in AFF has a departure created by the disease: here it is ?, the SNP the simulation made causal. Print its three rows with grep -w and its ID to check that only AFF and ALL depart.

Run it in the lab: among the controls' 10 most extreme SNPs, which have too few heterozygotes and which too many? What could cause each?

Run grep UNAFF ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.hwe | sort -g -k9 | head and compare column 7, O(HET), with column 8, E(HET). Too few heterozygotes (O(HET) < E(HET)) fits heterozygotes miscalled as homozygotes, a variant under the probe, a deletion, or a mixture of ancestry groups. Too many (O(HET) > E(HET)) fits a probe that reads two copies of a duplicated sequence. In the toy data the deficits ? were planted as heterozygotes miscalled as the common homozygote, and the excesses ? as a probe that also binds a second place in the genome, one that always carries the minor allele; the other SNPs among the 10 have p-values that chance explains.

A SNP has 20% of its heterozygotes miscalled in every person. With 470 controls its p-value is about 3 × 10⁻⁵. Would --hwe 1e-6 remove it? What does this say about the test?

No: 3 × 10⁻⁵ is above 10⁻⁶, so the SNP stays. The departure is large (\(\hat{F} = 0.20\)), but 470 people do not give strong evidence for it. The HWE filter in a study of this size removes only gross errors (the PLINK documentation's example is a p-value of 10⁻⁵⁰); moderate error rates pass. Other checks, such as missingness patterns, cluster plots and comparisons with external allele frequencies, are needed to find them.

Why can ALL depart from Hardy–Weinberg proportions even when cases and controls are each in equilibrium?

If allele 1's frequency differs between cases and controls (as it does at an associated SNP, even under a multiplicative model that keeps both groups in HWE), the ALL row pools two groups with different frequencies. A pooled sample of groups with different allele frequencies has fewer heterozygotes than its pooled frequency predicts: the Wahlund effect. The widget's multiplicative model shows it: AFF and UNAFF have p-values near 1, while ALL in the larger study has about 2 × 10⁻⁶.
