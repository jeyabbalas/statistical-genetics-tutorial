Estimating frequencies and testing Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

At one SNP, everything a study measured comes down to three numbers: how many people carry each of the three genotypes. This page turns those counts into an allele frequency together with a statement of how precise it is, predicts the genotype counts that random mating would produce, and tests whether the observed counts fit that prediction. Quality control of genome-wide data runs exactly this test at every SNP.

Mental model

One table of genotype counts gives an estimate, a prediction and a test

At one SNP the data are the numbers of people with each genotype. Counting allele copies turns them into an allele frequency, and the binomial distribution of those copies says how precise it is. Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium turns the allele frequency back into expected genotype counts, and a test measures how far the observed counts lie from them. Quality control repeats the test at every SNP and removes SNPs whose counts are too far off to be chance.

  • Genotype counts. \(n_{ij}\) people have genotype \(ij\), and \(P_{ij} = n_{ij}/n\). \(K\) alleles give \(K(K+1)/2\) genotypes: 3 at a SNP, 6 for the ABO blood group (slides 71–74).
  • Allele counts. \(n\) people carry \(2n\) allele copies, and \(\hat{p}_i\) is the share of them that are allele \(i\). Always name the allele that \(p\) refers to (slides 75–79).
  • Precision. \(\hat{p}\) is the maximum-likelihood estimate. Its standard error is \(\sqrt{\hat{p}\hat{q}/2n}\) when genotypes are in Hardy–Weinberg proportions, larger when heterozygotes are scarce, and a 95% confidence interval is about \(\hat{p} \pm 2\) standard errors.
  • The prediction. Under random mating the genotype frequencies are \(p^2\), \(2pq\) and \(q^2\) (\(p_i^2\) and \(2p_ip_j\) with more alleles), reached in one generation (slides 80–82).
  • The test. Pearson’s \(X^2\) with 1 degree of freedom, or the exact test that PLINK reports, compares observed with expected counts. The p-value is the probability, if the SNP were in equilibrium, of counts at least this far off.
  • Quality control. A departure can mean a genotyping error, mixed ancestry, inbreeding or an association with the disease. QC therefore tests the controls, and uses a very small threshold because it runs hundreds of thousands of tests (slide 83).

Genotypes and their frequencies Core

Slide 71 restates three definitions from Lecture 0. An allele is one version of the DNA sequence at a polymorphic locus, a place in the genome where people differ. A person carries two alleles at a locus on an autosome, one from each parent, but the population as a whole can carry many. The pair a person carries is their genotype.

The slides use two loci as running examples. MTHFR C677T is a SNP with two alleles, C and T. The ABO blood group has three common alleles, A, B and O. Alleles A and B each code for an enzyme that attaches a different sugar to a molecule on the surface of red blood cells, making the A antigen or the B antigen; an antigen is a molecular structure that the immune system can recognize. The common O allele carries a one-base deletion that disrupts the enzyme, so it adds neither sugar1. A person with both A and B shows both antigens (A and B are codominant), and one copy of A or B is enough to show that antigen (both are dominant over O). Six genotypes therefore produce four blood groups, as Table 1 shows.

Slide 72 sets up the notation that the rest of the lecture uses. Number the alleles \(1, \ldots, K\). Then \(n_{ij}\) is the number of people with genotype \(ij\), one copy of allele \(i\) and one of allele \(j\); \(n = \sum n_{ij}\) is the number of people; and the genotype frequency is \(P_{ij} = n_{ij}/n\). The order of \(i\) and \(j\) carries no meaning: OA and AO are the same people, counted once. \(K\) alleles give \(K\) homozygous genotypes and \(K(K-1)/2\) heterozygous ones, \(K(K+1)/2\) in all: 3 for a SNP, 6 for ABO.

Wolpin and colleagues determined each participant’s ABO genotype from DNA. Their 3,117 participants were 1,534 people with pancreatic cancer and 1,583 controls from 12 cohort studies, and they found that each non-O allele raised the risk of the cancer2. A blood test that detects antigens would have seen only the four blood groups: O 1,168, A 1,342 (262 AA plus 1,080 OA), B 421 and AB 186. Gene counting, below, recovers allele frequencies from such data.

A genotype frequency in a sample estimates the frequency in the population the sample came from. Each person either has genotype \(ij\) or does not, so \(n_{ij}\) is the number of “yes” answers among \(n\) independent people, a count that follows a binomial distribution. Its uncertainty is measured by the standard error: the standard deviation of the estimate over repeated samples of the same size from the same population.

\[ \SE(\hat{P}_{ij}) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{P}_{ij}(1 - \hat{P}_{ij})}{n}} \]
\(\hat{P}_{ij}\)
Estimated frequency of genotype \(ij\): \(n_{ij}/n\), between 0 and 1.
\(n\)
Number of people genotyped.
\(\SE(\hat{P}_{ij})\)
Standard error of the estimate: how much \(\hat{P}_{ij}\) would vary from one sample of \(n\) people to the next.

A count of “yes” answers among \(n\) independent people with probability \(P\) has variance \(nP(1 - P)\); dividing the count by \(n\) divides the variance by \(n^2\), and the square root of \(P(1-P)/n\) is the standard error. It is largest when \(P = 0.5\) and shrinks with \(\sqrt{n}\): four times as many people halve it. Worked number: \(\hat{P}_{\mathrm{CT}} = 350/766 = 0.457\), with standard error \(\sqrt{0.457 \times 0.543/766} = 0.018\). The denominator is \(n\), the number of people, because each person contributes one genotype (slide 79).

From genotype counts to allele frequencies Core

An allele frequency counts allele copies instead of people (slide 75). Each person carries two copies of an autosomal locus, so \(n\) people carry \(2n\) copies. A person homozygous for allele \(i\) carries two copies of it, and a heterozygote \(ij\) carries one.

\[ \begin{aligned} m_i &= 2n_{ii} + \sum_{j \ne i} n_{ij} \\ \hat{p}_i &= \frac{m_i}{2n} \end{aligned} \]
\(m_i\)
Allele count: the number of copies of allele \(i\) among the \(n\) people, 0 to \(2n\). Slide 75 writes \(m_i\); the total is \(m = 2n\).
\(n_{ii}\)
Number of people homozygous for allele \(i\); each carries two copies.
\(\sum_{j \ne i} n_{ij}\)
Number of people heterozygous with one copy of allele \(i\), summed over the other alleles \(j\).
\(\hat{p}_i\)
Estimated allele frequency of allele \(i\): the share of all copies that are allele \(i\). The \(K\) frequencies add up to 1.

Every copy of allele \(i\) is counted exactly once: twice for each \(ii\) person, once for each heterozygote that carries it. Dividing numerator and denominator by \(n\) gives the same estimate from genotype frequencies, \(\hat{p}_i = \hat{P}_{ii} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j \ne i} \hat{P}_{ij}\), the form on slide 75. If everyone were homozygous \(ii\), \(\hat{p}_i = 1\); if nobody carried allele \(i\), \(\hat{p}_i = 0\). Worked numbers below.

How precise is an allele frequency? Core

The 766 women are a sample from a much larger population, and another 766 women would have carried a slightly different number of C alleles. The slides report \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{C}} = 0.664\) and move on; this section asks how far that number could be from the frequency in the population. It uses three standard tools: the likelihood, which shows why counting allele copies is the right estimate; the standard error, which measures how much the estimate varies from sample to sample; and the confidence interval, which turns that variation into a range.

The estimate that makes the data most probable

Assume for now that genotypes are in Hardy–Weinberg proportions. Then each of the \(2n\) allele copies in the sample is C with probability \(p\), independently of every other copy (the next section shows why random mating gives this independence, and the end of this section what changes without it). The number of C copies, \(m\), then follows a binomial distribution: the distribution of the number of successes in \(N\) independent trials that each succeed with the same probability. Here the trials are the \(N = 2n\) copies and a success is a copy of C. The likelihood reads this distribution the other way round: it holds the observed count fixed and asks how probable that count is under each candidate value of \(p\).

\[ L(p) = \binom{2n}{m}\, p^{m} (1 - p)^{2n - m} \]
\(L(p)\)
Likelihood of \(p\): the probability of observing exactly \(m\) copies of C among the \(2n\) copies if the population frequency of C were \(p\). A function of the candidate \(p\), for fixed data.
\(p\)
A candidate value for the frequency of allele C in the population, between 0 and 1.
\(m\)
Number of copies of C in the sample, \(2n_{11} + n_{12}\): 1,018 for MTHFR.
\(2n\)
Number of allele copies, twice the number of people: 1,532.
\(\binom{2n}{m}\)
Number of ways to choose which \(m\) of the \(2n\) copies are C. It does not depend on \(p\).

Each copy of C contributes a factor \(p\) and each copy of T a factor \(1 - p\); independence lets the factors multiply. The data favor candidates near the observed share of C: for MTHFR, \(L(0.664)\) is about 780,000 times \(L(0.6)\) and about 90 times \(L(0.7)\). The binomial coefficient is the same for every candidate, so it cancels from any comparison of two candidates and is usually dropped.

The maximum-likelihood estimate is the candidate with the largest likelihood. A product of 1,532 factors is awkward to maximize, so take the natural logarithm, which turns the product into a sum and peaks at the same place. The peak is where the slope is zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(p) &= m \ln p + (2n - m) \ln(1 - p) \\ \ell'(p) &= \frac{m}{p} - \frac{2n - m}{1 - p} \end{aligned} \]
\(\ell(p)\)
Log-likelihood: \(\ln L(p)\) without the constant \(\ln \binom{2n}{m}\). Always negative; highest where \(L(p)\) is highest.
\(\ln\)
Natural logarithm.
\(2n - m\)
Number of copies of T: 514.
\(\ell'(p)\)
Slope (derivative) of the log-likelihood at \(p\): positive left of the peak, zero at it, negative right of it.

Each copy of C raises the slope by \(1/p\) and each copy of T lowers it by \(1/(1 - p)\). Setting the slope to zero gives \(m(1 - p) = (2n - m)p\), so \(\hat{p} = m/2n\): the maximum-likelihood estimate is exactly the allele count of slide 75 divided by the number of copies. Worked number: \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{C}} = 1{,}018/1{,}532 = 0.664\). At the extremes, a sample with no copy of C has its maximum at \(p = 0\), and a sample of CC people at \(p = 1\).

The standard error

The standard deviation measures the spread of a set of numbers: it is the square root of their variance, the average squared distance from their mean. Imagine drawing many samples of 766 people from the same population and computing \(\hat{p}\) in each. The estimates would scatter around the population value \(p\). Their distribution is the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}\), and its standard deviation is the standard error of \(\hat{p}\). A binomial count of \(N\) trials with success probability \(p\) has mean \(Np\) and variance \(Np(1 - p)\), which gives:

\[ \begin{gathered} \Var(\hat{p}) = \frac{p(1 - p)}{2n} \\ \SE(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})}{2n}} \end{gathered} \]
\(\Var(\hat{p})\)
Variance of the estimate over repeated samples of \(n\) people from the same population.
\(p\)
Frequency of the allele in the population; unknown.
\(2n\)
Number of allele copies in each sample, treated as independent (Hardy–Weinberg proportions).
\(\SE(\hat{p})\)
Standard error: the square root of the variance, with the estimate \(\hat{p}\) in place of the unknown \(p\). In the same units as \(\hat{p}\).

\(\hat{p} = m/2n\) divides a binomial count by \(2n\), which divides its variance, \(2n\,p(1 - p)\), by \((2n)^2\). The standard error is largest at \(p = 0.5\), falls to 0 as \(p\) approaches 0 or 1, and shrinks with the square root of the number of people: four times as many people halve it. The same number comes out of Figure 1: the curvature of the log-likelihood at its peak, \(-\ell''(\hat{p}) = 2n/[\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})]\), is one over the variance, so a sharply peaked curve means a precise estimate. Worked number: \(\sqrt{0.664 \times 0.336/1{,}532} = 0.0121\) for MTHFR. Swapping \(\hat{p}\) and \(1 - \hat{p}\) changes nothing, so the frequencies of C and T have the same standard error.

With 192 people instead of 766, the standard error doubles to 0.024: the dashed curve of Figure 1. The formula also plans a study. To estimate a frequency near 0.3 with a standard error of 0.01, solve \(0.3 \times 0.7/(2n) = 0.01^2\): \(2n = 2{,}100\) allele copies, or 1,050 people. Page 4 used the same formula when it compared frequencies between datasets: near 0.45, 1,401 people give a standard error of 0.009 and 503 people 0.016 (A/T and C/G SNPs).

Confidence intervals

A confidence interval turns a standard error into a range. It is computed from the data by a rule that, over repeated samples, captures the population value a stated share of the time, usually 95%. The most common rule uses the normal distribution, the bell-shaped distribution that a sum of many independent pieces approaches. A binomial count with many trials is close to normal, and 95% of a normal distribution lies within 1.96 standard deviations of its mean. The resulting interval is called the Wald interval:

\[ \hat{p} \pm 1.96\, \SE(\hat{p}) \]
\(\hat{p}\)
Estimated allele frequency.
\(1.96\)
The number of standard deviations that leaves 2.5% of a normal distribution in each tail, 95% in between.
\(\SE(\hat{p})\)
Standard error of \(\hat{p}\), from the equation above.

If \(\hat{p}\) is close to normal around \(p\) with standard deviation \(\SE(\hat{p})\), it falls within 1.96 standard errors of \(p\) in 95% of samples, and in exactly those samples the interval around \(\hat{p}\) contains \(p\). Worked numbers: for C, \(0.664 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0121\) runs from 0.641 to 0.688; for T, from 0.312 to 0.359. The 95% describes the rule, not this one interval: the population frequency is a fixed number, and this interval either contains it or does not.

A third rule reads the interval straight off Figure 1: every candidate whose log-likelihood lies within 1.92 of the peak (1.92 is half of 3.84, which is \(1.96^2\)). For MTHFR it gives 0.641–0.688 for C, the same as the Wald interval to three decimals. The rules part ways when an allele is rare. Suppose 3 of 200 allele copies (100 people) carry an allele: \(\hat{p} = 0.015\), \(\SE(\hat{p}) = 0.0086\), and the Wald interval runs from −0.002 to 0.032, below zero. With no copies at all the standard error is 0, and the Wald interval shrinks to the single point 0, as if the frequency were known exactly. In simulations the Wald interval contains the true value far less often than 95% when the expected number of copies is small7.

Wilson’s score interval of 1927 avoids both failures8. It keeps every candidate \(p\) for which the observed \(\hat{p}\) lies within 1.96 standard errors of it, with the standard error computed at the candidate, \(\sqrt{p(1 - p)/N}\), instead of at \(\hat{p}\). Solving that condition for \(p\) gives:

\[ \begin{gathered} \tilde{p} = \frac{\hat{p} + z^2/(2N)}{1 + z^2/N} \\ h = \frac{z \sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})/N + z^2/(4N^2)}}{1 + z^2/N} \\ \text{interval: } \tilde{p} - h \text{ to } \tilde{p} + h \end{gathered} \]
\(\tilde{p}\)
Center of the interval: the estimate pulled toward 0.5.
\(\hat{p}\)
Estimated allele frequency, \(m/N\).
\(z\)
1.96 for a 95% interval.
\(N\)
Number of allele copies, \(2n\) (slide 79’s “N” for alleles).
\(h\)
Half the width of the interval.

The center equals \((m + z^2/2)/(N + z^2)\): the allele frequency after adding \(z^2/2 \approx 1.92\) copies of each allele to the data, which keeps it away from 0 and 1. The \(z^2/(4N^2)\) term keeps the width above zero even when \(\hat{p} = 0\). As \(N\) grows, \(\tilde{p}\) approaches \(\hat{p}\) and \(h\) approaches \(1.96\,\SE(\hat{p})\), so for common alleles in large samples the two intervals agree: for T in MTHFR, Wilson gives 0.312–0.360 against Wald’s 0.312–0.359. For 3 copies of 200, Wilson gives 0.005–0.043; for 0 copies of 200, 0–0.019. The interval never leaves the range 0 to 1.

Comparisons of many intervals for a binomial proportion recommend Wilson’s over Wald’s, especially when \(N\hat{p}\) or \(N(1 - \hat{p})\) is small9,7. For allele frequencies that is the case for rare variants, even in large studies.

When heterozygotes are too few or too many

Everything so far treated the \(2n\) copies as independent. That holds only if each person’s two copies are independent of each other, which is what Hardy–Weinberg proportions express. A departure is measured by \(F\), the relative shortfall of heterozygotes, \(F = 1 - P_{12}/(2pq)\). \(F = 0\) in Hardy–Weinberg proportions. \(F > 0\) when heterozygotes are too few: in the children of related parents, where \(F\) is Lecture 0’s inbreeding coefficient, or in a sample that pools populations with different allele frequencies (Lecture 0’s Wahlund effect). \(F < 0\) when they are too many. With a shortfall \(F\), the genotype frequencies are \(p^2 + Fpq\), \(2pq(1 - F)\) and \(q^2 + Fpq\), and the variance of \(\hat{p}\) becomes:

\[ \Var(\hat{p}) = \frac{p(1 - p)(1 + F)}{2n} \]
\(p\)
Frequency of the counted allele in the population.
\(F\)
Relative shortfall of heterozygotes in the population, \(1 - P_{12}/(2pq)\): 0 in Hardy–Weinberg proportions, 1 when nobody is heterozygous, negative when heterozygotes are in excess.
\(2n\)
Number of allele copies in the sample, from \(n\) unrelated people.

Let \(g\) be one person’s number of copies of the allele. With the genotype frequencies above, \(\Var(g) = 2pq(1 + F)\) instead of the \(2pq\) of two independent copies, and \(\hat{p}\) adds \(n\) independent values of \(g\) and divides by \(2n\), so its variance is \(n \cdot 2pq(1 + F)/(2n)^2\). At \(F = 1\) everyone carries two copies of the same allele, so \(n\) people carry only \(n\) independent copies and the variance doubles. With an excess of heterozygotes a person’s two copies tend to differ, which evens out the counts and shrinks the variance. Worked number: the MTHFR sample has \(\hat{F} = 1 - 350/341.55 = -0.025\), which lowers the standard error from 0.0121 to 0.0119; a shortfall of \(F = 0.3\) would raise it by a factor \(\sqrt{1.3} = 1.14\).

The formula is from population-genetics texts such as Weir’s10. The same reasoning applies to relatives. Two siblings share many of their alleles, so they carry less independent information than two unrelated people, and formulas that count \(2n\) independent copies understate the uncertainty; that is one reason quality control finds close relatives (QC step 4: relatedness). The formulas also assume that the people are a random sample of the population whose frequency is wanted. People with a disease estimate the frequency among people with that disease.

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium: the prediction Core

Slide 80 returns to the outline of “a few basic definitions” and pairs allele frequencies with heterozygosity: the share of people who are heterozygous at a locus. In the MTHFR sample it is \(350/766 = 0.457\); among Wolpin’s ABO genotypes it is \((1{,}080 + 377 + 186)/3{,}117 = 0.527\). Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium predicts both from the allele frequencies alone, as 0.446 and 0.536. This section derives the prediction, lists what it assumes, and uses it to estimate allele frequencies that cannot be counted.

Where p², 2pq and q² come from

Random mating means that people’s genotypes at the locus do not influence who has children with whom. A child receives one allele in the egg and one in the sperm, and under random mating these are two independent draws from the population’s alleles. If a share \(p\) of those alleles is C, the egg carries C with probability \(p\) and so does the sperm, independently, so the child is CC with probability \(p \times p = p^2\). A CT child can arise in two ways, C from the egg and T from the sperm or the other way round, each with probability \(pq\). Figure 2 draws the four combinations to scale.

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{ii} &= p_i^2 \\ P_{ij} &= 2 p_i p_j, \quad i \ne j \end{aligned} \]
\(P_{ii}\)
Frequency in the population of genotype \(ii\), homozygous for allele \(i\).
\(p_i\)
Frequency of allele \(i\) in the population, \(i = 1, \ldots, K\); the \(K\) frequencies add up to 1.
\(P_{ij}\)
Frequency of the heterozygous genotype \(ij\), counted once whichever parent gave which allele.

A homozygote needs allele \(i\) from both parents, \(p_i \times p_i\). A heterozygote can get allele \(i\) from either parent, hence the factor 2. All genotype frequencies together add up to \((p_1 + \cdots + p_K)^2 = 1\); with two alleles, \(p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = (p + q)^2 = 1\). The heterozygote frequency \(2pq\) is at most 0.5, reached at \(p = 0.5\). Worked numbers for MTHFR: 0.442, 0.446 and 0.113, which in 766 people predict 338.2 CC, 341.5 CT and 86.2 TT, against 334, 350 and 82 observed.

One generation of random mating is enough

The prediction does not depend on how the parents’ genotypes were distributed. Start from any genotype frequencies \(P_{11}\), \(P_{12}\), \(P_{22}\) among the parents. Each egg or sperm carries one of its parent’s two alleles, each with probability ½ (Mendel’s first law), so the share of allele 1 among eggs and sperm is the parents’ allele frequency. Random union then gives children in the proportions \(p^2\), \(2pq\) and \(q^2\), whatever the parents’ proportions were, and the children’s allele frequency is the parents’:

\[ \begin{gathered} p = P_{11} + \tfrac{1}{2} P_{12} \\ p' = p^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}(2pq) = p(p + q) = p \end{gathered} \]
\(P_{11}, P_{12}\)
Frequencies of genotypes 11 and 12 among the parents; any values.
\(p\)
Frequency of allele 1 among the parents, which is also its frequency among their eggs and sperm.
\(p'\)
Frequency of allele 1 among the children: all of a homozygote’s alleles and half of a heterozygote’s.

Allele frequencies pass unchanged from parents to children because every parent passes on each of their two alleles with probability ½. Genotype frequencies, in contrast, are rebuilt in every generation by random union. So one generation of random mating produces \(p^2\), \(2pq\) and \(q^2\), and the next generation, with the same \(p\), reproduces them: that is the equilibrium. Worked example: parents who are half CC and half TT, with no heterozygotes, have \(p = 0.5\); their children are 25% CC, 50% CT and 25% TT. If mothers and fathers differ in allele frequency, the first generation of children has a single allele frequency, the average of the two, and the proportions are reached one generation later.

Story 1908: a lecture in Stuttgart and a letter to Science

On 28 February 1908, at the Royal Society of Medicine in London, the geneticist Reginald Punnett lectured on Mendelism and disease. In the discussion the statistician Udny Yule remarked that if brachydactyly (short fingers) were caused by a dominant allele, one would expect, with random mating, “to get three brachydactylous persons to one normal”12. Punnett could not answer, and he later wrote: “I at once sought out G. H. Hardy with whom I was then very friendly.” Hardy, a Cambridge mathematician, showed in a letter published in Science in July 1908 that random mating keeps genotype proportions stable from one generation to the next, so that “there is not the slightest foundation for the idea that a dominant character should show a tendency to spread” through a population13. A dominant allele stays at whatever frequency it has; brachydactyly is rare because its allele is rare.

Six months earlier, on 13 January 1908, the Stuttgart physician Wilhelm Weinberg had read a paper to an evening meeting in Stuttgart that derived the same equilibrium for one locus with two alleles14,12. English-language genetics knew the result as Hardy’s law until Curt Stern drew attention to Weinberg’s paper in 1943 under the title “The Hardy–Weinberg law”15.

Using the prediction: allele frequencies from blood groups

Hardy–Weinberg proportions also make it possible to estimate allele frequencies that cannot be counted. A blood test detects antigens, not genotypes: it cannot tell AA from AO, or BB from BO (Table 1), so the copies of A, B and O cannot be counted as in Table 2. Under Hardy–Weinberg proportions, though, each blood group’s frequency is a known function of the three allele frequencies:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\mathrm{O}) &= p_{\mathrm{O}}^2 \\ P(\mathrm{A}) &= p_{\mathrm{A}}^2 + 2 p_{\mathrm{A}} p_{\mathrm{O}} \\ P(\mathrm{B}) &= p_{\mathrm{B}}^2 + 2 p_{\mathrm{B}} p_{\mathrm{O}} \\ P(\mathrm{AB}) &= 2 p_{\mathrm{A}} p_{\mathrm{B}} \end{aligned} \]
\(P(\mathrm{O}), \ldots, P(\mathrm{AB})\)
Frequencies of the four blood groups in the population.
\(p_{\mathrm{A}}, p_{\mathrm{B}}, p_{\mathrm{O}}\)
Frequencies of alleles A, B and O, adding up to 1.

Group A collects two genotypes, AA and AO, so its frequency is the sum of their Hardy–Weinberg frequencies; group O is OO alone. Two quick estimates follow. \(P(\mathrm{O}) = p_{\mathrm{O}}^2\), so \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{O}} = \sqrt{f_{\mathrm{O}}}\), where \(f_{\mathrm{O}}\) is the share of people in group O. Groups B and O together have frequency \((p_{\mathrm{B}} + p_{\mathrm{O}})^2 = (1 - p_{\mathrm{A}})^2\), so \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{A}} = 1 - \sqrt{f_{\mathrm{B}} + f_{\mathrm{O}}}\), and likewise for B. Worked numbers from Wolpin’s blood groups: \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{O}} = \sqrt{0.3747} = 0.612\), \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{A}} = 1 - \sqrt{0.5098} = 0.286\) and \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{B}} = 1 - \sqrt{0.8053} = 0.103\).

These square-root estimates add up to 1.0008, not exactly 1, because each uses only part of the data. Formulas of this kind settled how ABO is inherited. In 1924 the mathematician Felix Bernstein compared blood-group frequencies from many populations with the Hardy–Weinberg predictions of two models: two genes with two alleles each, the model accepted at the time, and one gene with three alleles. The frequencies fit only the three-allele model16.

The maximum-likelihood estimates use all four groups at once. They have no closed form, but an iterative method finds them. Ceppellini, Siniscalco and Smith described it in 1955 and called it gene counting17. Gene counting is one instance of the EM (expectation–maximization) algorithm, the general method for maximum likelihood with incomplete data that Dempster, Laird and Rubin set out in 197718; Laird’s review of the EM algorithm in genetics opens with this ABO example19. Each round has two steps. The E-step splits each blood group that mixes two genotypes in proportion to their Hardy–Weinberg frequencies at the current allele frequencies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{n}_{\mathrm{AA}} &= n_{\mathrm{A}} \frac{p_{\mathrm{A}}^2}{p_{\mathrm{A}}^2 + 2 p_{\mathrm{A}} p_{\mathrm{O}}} \\ \hat{n}_{\mathrm{AO}} &= n_{\mathrm{A}} - \hat{n}_{\mathrm{AA}} \end{aligned} \]
\(\hat{n}_{\mathrm{AA}}\)
Expected number of AA people among the people of group A, given the current allele frequencies. Need not be a whole number.
\(n_{\mathrm{A}}\)
Number of people with blood group A: 1,342.
\(p_{\mathrm{A}}, p_{\mathrm{O}}\)
Current values of the allele frequencies: a starting guess in the first round, the previous round’s estimates after that.
\(\hat{n}_{\mathrm{AO}}\)
Expected number of AO people: the rest of group A.

Among people of group A, the share who are AA is the Hardy–Weinberg frequency of AA divided by that of the whole group, which simplifies to \(p_{\mathrm{A}}/(p_{\mathrm{A}} + 2p_{\mathrm{O}})\). Group B is split the same way with \(p_{\mathrm{B}}\). Starting from equal frequencies of 1/3, the share is 1/3: 447.3 AA and 894.7 AO. At the final estimates it is 0.19: 254.3 AA and 1,087.7 AO, against the 262 AA that genotyping found.

\(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{A}}, \hat{p}_{\mathrm{B}}, \hat{p}_{\mathrm{O}}\)
Updated allele frequencies.
\(n_{\mathrm{AB}}, n_{\mathrm{O}}\)
People in groups AB and O, whose genotypes, AB and OO, are known: 186 and 1,168.
\(\hat{n}_{\mathrm{BB}}, \hat{n}_{\mathrm{BO}}\)

\(2n\)

This is slide 75’s allele count applied to the completed table: two copies per homozygote and one per heterozygote. Worked first round, from 1/3 each: \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{A}} = (2 \times 447.3 + 894.7 + 186)/6{,}234 = 0.317\), \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.120\) and \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{O}} = 0.563\). The next E-step splits the groups with these values, and the rounds repeat until the frequencies stop changing.

Testing Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium Core

The MTHFR sample has 350 heterozygotes where Hardy–Weinberg proportions predict 341.5. Is a gap of 8.5 people more than chance produces in a sample of 766? A statistical test answers such questions by a fixed recipe. The same recipe returns in every later page of this lecture, in association tests and in each quality-control filter, so this section spells it out once.

How a test works

A test starts from a null hypothesis: a precise statement about the population that the test looks for evidence against. Here the null hypothesis is that the genotype frequencies in the population are \(p^2\), \(2pq\) and \(q^2\) for some allele frequency \(p\). A test statistic is a number computed from the data that is near zero when the data agree with the null hypothesis and grows as they disagree; below, it measures the gap between observed and expected genotype counts. If the null hypothesis were true, repeated samples would give a spread of values of the statistic, its null distribution. The p-value is the probability, under that null distribution, of a statistic at least as far from the null hypothesis as the one observed. A small p-value means that data like these would be rare if the null hypothesis were true.

To decide, compare the p-value with a significance level chosen in advance, often 0.05, and reject the null hypothesis when the p-value falls below it. When the null hypothesis is true, this happens in at most 5% of samples; each such rejection is a type 1 error, a false positive. For MTHFR the p-value turns out to be about 0.5: counts this far from the prediction are common in samples of 766 people from a population in equilibrium.

Pitfall What a p-value is not
  • It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. It is computed by assuming that the null hypothesis is true.
  • A large p-value does not show that a SNP is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, only that the data cannot tell. In 200 people, a real shortfall of heterozygotes of 5% (\(F = 0.05\)) gives a statistic near \(nF^2 = 0.5\), a value that chance alone exceeds about half the time.
  • A small p-value does not say why the counts depart. Genotyping errors, a sample that mixes groups with different allele frequencies, relatives, selection, and in people with a disease an association with it, all move the counts.
  • Across many tests, small p-values appear by chance: one SNP in 20 that is in equilibrium falls below 0.05 (Hundreds of thousands of tests).

Pearson’s chi-square test

The oldest test compares observed with expected counts. An expected count is the number of people the null hypothesis predicts in a category: the number of people times the category’s probability, here with the allele frequency estimated from the same people.

\[ \begin{aligned} E_{11} &= n \hat{p}^2 \\ E_{12} &= 2 n \hat{p} \hat{q} \\ E_{22} &= n \hat{q}^2 \end{aligned} \]
\(E_{11}, E_{12}, E_{22}\)
Expected numbers of people with genotypes 11, 12 and 22 under Hardy–Weinberg proportions. Need not be whole numbers.
\(n\)
Number of people tested.
\(\hat{p}, \hat{q}\)
Allele frequencies estimated from the same people, \(\hat{q} = 1 - \hat{p}\).

The expected counts add up to \(n\), and they contain the same number of copies of allele 1 as the data: \(2E_{11} + E_{12} = 2n\hat{p}(\hat{p} + \hat{q}) = 2n\hat{p}\). So the comparison asks only how the observed allele copies are paired into people. Worked numbers for MTHFR: 338.2 CC, 341.5 CT and 86.2 TT.

\[ \begin{aligned} X^2 &= \sum_{\text{genotypes}} \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} \\ &= n \hat{F}^2 \end{aligned} \]
\(X^2\)
Pearson’s chi-square statistic: 0 when the observed counts equal the expected ones, larger as they differ.
\(O\)
Observed number of people with a genotype.
\(E\)
Expected number of people with that genotype, from the equation above.
\(\sum_{\text{genotypes}}\)
Sum over the three genotypes.
\(\hat{F}\)
Estimated relative shortfall of heterozygotes, \(1 - O_{12}/E_{12}\); negative when heterozygotes are in excess.

Each genotype contributes its squared gap divided by its expected count, so a gap of 8 people weighs more in a small category than in a large one. Because the expected counts carry the observed allele counts, the three gaps are tied together: they are always \(-d\), \(+2d\) and \(-d\) for some \(d\). For MTHFR they are −4.23, +8.45 and −4.23, and the contributions are 0.053, 0.209 and 0.207, so \(X^2 = 0.469\). One number carries all the information, and the sum simplifies to \(n\hat{F}^2\): \(\hat{F} = 1 - 350/341.55 = -0.0247\) and \(766 \times 0.0247^2 = 0.469\).

The statistic needs a null distribution. When the null hypothesis is true and every expected count is reasonably large (a common rule is at least 5), \(X^2\) follows approximately a chi-square distribution: the distribution of a sum of squares of independent standard normal variables, whose number is its degrees of freedom20. The degrees of freedom count the independent ways in which the observed counts can depart from the expected ones:

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{df} = (\text{classes} - 1) - \text{parameters} \\ \text{SNP: } (3 - 1) - 1 = 1 \\ K \text{ alleles: } K(K - 1)/2 \end{gathered} \]
\(\text{df}\)
Degrees of freedom of the chi-square distribution used for the p-value.
\(\text{classes}\)
Number of genotype categories, \(K(K + 1)/2\) for \(K\) alleles: 3 at a SNP, 6 for ABO.
\(\text{parameters}\)
Number of allele frequencies estimated from the data: \(K - 1\), because the \(K\) frequencies add up to 1.
\(K\)
Number of alleles at the locus.

Three counts that must add up to \(n\) leave two free numbers. Estimating \(p\) from the same counts fixes one more, because the expected counts are forced to carry the observed allele count, and one free number is left: the \(d\) above. With \(K\) alleles, \(K(K+1)/2 - 1 - (K - 1) = K(K - 1)/2\): 1 for a SNP, 3 for ABO. Pearson’s original test subtracted only the 1; Fisher showed in 1922 that each parameter estimated from the data costs one more degree of freedom21. With 2 degrees of freedom instead of 1, every p-value from a SNP would be too large.

With 1 degree of freedom, \(X^2\) is the square of one standard normal variable, so 5% of its distribution lies above \(1.96^2 = 3.84\). For MTHFR, 49% of the distribution lies above 0.469: the p-value is 0.49 (Figure 3).

The exact test

The chi-square distribution is an approximation that needs large expected counts, and at a SNP with a rare allele the expected number of people homozygous for it can be far below 1. The exact test does without the approximation. Levene worked out in 1949 the exact distribution of the number of heterozygotes when the number of people and the allele counts are fixed, and Haldane used it as a test in 195422,23. Wigginton, Cutler and Abecasis gave a fast way to compute it in 2005 and showed that the chi-square test produces too many small p-values “even in relatively large samples”24. It is the test that PLINK’s --hardy and --hwe report5.

\[ \begin{gathered} \Pr(n_{12} \mid n, m_1) = \\ \frac{2^{n_{12}}\, n!\, m_1!\, m_2!}{n_{11}!\, n_{12}!\, n_{22}!\, (2n)!} \end{gathered} \]
\(\Pr(n_{12} \mid n, m_1)\)
Probability of exactly \(n_{12}\) heterozygotes among \(n\) people who carry \(m_1\) copies of allele 1 between them, if genotypes are in Hardy–Weinberg proportions.
\(m_1, m_2\)
Copies of alleles 1 and 2 in the sample, \(m_1 = 2n_{11} + n_{12}\) and \(m_2 = 2n - m_1\). Fixed at their observed values.
\(n_{11}, n_{22}\)
Numbers of homozygotes, determined by \(n_{12}\): \(n_{11} = (m_1 - n_{12})/2\) and \(n_{22} = (m_2 - n_{12})/2\).
\(k!\)
Factorial: \(1 \times 2 \times \cdots \times k\), with \(0! = 1\).

Under Hardy–Weinberg proportions a sample is its \(2n\) allele copies paired at random into \(n\) people. There are \((2n)!/(m_1!\,m_2!)\) distinct ways to line up the copies, and \(2^{n_{12}}\, n!/(n_{11}!\,n_{12}!\,n_{22}!)\) of them give \(n_{12}\) heterozygotes: \(n!/(n_{11}!\,n_{12}!\,n_{22}!)\) ways to hand out the genotypes and two orders within each heterozygote. The probability is their ratio. The allele frequency does not appear, so the test needs no estimate of it. \(n_{12}\) is even when \(m_1\) is even and odd when it is odd. Worked numbers: with 1,018 copies of C and 514 of T among 766 people, \(n_{12}\) can be 0, 2, …, 514; the most probable value is 342 (probability 0.065), and the observed 350 has probability 0.052.

The exact test’s p-value adds the probabilities of every possible heterozygote count that is no more probable than the observed one:

\[ \sum_{h:\ \Pr(h) \le \Pr(n_{12})} \Pr(h \mid n, m_1) \]
\(h\)
Each possible number of heterozygotes for these allele counts.
\(\Pr(h) \le \Pr(n_{12})\)
The condition: counts no more probable than the observed count \(n_{12}\).
\(\Pr(h \mid n, m_1)\)
Probability of \(h\) heterozygotes, from the equation above.

This sum is the p-value: the probability, under the null hypothesis, of a table as unusual as the observed one or more so. It counts departures in both directions, too few and too many heterozygotes, because both sides of the distribution enter wherever their probabilities are small. For MTHFR, counts of 332 or fewer contribute 0.238 and counts of 350 or more 0.280, so the p-value is 0.518, close to the chi-square test’s 0.493 because all expected counts are large (Figure 4).

In the first row, one person carries both copies of a rare allele and nobody carries one copy. Hardy–Weinberg proportions expect 0.01 rare homozygotes, and dividing by that tiny expected count makes \(X^2 = 100\), with an approximate p-value of 10⁻²³. The exact test sees what is actually possible. With 2 copies of the allele among 100 people, either one person carries both (probability 0.005) or two people carry one each (probability 0.995). The observed table is the less probable one, and its p-value is 0.005; no table with these allele counts can do better. That floor is the smallest p-value the SNP can reach, and with 2 copies among 1,000 people it is 0.0005. A SNP whose rare allele is carried by a handful of people cannot reach the thresholds used in quality control, however its genotypes fall, so the exact test does not remove it on the strength of one person, while the chi-square test would.

The rule of thumb that every expected count should be at least 5 fails first for the rare homozygotes. Their expected count, \(n\hat{q}^2\), falls below 5 when \(\hat{q} < \sqrt{5/n}\): below 0.071 with 1,000 people and below 0.032 with 5,000. SNP arrays carry many SNPs with frequencies below these, which is one reason quality control uses the exact test.

The mid-p value

A test whose statistic takes only a few values is conservative: when a SNP is in equilibrium, its p-value falls below a threshold \(t\) less often than a share \(t\) of the time, much less for rare alleles, whose p-values cannot fall below their floor at all. The mid-p value counts the probability of tables exactly as probable as the observed one, including the observed table, only half:

\[ \begin{gathered} \sum_{h:\ \Pr(h) < \Pr(n_{12})} \Pr(h \mid n, m_1) \\ {}+ \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{h:\ \Pr(h) = \Pr(n_{12})} \Pr(h \mid n, m_1) \end{gathered} \]
First sum
Probabilities of all heterozygote counts strictly less probable than the observed count.
Second sum
Probabilities of the counts exactly as probable as the observed one, the observed count included, counted at half weight.

The ordinary exact p-value counts the observed table in full; the mid-p value counts half of it. When a test has many possible outcomes, the difference is small: for MTHFR the mid-p value is 0.492 instead of 0.518. When it has few, the difference is large: for the first row of Table 5 the mid-p value is 0.0025 instead of 0.0050. Under the null hypothesis the mid-p value falls below \(t\) about a share \(t\) of the time, sometimes a little more.

Graffelman and Moreno showed that for exact tests of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium the mid-p value keeps the rate of false rejections closer to the significance level and has more power, the probability of detecting a departure that is really there, at the price of occasionally exceeding the significance level25. PLINK computes mid-p values with the midp modifier (--hardy midp, --hwe 1e-6 midp), and its documentation recommends the modifier for --hwe26. The practical’s --hardy run reports the ordinary exact p-value.

What a genotyping error does to the counts

Page 4 describes how genotypes are called from two signal intensities (From a DNA sample to a genotype matrix). A common failure is that heterozygotes, whose signals lie between the two homozygous clusters, are called homozygous. Suppose that at a problem SNP each heterozygote is called homozygous with probability \(e\), half the time as each homozygote. The allele frequency stays the same on average, but the heterozygote count falls by the share \(e\), and the test statistic follows:

\[ \begin{gathered} \hat{F} \approx e \\ X^2 = n \hat{F}^2 \approx n e^2 \end{gathered} \]
\(e\)
Share of heterozygotes called homozygous at the SNP, 0 to 1.
\(\hat{F}\)
Estimated relative shortfall of heterozygotes.
\(n\)
Number of people tested.
\(X^2\)
Chi-square statistic, 1 degree of freedom.

The expected heterozygote count depends only on the allele frequency, which the error leaves unchanged, while the observed count shrinks by the share \(e\); so \(\hat{F} = 1 - O_{12}/E_{12} \approx e\). The statistic then grows with the number of people times the squared error rate. Worked numbers: \(e = 0.1\) in 1,000 people gives \(X^2 \approx 10\) and a p-value of 0.0016, far above the thresholds QC uses; in 5,000 people \(X^2 \approx 50\) and the p-value is 1.5 × 10⁻¹². A p-value below 10⁻⁶ needs \(X^2 > 23.9\), so \(ne^2 > 23.9\): 2,393 people for \(e = 0.1\), and 9,572 for \(e = 0.05\).

Gross failures are caught in any study: a SNP that calls nobody heterozygous has \(\hat{F} = 1\) and \(X^2 = n\). Subtle ones are caught only in large studies. Errors in the other direction exist too, such as a probe whose sequence also matches a second, slightly different place in the genome, which makes homozygotes look heterozygous (\(\hat{F} < 0\)); QC step 2 lists the causes. Not every departure is an error. A sample that mixes groups of people with different allele frequencies, such as people of different genetic ancestry, has too few heterozygotes at every SNP whose frequency differs between the groups, and inbred people have too few at every SNP.

Why QC tests the controls

People with a disease, the cases, are selected for the disease. At a SNP that affects the disease, selecting on the disease selects on genotype, and that can move the cases’ genotype counts away from Hardy–Weinberg proportions even when the population is in equilibrium. The genotype frequencies among cases follow from the definition of conditional probability:

\[ P(g \mid \text{case}) = \frac{P(g)\, R_g}{\sum_h P(h)\, R_h} \]
\(g\)
Genotype: the number of copies of the risk allele, 0, 1 or 2.
\(P(g \mid \text{case})\)
Frequency of genotype \(g\) among cases.
\(P(g)\)
Frequency of genotype \(g\) in the population: \((1 - q)^2\), \(2q(1 - q)\) and \(q^2\) under Hardy–Weinberg proportions, where \(q\) is the frequency of the risk allele.
\(R_g\)
Genotype relative risk: the risk of the disease for genotype \(g\) divided by the risk for genotype 0, so \(R_0 = 1\).
\(\sum_h P(h)\, R_h\)
Sum over the three genotypes \(h\); it makes the case frequencies add up to 1.

Each genotype is represented among cases in proportion to its population frequency times its risk. Whether the result is in Hardy–Weinberg proportions depends on the pattern of risks. If each copy multiplies the risk by the same factor \(r\) (\(R = 1, r, r^2\)), the cases are in Hardy–Weinberg proportions with a higher allele frequency, because each copy acts separately. If only two copies raise the risk (a recessive model, \(R = 1, 1, r\)), cases have too many risk homozygotes and \(F > 0\); if one copy is enough (dominant, \(R = 1, r, r\)), they have too many heterozygotes and \(F < 0\). Worked numbers: with a risk allele at frequency 0.3 and a recessive relative risk of 2, cases are 45.0%, 38.5% and 16.5% with 0, 1 and 2 copies, and \(F = 0.16\). In 1,000 cases that gives \(X^2 \approx 26\) and a p-value of 3 × 10⁻⁷.

Tested in cases, such a SNP would be removed by a QC threshold of 10⁻⁶, although its genotypes are correct and its association is real. Controls, people without the disease, are not selected on it. For a disease that affects about 1% of people they are almost a random sample of the population: in the recessive example their \(F\) is −0.002. For a common disease they depart a little in the other direction, \(F = -0.057\) when the disease affects 22% of people, still far from any QC threshold in a study of normal size.

Slide 83’s last use turns this round: a departure among cases can be evidence of an association27. It has low power. A multiplicative pattern of risks produces no departure at all, and a recessive relative risk of 1.2 at the same allele frequency gives \(F = 0.04\) and \(X^2 \approx 1.6\) in 1,000 cases, a p-value of 0.21; reaching 10⁻⁶ would take about 15,000 cases. Association tests that compare cases with controls (GWAS analysis) are the standard approach.

PLINK builds this in. For case–control data, --hardy reports each SNP three times: ALL (everyone), AFF (cases, phenotype code 2) and UNAFF (controls, code 1)5. --hwe removes SNPs on the controls alone: “with case/control data, cases and missing phenotypes are normally ignored”, unless the modifier include-nonctrl is added26. The practical reads the UNAFF rows “because a genuine disease association can cause HWE departures among cases”6. In PennCATH these are the people coded 1, the practical’s controls; What column 6 holds explains why that code needs checking against the study’s records.

Hundreds of thousands of tests

Quality control runs the test once per SNP: 861,473 times for PennCATH. If every SNP were in equilibrium and the test were continuous, the p-values would be spread evenly between 0 and 1 (a uniform distribution), so a share \(t\) of them would fall below any threshold \(t\). For the exact test the share is at most \(t\).

\[ \E[\text{SNPs below } t] \le M t \]
\(\E[\text{SNPs below } t]\)
Expected number of SNPs in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium whose p-value falls below the threshold.
\(M\)
Number of SNPs tested.
\(t\)
The p-value threshold, between 0 and 1.

Each SNP in equilibrium falls below \(t\) with probability at most \(t\), and expected values add up across SNPs whether or not the tests are independent. Worked numbers for 861,473 SNPs: about 43,074 below 0.05, 861 below 10⁻³, 0.86 below 10⁻⁶ and 0.009 below 10⁻⁸. A threshold of 10⁻³ therefore removes hundreds of good SNPs by chance, and 10⁻⁶ about one. The Bonferroni correction, \(0.05/M = 5.8 \times 10^{-8}\), keeps the probability of even one chance removal below 5%.

A quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plot shows all \(M\) p-values at once. Sort them and plot \(-\log_{10}\) of the \(k\)-th smallest against \(-\log_{10}[(k - 0.5)/M]\), the value expected for the \(k\)-th smallest of \(M\) evenly spread p-values. SNPs in equilibrium lie on the diagonal, and SNPs with errors rise above it at the top right. Lecture 0 introduces the plot for GWAS results (QQ plots), and explains why neighboring SNPs make the tests of a GWAS less independent than they look (the effective number of tests).

Choosing a threshold

The practical runs --hardy, keeps the UNAFF rows, draws a histogram of their p-values and counts the SNPs below 10⁻⁸. It applies no filter and leaves the threshold to a discussion question: “a higher cutoff (e.g., 1e-3) is more aggressive and removes more markers, while a lower cutoff (e.g., 1e-6) is more permissive and retains more”6. The calculations on this page frame the choice:

  • Chance removals. About \(Mt\) SNPs in equilibrium fall below \(t\): for PennCATH’s 861,473 SNPs, 861 at 10⁻³ and about one at 10⁻⁶.
  • Errors caught. For a given error rate the statistic grows with the number of people, \(X^2 \approx ne^2\), so the same threshold catches subtler errors in a larger study. PLINK’s documentation recommends a low threshold, because “serious genotyping errors often yield extreme p-values like 1e-50”26.
  • Real departures. A sample that mixes groups with different allele frequencies departs at every SNP whose frequencies differ between the groups, so a threshold applied to the mixed sample removes good SNPs; testing within each group avoids that (Several ancestry groups in one study).

In the reading Uffelmann et al. 2021 on Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

The primer defines Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium in a margin glossary of its “Experimentation” section and adds: “A test for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium is often used in quality control of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to filter out variants with possible genotype calling errors”28. Its quality-control paragraph lists the removal of variants that are not in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium among the standard steps, and Table 1 names PLINK as a tool for it. The primer does not discuss which people to test or which threshold to use; this page and QC step 2 do.

Key points
  • At one locus the data are genotype counts. Genotype frequencies divide by \(n\) people; allele frequencies divide by \(2n\) copies: \(\hat{p} = (2n_{11} + n_{12})/2n\), which is also the maximum-likelihood estimate.
  • In Hardy–Weinberg proportions \(\SE(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})/2n}\), multiplied by \(\sqrt{1 + F}\) when heterozygotes fall short by \(F\). A 95% interval is \(\hat{p} \pm 1.96\) standard errors; use Wilson’s interval for rare alleles.
  • Random mating makes a person’s two alleles independent draws, which gives \(p^2\), \(2pq\) and \(q^2\) (\(p_i^2\) and \(2p_ip_j\) with \(K\) alleles) after one generation, with the allele frequency unchanged.
  • Gene counting, an EM algorithm, uses Hardy–Weinberg proportions to estimate ABO allele frequencies from blood groups.
  • Pearson’s \(X^2 = \sum (O - E)^2/E = n\hat{F}^2\) has 1 degree of freedom at a SNP and \(K(K - 1)/2\) with \(K\) alleles. The exact test, which PLINK reports, stays valid for rare alleles; the mid-p value makes it less conservative.
  • QC tests the controls, because a real association can move the cases’ counts. About \(Mt\) of \(M\) good SNPs fall below a threshold \(t\) by chance, and \(X^2 \approx ne^2\) means that larger studies catch subtler errors.

Check your understanding

From the ABO genotypes of Table 1, estimate the frequency of allele B, its standard error and a 95% confidence interval.

Copies of B: \(2 \times 44 + 377 + 186 = 651\) (two per BB person, one per OB and AB person), out of \(2 \times 3{,}117 = 6{,}234\) copies, so \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.104\).

\(\SE(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{B}}) = \sqrt{0.104 \times 0.896/6{,}234} = 0.0039\), and the Wald interval is \(0.104 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0039\), from 0.097 to 0.112. The formula assumes Hardy–Weinberg proportions, which Table 4 does not contradict (p-value 0.24). With 651 copies of the allele, the Wilson interval would be almost the same.

The 95% interval for the MTHFR frequency of C runs from 0.641 to 0.688. How many people would a study need to make the interval half as wide?

The width is \(2 \times 1.96 \times \SE(\hat{p})\), and the standard error falls with the square root of the number of people. Halving it takes four times as many people: \(4 \times 766 = 3{,}064\). The interval would then be about \(0.664 \pm 0.012\).

Why does the chi-square test of Hardy–Weinberg proportions have 1 degree of freedom at a SNP but 3 for the ABO genotypes?

A SNP has 3 genotype counts that must add up to \(n\), which leaves 2 free numbers, and the expected counts use one allele frequency estimated from the same data: \(2 - 1 = 1\). The single free number is the shortfall or excess of heterozygotes; the two homozygote gaps are each minus half the heterozygote gap.

ABO has 6 genotypes (5 free numbers) and 2 estimated frequencies (the third is 1 minus the other two): \(5 - 2 = 3\), which is \(K(K - 1)/2\) for \(K = 3\). With 3 degrees of freedom the 5% cutoff is 7.81 instead of 3.84.

Among 100 people at a SNP, 1 is homozygous for the rare allele, none is heterozygous and 99 are homozygous for the common allele. The chi-square test gives a p-value of 10⁻²³ and the exact test 0.005. Which do you trust, and could this SNP fail a QC threshold of 10⁻⁶?

Trust the exact test. The expected number of rare homozygotes is 0.01, far below the 5 that the chi-square approximation needs, and dividing by it inflates \(X^2\) to 100. With only 2 copies of the rare allele, only two tables are possible: both copies in one person (probability 0.005) or in two heterozygotes (0.995).

So 0.005 is the smallest p-value this SNP can have, and it cannot fail a threshold of 10⁻⁶ with the exact test, whatever its genotypes. With the chi-square test it would be removed on the strength of a single person.

Gene counting starts with \(p_{\mathrm{A}} = p_{\mathrm{B}} = p_{\mathrm{O}} = 1/3\). Why does the first E-step split blood group A into one third AA and two thirds AO?

Within group A, the share of AA is \(p_{\mathrm{A}}^2/(p_{\mathrm{A}}^2 + 2p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{O}})\). With all three frequencies at 1/3 this is \((1/9)/(1/9 + 2/9) = 1/3\). So the 1,342 people of group A become 447.3 AA and 894.7 AO.

The M-step then counts \(2 \times 447.3 + 894.7 + 186 = 1{,}975.3\) copies of A among 6,234, so \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{A}} = 0.317\). As \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{O}}\) grows over the rounds, the AA share falls, to 0.19 at convergence.

At a SNP genotyped in 2,000 controls, 5% of heterozygotes are called homozygous, half each way. Roughly what p-value does the test give, and does a threshold of 10⁻⁶ catch the error?

\(\hat{F} \approx e = 0.05\), so \(X^2 \approx ne^2 = 2{,}000 \times 0.0025 = 5\), a p-value of about 0.025. The threshold of 10⁻⁶ needs \(X^2 > 23.9\), so the error goes through.

The same error rate would be caught with about \(23.9/0.0025 \approx 9{,}600\) people. Other checks, such as the call rate of the SNP (QC step 1), can still catch a poorly clustering SNP.

A study tests 500,000 SNPs that are all in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. About how many fall below 10⁻³ by chance, and how many below 10⁻⁶? What threshold keeps the chance of any false removal below 5%?

At most \(500{,}000 \times 10^{-3} = 500\) below 10⁻³ and \(500{,}000 \times 10^{-6} = 0.5\) below 10⁻⁶; the exact test gives somewhat fewer, because it is conservative.

The Bonferroni threshold is \(0.05/500{,}000 = 10^{-7}\). At that threshold the expected number of chance removals is 0.05, and the probability of at least one is at most 5%.

Why does PLINK’s --hwe ignore cases by default? What would happen at a SNP with a real recessive effect if all people were tested?

At a SNP that affects the disease, cases are selected on genotype. With a recessive effect they carry too many risk homozygotes: for a risk allele at 0.3 and a relative risk of 2, \(F = 0.16\) among cases, and 1,000 cases alone give a p-value near 3 × 10⁻⁷. Tested in cases, or in everyone with cases included, the SNP could be removed at 10⁻⁶ although its genotypes are correct and its association is exactly what the study looks for.

Controls are not selected on the disease and stay close to equilibrium, so a departure there points to the genotyping or to the mix of people. include-nonctrl makes --hwe test all founders instead.

A colleague says: “The exact test gives MTHFR a p-value of 0.52, so the SNP is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.” What is wrong with this?

A p-value of 0.52 says that counts this far from the prediction are common when the null hypothesis is true. It does not show that the null hypothesis is true: small departures produce such p-values too, especially in small samples.

Here \(\hat{F} = -0.025\). A real shortfall of 5% in 766 people would give \(X^2 \approx 766 \times 0.05^2 = 1.9\), a typical p-value near 0.17, also unremarkable. The right statement is that the data show no evidence against Hardy–Weinberg proportions.
