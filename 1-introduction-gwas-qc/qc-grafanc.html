QC step 8: genetic ancestry assignment with GrafAnc

Step 7 placed the study's people among reference samples, but only after a careful merge, and its axes depend on who is in the merged file. GrafAnc answers the same question with reference allele frequencies built into the program: for each person on their own, it measures how probable their genotypes are under each of several reference populations, and turns those distances into scores, three ancestry percentages and a group code. This page shows how the numbers are made, how to run the program, and what its groups do and do not mean.

Mental model

Distances to reference populations become scores, and fixed rules turn scores into groups

GrafAnc measures, for each person, how probable their genotypes are under the allele frequencies of reference populations. Three of these distances place the person relative to a triangle of European, African and East Asian references (GD1–GD3, Pe, Pf, Pa); fifteen more compare pairs of finer populations; and a fixed set of rules turns the scores into a group code. The scores are continuous measures of genetic similarity, and the group is a summary of them.

  • A fixed reference built into the program. GrafAnc compares each person with stored allele frequencies at up to 282,424 SNPs, so no merge is needed and the result does not depend on the rest of the study (what the step finds).
  • Distances become a point in a triangle. The person's distances to E, F and A are compared with the distances expected for members of each; barycentric coordinates turn the comparison into Pe, Pf and Pa, and contrast scores resolve finer structure (the statistics).
  • Unique IDs first, then one command. GrafAnc names people by their individual ID, and every PennCATH individual ID is 1, so --update-ids comes first (run it).
  • People on the GD1–GD2 plane. The practical's plot colors each person by GrafAnc's continental group; comparing it with step 7's PCA checks both methods (look at the plane).
  • Rules make groups; groups are not identities. The group codes come from thresholds on scores relative to GrafAnc's reference populations, and the practical warns against excluding anyone because of them (choose a rule).

What the step finds Core

GrafAnc answers the question of step 7, where each person sits relative to reference populations, in a way that does not need a merge. It grew out of GRAF-pop, a tool written at NCBI for the quality control of dbGaP, NIH's database of genotypes and phenotypes, which by 2019 held genotypes of more than a million people from hundreds of studies, typed on many different arrays.2 GRAF-pop used 10,000 SNPs and five reference populations whose allele frequencies came from dbGaP participants. GrafAnc, released in 2025 by GRAF-pop's first author and colleagues, uses 282,424 SNPs and 26 reference populations, mostly UK Biobank participants grouped by country of birth plus 1000 Genomes samples. It computes 18 scores per person and assigns each person to one of 8 continental and 38 subcontinental groups.1,3

Four design choices make GrafAnc different from a PCA:

  • Each person is scored on their own. Adding, removing or duplicating other people changes nothing, so relatives and duplicates can stay in the input, and results from separate runs can be combined.3
  • The reference is a table of allele frequencies, AncSnpPopAFs.txt, shipped with the program. GrafAnc reads the person's genotypes at the ancestry SNPs and ignores every other SNP.
  • The SNPs were chosen once, carefully. From 784,256 UK Biobank SNPs the authors kept biallelic SNPs, removed A/T and G/C SNPs "to minimize strand alignment issues", removed regions of high LD, pruned for LD, kept SNPs at least 500 base pairs apart, and dropped SNPs whose allele frequency differs by less than 0.05 between every pair of 1000 Genomes super-populations.1 So no LD pruning is needed before running it.
  • Missing genotypes are tolerated. Each score uses the SNPs the person has, and the documentation asks for at least 10,000 genotyped ancestry SNPs for continental groups and 50,000 for subcontinental ones; with fewer than 100 it reports nothing.3

The strand problem of step 7 is handled inside the program. For each ancestry SNP, GrafAnc compares the two letters in the .bim file with its own: it accepts the same letters, the letters swapped, their complements (the other strand) or the complements swapped, and leaves out SNPs whose letters match none of these. Because no ancestry SNP is A/T or C/G, a complement can always be recognized.5

As a QC step it finds what step 7 finds, people whose genetic similarity differs from most of the study, and it can expose data errors: since 2018, dbGaP has used GRAF-pop to find inconsistencies between the populations that studies report and those that the genotypes predict.2 It also gives every person continuous scores that can serve as covariates and a group that can define strata, for example for Hardy–Weinberg tests within groups of similar people, a use the GrafAnc authors describe.1

Slides Slides 98–99 · Model-based and model-free maps of genetic similarity

Slide 98 sorts the methods into model-based (STRUCTURE, GRAF-pop) and model-free (PCA). "Model-based" here means that the method writes down a probability for each genotype: in each population the two copies of a SNP are drawn independently with that population's allele frequency (Hardy–Weinberg proportions). Slide 99 says GRAF-pop "uses a similar conceptual model" to STRUCTURE in its supervised form, with the reference populations fixed in advance. The difference: STRUCTURE fits ancestry proportions by maximizing a likelihood, while GRAF-pop and GrafAnc compute a distance to each reference and read proportions from a triangle, as below.

The statistics: distances, a triangle and contrast scores Core

The distance from a person to a reference population

Take one SNP and call its alleles allele 1 and allele 2. A person's genotype \(g\) is the number of copies of allele 1 they carry: 0, 1 or 2. In a reference population where allele 1 has frequency \(p\) and allele 2 frequency \(q = 1 - p\), and the two copies are inherited independently (Hardy–Weinberg proportions), each genotype has a probability:

\[ P(g \mid p) = \begin{cases} q^2 & g = 0 \\ 2pq & g = 1 \\ p^2 & g = 2 \end{cases} \]
\(g\)
The person's genotype at the SNP: copies of allele 1 (0, 1 or 2). GrafAnc counts copies of the allele listed in its reference file.
\(p\), \(q\)
The frequencies of allele 1 and allele 2 in the reference population, \(q = 1 - p\).
\(P(g \mid p)\)
The probability that a person drawn from that population has genotype \(g\).

Two copies, each allele 1 with probability \(p\): both are allele 1 with probability \(p^2\), both allele 2 with \(q^2\), and one of each in two orders, \(2pq\). With \(p = 0.9\): \(P(2) = 0.81\), \(P(1) = 0.18\), \(P(0) = 0.01\). A person with no copy of allele 1 is unusual in this population; in a population with \(p = 0.2\) the same genotype has probability 0.64.

Over many SNPs, GRAF-pop multiplies these probabilities, which treats the SNPs as independent (they were chosen far apart and pruned for LD), takes the negative logarithm, and divides by the number of SNPs:2

\[ D_j = -\frac{1}{S} \sum_{l=1}^{S} \ln P(g_l \mid p_{jl}) \]
\(S\)
The number of ancestry SNPs at which this person has a genotype (at most 282,424 in GrafAnc).
\(l\)
Indexes those SNPs, \(l = 1, \dots, S\).
\(g_l\)
The person's genotype at SNP \(l\).
\(p_{jl}\)
The frequency of allele 1 of SNP \(l\) in reference population \(j\).
\(D_j\)
The genetic distance from the person to population \(j\): the average, over SNPs, of \(-\ln\) of the probability of the person's genotype. Always positive; smaller means more similar.

The product of the probabilities is the likelihood of the person's genotypes if they were a member of population \(j\); the logarithm turns the product into a sum, and dividing by \(S\) makes people with different numbers of genotyped SNPs comparable. Each SNP adds \(-\ln P\): with \(p = 0.9\), genotype 2 adds \(-\ln 0.81 = 0.21\) and genotype 0 adds \(-\ln 0.01 = 4.61\), so genotypes that are rare in a population push the distance up. If a person had to belong to one of the populations, and each were equally likely beforehand, the population with the smallest \(D_j\) would be the most probable by Bayes' theorem; GRAF-pop uses the distances as coordinates instead, because many people do not belong to one population.

From three distances to a point in a triangle

Three reference populations define the continental scores: European (E), African (F) and East Asian (A). A person has three distances, \(D_{\mathrm{E}}\), \(D_{\mathrm{F}}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{A}}\), which can be drawn as a point in three dimensions. To know what a point means, GRAF-pop computes where a typical member of each reference group would sit: the expected distances, averaging over the genotypes such a member could have.

\[ \bar{D}_{Uj} = -\frac{1}{S} \sum_{l=1}^{S} \sum_{g=0}^{2} P(g \mid p_{Ul}) \ln P(g \mid p_{jl}) \]
\(U\)
One of the three corner groups: E, F or A.
\(p_{Ul}\)
The frequency of allele 1 of SNP \(l\) in group \(U\).
\(\bar{D}_{Uj}\)
The distance to population \(j\) expected for a member of group \(U\), over the same \(S\) SNPs the person has.

The person's genotype is replaced by every possible genotype weighted by how often a member of \(U\) has it. For one SNP with \(p = 0.9\) in both \(U\) and \(j\), the expected contribution is \(-(0.81 \ln 0.81 + 0.18 \ln 0.18 + 0.01 \ln 0.01) = 0.53\); if \(j\) has \(p = 0.5\) instead, it is 1.26. Members are closer to their own population than to others. The three points \(V_{\mathrm{E}} = (\bar{D}_{\mathrm{EE}}, \bar{D}_{\mathrm{EF}}, \bar{D}_{\mathrm{EA}})\), \(V_{\mathrm{F}}\) and \(V_{\mathrm{A}}\) are the corners of a triangle. They are recomputed for every person on that person's own SNPs, so missing genotypes do not move the corners away from the person.

The person's point \(Q = (D_{\mathrm{E}}, D_{\mathrm{F}}, D_{\mathrm{A}})\) is projected onto the plane of the triangle, and its position is written as a weighted average of the corners:

\[ Q' = \lambda_{\mathrm{E}} V_{\mathrm{E}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{F}} V_{\mathrm{F}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{A}} V_{\mathrm{A}} \] \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{E}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{F}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{A}} = 1 \]
\(Q'\)
The projection of the person's point onto the plane of the triangle.
\(V_{\mathrm{E}}, V_{\mathrm{F}}, V_{\mathrm{A}}\)
The corners: expected distances of typical members of E, F and A.
\(\lambda_{\mathrm{E}}, \lambda_{\mathrm{F}}, \lambda_{\mathrm{A}}\)
The barycentric coordinates of \(Q'\): weights that sum to 1 and reproduce \(Q'\) from the corners. Any real numbers; all between 0 and 1 exactly when \(Q'\) lies inside the triangle.

A point on a corner has weights (1, 0, 0); a point halfway along the edge from E to F has (0.5, 0.5, 0); the center of the triangle has one third each. A point outside the triangle has at least one negative weight. For a person whose ancestors come from two of the reference populations, the weights are close to the shares of their genome from each, which is why the weights are read as ancestry proportions.

GRAF-pop then places every person on one fixed triangle, so that people genotyped on different SNPs can be plotted together. GD1 and GD2 are the person's weights applied to fixed corner coordinates, and GD3 is the distance of the person's point from the plane of the triangle.2,1 GrafAnc's documentation gives the fixed corners: E at (1.4758, 1.4370), F at (1.0800, 1.1000) and A at (1.7045, 1.1000).3 Writing \(x\) for GD1 and \(y\) for GD2:

\[ x = 1.08 + 0.3958\,\lambda_{\mathrm{E}} + 0.6245\,\lambda_{\mathrm{A}} \] \[ y = 1.1 + 0.337\,\lambda_{\mathrm{E}} \]
\(x\), \(y\)
The person's GD1 and GD2 scores.
1.08, 1.1
The coordinates of the F corner, where \(\lambda_{\mathrm{F}} = 1\).
0.3958, 0.337
How far the E corner lies from the F corner along GD1 and GD2: \(1.4758 - 1.08\) and \(1.437 - 1.1\).
0.6245
How far the A corner lies from the F corner along GD1, \(1.7045 - 1.08\); the A corner has the same GD2 as F.

These two lines are \((x, y) = \lambda_{\mathrm{E}} c_{\mathrm{E}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{F}} c_{\mathrm{F}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{A}} c_{\mathrm{A}}\) for the fixed corners \(c\), with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{F}} = 1 - \lambda_{\mathrm{E}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{A}}\) substituted. Because F and A share the same GD2, the weights can be read back directly: \(\lambda_{\mathrm{E}} = (y - 1.1)/0.337\), then \(\lambda_{\mathrm{A}} = (x - 1.08 - 0.3958\,\lambda_{\mathrm{E}})/0.6245\), then \(\lambda_{\mathrm{F}} = 1 - \lambda_{\mathrm{E}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{A}}\). GD2 measures European similarity alone; GD1 mixes European and East Asian similarity.

The reported percentages set negative weights to zero and rescale the rest to sum to 100:

\[ P_m = \frac{100 \max(0, \lambda_m)}{\sum_{k} \max(0, \lambda_k)} \]
\(m\), \(k\)
Each run over the three corners E, F, A.
\(P_m\)
The reported percentage for corner \(m\): the output columns Pe, Pf and Pa, between 0 and 100. The unclipped weights times 100 are RawPe, RawPf and RawPa.

Inside the triangle nothing changes. Just outside the E corner, small negative weights are set to 0 and the person is reported as 100% E. Real numbers, from GrafAnc's mean scores of the 1000 Genomes populations:4 CEU (Utah residents with Northern and Western European ancestry) has \(x = 1.4751\), \(y = 1.4385\), so \(\lambda_{\mathrm{E}} = 1.0045\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{A}} = -0.0039\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{F}} = -0.0005\), reported as 100, 0, 0. ASW (African Ancestry in Southwest US) at \(x = 1.1597\), \(y = 1.1562\) gives weights 0.167, 0.811 and 0.022 for E, F and A. GIH (Gujarati Indians in Houston) at \(x = 1.5202\), \(y = 1.2961\) gives 0.582, 0.082 and 0.336: a South Asian population is reported as a mixture of the three corners, because the triangle has no South Asian corner.

GD3: the height above the triangle

The distance from the person's point to the plane of the triangle is GD3. It is close to zero for members of the three corner populations and for people whose ancestry is a mixture of them. Two things move it.

  • Ancestry the triangle does not contain raises it. A member of a population with no corner has genotypes that are less probable under all three references than those of a mixture of E, F and A at the same place in the triangle, so all three distances are larger and the point lies above the plane. Among the 1000 Genomes means the largest GD3 is PEL's, 0.054, and GrafAnc's documentation lists PEL among the populations of its "Native American" group (603).3,4
  • Recent mixing lowers it slightly. A person whose two copies of a SNP often come from different populations is heterozygous more often than the average of those populations. The probability of a heterozygous genotype, \(2pq\), contains a factor 2 that is the same in every population, so the extra heterozygous SNPs lower all three distances by the same amount, which moves the point below the plane. The three 1000 Genomes populations with mostly African and European ancestry, ASW, ACB and PUR, have the lowest mean GD3 (−0.0125, −0.0062 and −0.0050).

GrafAnc's documentation suggests plotting GD1 against GD3 to see Latin American and Native American ancestry "and also for data QC".3

Contrast scores for finer structure

The triangle separates continents. For finer structure, GrafAnc compares the person's distances to two related reference populations, X and Y, such as the UK Biobank participants in its Irish and Italy reference groups:1

\[ \Delta d_{XY} = D_X - D_Y \] \[ \Delta d' = -1 + 2\,\frac{\Delta d_{XY} - e_X}{e_Y - e_X} \]
\(D_X\), \(D_Y\)
The person's distances to reference populations X and Y, as above.
\(\Delta d_{XY}\)
The raw contrast: positive when the person's genotypes are more probable under Y than under X.
\(e_X\), \(e_Y\)
The raw contrast expected for a typical member of X and of Y, computed on the same SNPs the person has.
\(\Delta d'\)
The rescaled contrast score reported by GrafAnc (for example EU1): about −1 for a typical member of X and +1 for a typical member of Y.

Written per SNP, the raw contrast adds \(\ln(p_Y/p_X)\) for each copy of allele 1 and \(\ln(q_Y/q_X)\) for each copy of allele 2: a log-likelihood ratio, averaged over SNPs. The rescaling puts the two reference populations at −1 and +1 for every contrast and every SNP set, so that one threshold works for everyone. A person halfway between, for example with one parent from each population, scores near 0. GRAF-pop's GD4 was an unscaled contrast of this kind, between its South Asian and Latin American references.2

From scores to AncGroupID

GrafAnc assigns a group by checking a fixed sequence of if–then rules on the scores; the first rule that matches decides.5 The authors drew the rules from reference data in UK Biobank (especially participants' country of birth), the 1000 Genomes Project and the Human Genome Diversity Project, and kept them "simple to promote generalizability and reduce the risk of overfitting".1,3 The continental part of the sequence, as written in GrafAnc's source code (Pe, Pf, Pa in percent):

1Pa > 50, Pf > 10, IC1 > 0.4 and GD3 > 0.035700 Oceania
2IC1 > 0.5 and Pf < 154xx South Asian
3Pa > 15 and Pf > 15800 multi-ancestry
4IC1 > −0.3 and Pa > 405xx East Asian
5EU1 < 1.6 and IC2 < −0.253xx European if IC3 < 3.5 − 3.1 × EU1, otherwise 202 or 203 Middle East
6EU1 > 1.6 and IC2 < 0.4201 Northern Africa if IC3 < −0.3, otherwise 202 Middle East 1
7EU1 > 2.2 and IC2 < 1.4106 Northeastern Africa
8GD1 > 1.4758602 Latin American 2 or 603 Native American if IC1 < −0.2, otherwise 308 Other Europe
9none of the above601 Latin American 1 if IC1 < 0.9 × GD1 − 1.36, otherwise one of 101–108 (Africa) by AF1–AF3

Within each continental group, further rules on the contrast scores choose the last two digits; for example 303 is GrafAnc's "Western Europe" group, which includes the 1000 Genomes CEU and GBR samples, and 301 is "Finland".3 The practical's R code keeps only the first digit, with AncGroupID %/% 100: 1 AFR, 2 MEN (Middle East and North Africa), 3 EUR, 4 SAS, 5 EAS, 6 AMR, 7 OCN, 8 MIX. Every group name describes the reference people whose average scores fall in that region, and the documentation states that "there are no clear boundaries between human populations".3

Slides Slides 100–101 · Do not overinterpret ancestry proportions

The same people get different "proportions" when the number of assumed ancestral populations changes from 5 to 7 (slide 100). Slide 101 draws the lesson, citing Lawson et al.: the proportions depend on the references. Pe, Pf and Pa are the extreme case: with only three corners, every person is described as a mix of European, African and East Asian references, including people whose ancestry is mostly from elsewhere, such as the GIH and PEL samples above. The practical says the same: Pe, Pf and Pa "should not be interpreted as a complete decomposition across every GrafAnc group such as South Asian or Middle East/North Africa".6

The lab's GrafAnc. The program that the terminal runs as grafanc is an emulator written for this site, not GrafAnc itself. It follows GrafAnc 1.0.0's source code for the console messages, the matching of SNPs, the scores and the output table, but it carries a toy reference. Real GrafAnc cannot use the toy files: it looks for its own 282,424 ancestry SNPs by rsID or by genome position, and the toy SNPs have made-up IDs (tsnp and five digits) at simulated positions. Run on the toy study, it finds 1 ancestry SNP among them and stops without writing a table. Step 8 therefore works only in the lab. The emulator's reference holds the toy model's allele frequencies at the toy study's SNPs that are not A/T or C/G, for two kinds of population: the European, African and East Asian ancestral populations from which the simulation derived all its populations, which serve as the three corners, and the ? toy populations of step 7's toy_1kg. The scores mean what GrafAnc's mean, with three differences. The toy triangle is smaller than the real one, so its GD values cannot be compared with real GrafAnc plots. The 15 subcontinental scores need GrafAnc's real reference populations and are written as NA. AncGroupID is continental only: 100, 300, 400, 500, 600, or 800 for several continents. The emulator was checked against the real program, built from the source code the workshop uses: given GrafAnc's own reference, it reports the same number of SNPs, GD1–GD3, Pe, Pf and Pa as the real program for all 1,380 PennCATH people, and its console messages match (The practical · How the lab was checked).

Unique individual IDs first

GrafAnc names each output row by the individual ID, the second column of the .fam file, and ignores the family ID. In PennCATH every individual ID is 1 and the family ID is the study number, written with quotation marks as part of the ID (The data: PLINK files). Run on that file, GrafAnc prints the warning "Fam file includes at least one duplicate sample … Please create unique sample IDs for all genotypes", and every row of its output would be named 1. The practical therefore builds new IDs from both columns:

bash 
$ awk '{
    gsub("\"", "", $1);
    print "\"" $1 "\"", $2, "\"" $1 "\"", "\"" $1 "_" $2 "\""
}' ${analysis_dir}/penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.fam > ${analysis_dir}/penncath.updateIDs.txt

For each line of the .fam file the awk program removes the quotation marks from the family ID and prints four fields: the old family ID and old individual ID (with the quotes put back, so they match the file), then the new family ID (unchanged) and a new individual ID that joins family and individual ID, such as "10002_1". The tools · shell syntax runs it on the first two lines of the file and explains each part.

plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01 --update-ids ${analysis_dir}/penncath.updateIDs.txt --make-bed --out ${analysis_dir}/penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.uniqueIDs
  1. The step 1 output: after the missingness filters, before any other QC step.
  2. --update-ids FILE — rename people. Each line of FILE has four fields: old family ID, old individual ID, new family ID, new individual ID. People not listed keep their IDs. PLINK applies the renaming before --keep and --remove, and it cannot be combined with --update-parents or --update-sex in one run.7
  3. --make-bed — write the renamed fileset; only the .fam file differs from the input.
  4. --out PREFIX — the new fileset that GrafAnc reads.

PLINK reports how many people were renamed, in a line of the form --update-ids: N people updated.7 Check with head that the second column of the new .fam file now differs from person to person, and count repeated IDs with awk '{ print $2 }' FILE.fam | sort | uniq -d | wc -l, which should print 0: awk prints the second column, sort puts equal IDs next to each other, uniq -d prints each value that occurs more than once, and wc -l counts those lines.

Compare GrafAnc with the reference PCA

The practical asks: "How do the GrafAnc assignments compare with the reference-based PCA pattern from Steps 6–7?" The two methods use different SNPs and different references, so agreement is a check on both. A person in the EUR cluster of the merged PCA should have a high Pe and a 3xx group; a person between the AFR and EUR clusters should have both Pf and Pe well above zero. A disagreement points to a problem worth chasing: a sample swap between the files, a strand error in step 7 that moved people, or a person whose ancestry neither reference describes well. To compare person by person in R, join the GrafAnc table to the step 7 .eigenvec by ID (below); note that GrafAnc's Sample holds the new individual IDs, so join on the uniqueIDs fileset's IDs.

Choose a rule and act Core

The practical's choice. The practical removes nobody at step 8. Its exercise makes ancestry-based flagging optional and adds: "Do not exclude a sample solely because its GrafAnc label differs from the majority. Depending on the scientific question and analysis method, defensible options can include retaining all samples with appropriate population-structure adjustment, conducting stratified analyses, or restricting an analysis to a more genetically homogeneous subset when that restriction is scientifically justified and prespecified."6

Options, and what each uses.

  • Keep everyone and adjust. Use GrafAnc's continuous scores, or PCs, as covariates (Session 2). The GrafAnc authors suggest exactly this: "GrafAnc covariates can help address subcontinental stratification and harmonize ancestry across studies".1
  • Stratify. Run analyses, or Hardy–Weinberg tests, within groups of genetically similar people, and combine the results. The GrafAnc authors propose its 38 subcontinental groups as such strata, merged into larger groups where a stratum is too small.1
  • Restrict, if prespecified. If the protocol defines the study population by genetic similarity, write the rule as a region of the scores (for example Pe above a value fixed in advance) and report it with the GrafAnc version. The authors recommend describing cohorts "by regions or thresholds in GrafAnc space" rather than by labels.1
  • Flag for checking. A person whose GrafAnc result disagrees with the reference PCA, or with recorded information, is a candidate for a sample-identity check, not an automatic exclusion.

Labels are not identities. An AncGroupID of 303 means that the person's scores satisfy the rules that GrafAnc's authors drew around the average scores of reference groups such as CEU and GBR. It does not state where the person was born, how they identify, or their race or ethnicity, which are social categories (Lecture 0 · Population descriptors). The GrafAnc authors warn that discrete labels "can be misread as fixed biological types" and "recommend prioritizing continuous measures over hard labels", following the National Academies' report on population descriptors.1,8 When a label is needed, the practical's wording is a model: these are "algorithmic assignments relative to GrafAnc's reference populations, not direct measurements of race, ethnicity, or personal identity".6

The toy data. The toy study's genetic ancestry was simulated: ? of its ? people were drawn from the allele frequencies of toy European populations, ? from African, ? from East Asian and ? from South Asian ones, and ? are admixed, with European-like and African-like ancestors in different proportions (The practical · The toy dataset). Of the ? people left after step 1, the lab's GrafAnc puts ? (?%) in EUR, ? in AFR, ? in EAS, ? in SAS and ? in AMR. Everyone who is not admixed lands in the group of the populations they were drawn from. The admixed people are split by their African-like share: those with the largest shares join the ? African-like people in AFR, and the ? with the smallest shares get AMR, because their scores are closest to those of the toy AMR populations, which the simulation built as mixtures that include European and African ancestors. The South-Asian-like people sit between the E and A corners. At the slider's default of 80%, the Pe flag catches exactly the people in AFR, EAS, SAS and AMR. Raise it to 90% and European-like people join them: with a few thousand ancestry SNPs instead of hundreds of thousands, their Pe scatters several points below 100. Where a flag line falls is a choice that depends on the data, which is why the practical leaves flagging optional.

Pitfalls Common mistakes in step 8
  • Duplicate individual IDs. GrafAnc uses only the individual ID; with PennCATH's IDs it warns and the output rows cannot be told apart. Run --update-ids first, and keep the mapping file to join results back.
  • Reading Pe, Pf and Pa as a complete ancestry breakdown. They measure position relative to three corners only. South Asian, Middle Eastern, Indigenous American and many other ancestries are reported as mixtures of E, F and A.
  • Using a group code as an identity or as a reason to exclude. The code summarizes scores relative to GrafAnc's references; the practical warns against excluding people solely because of it.
  • Too few ancestry SNPs. A small array, or heavy filtering before GrafAnc, can leave people below 10,000 ancestry SNPs, where the scores become noisy (their standard deviation grows as one over the square root of the number of SNPs).
  • Forgetting GRAFPATH or the decompression step. GrafAnc then stops with "didn't find file AncSnpPopAFs.txt".
  • Comparing numbers across GrafAnc versions. Thresholds and reference sets can change; report the version with the results.
Key points
  • GrafAnc scores each person on their own against stored allele frequencies of 26 reference populations at up to 282,424 ancestry SNPs; no merge, pruning or strand repair is needed.
  • The distance to a population is the average of −ln P(genotype) over SNPs; three distances, to European, African and East Asian references, place the person relative to a triangle.
  • Barycentric coordinates of that position give RawPe, RawPf, RawPa; clipping negative values and rescaling gives Pe, Pf, Pa. GD1 and GD2 are the position on a fixed triangle, GD3 the height above it.
  • Fifteen contrast scores, rescaled so that two reference populations sit at −1 and +1, separate finer groups, and fixed if–then rules on all scores give AncGroupID.
  • GrafAnc needs unique individual IDs. Its groups are algorithmic, reference-relative summaries of continuous scores, not race, ethnicity or identity, and the practical excludes nobody because of them.

Check your understanding

Run GrafAnc in the terminal on toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 without updating the IDs. What happens, and why does the practical run --update-ids first?

Run ${analysis_dir}/grafanc/bin/grafanc ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 ${analysis_dir}/test.txt and look for the duplicate-sample warning, then cut -f1 ${analysis_dir}/test.txt | sort | uniq -c to see repeated names. GrafAnc names each person by the individual ID alone; when those repeat, rows cannot be matched back to people. In the lab every toy individual ID is 1, as in PennCATH: the warning reads "Fam file includes at least one duplicate sample: 1.", and uniq -c counts ? rows named 1, plus the header line. --update-ids makes the individual IDs unique by joining family and individual ID.

A SNP has allele 1 frequency 0.9 in population E and 0.2 in population A. A person has genotype 0. How much does this SNP add to D_E and to D_A?

Genotype 0 has probability \(q^2\): \(0.1^2 = 0.01\) in E and \(0.8^2 = 0.64\) in A. The SNP adds \(-\ln 0.01 = 4.61\) to the sum for \(D_{\mathrm{E}}\) and \(-\ln 0.64 = 0.45\) to the sum for \(D_{\mathrm{A}}\), each divided by the number of SNPs \(S\). The genotype is much more probable in A, so it pulls the person toward A.

GrafAnc reports RawPe = 103.2, RawPf = −1.5, RawPa = −1.7. What are Pe, Pf and Pa, and where is the person on the GD1–GD2 plane?

Negative weights are set to 0, and the rest is rescaled to 100: Pe = 100, Pf = 0, Pa = 0. The weights sum to 100 and the E weight exceeds 100, so the person lies just outside the triangle beyond the E corner, which is where people genetically very similar to the European reference fall when sampling noise pushes them past the corner.

Why does a person with Gujarati ancestry get Pe ≈ 58%, Pf ≈ 8%, Pa ≈ 34%? Does that mean 8% African ancestry?

The triangle has only European, African and East Asian corners, so every person is placed as a weighted combination of those three. The GIH mean scores land inside the triangle at those weights (computed above from GD1 = 1.5202, GD2 = 1.2961), but the weights describe a position relative to the three references, not a history of mixing. GrafAnc's South Asian group is assigned by a separate rule (IC1 > 0.5 and Pf < 15), and Lawson et al. (slide 101) warn against reading such proportions literally.

In the toy output, find the people whose continental group is not EUR, and their Pe. Use the shell.

The first digit of AncGroupID is the continental code, and 3 is EUR. awk 'NR == 1 { for (i = 1; i <= NF; i++) col[$i] = i; next } int($col["AncGroupID"] / 100) != 3 { print $col["Sample"], $col["Pe"], $col["AncGroupID"] }' ${analysis_dir}/toy_grafanc_out.txt reads the header line first and stores each column's position under its name in the array col, so $col["Pe"] is the Pe column wherever it is. For every later row whose group divided by 100, rounded down by int, is not 3, it prints the sample, Pe and AncGroupID. In the lab it lists ? people in AFR, ? in EAS, ? in SAS and ? in AMR. Compare the list with the toy people outside the EUR cluster in step 7's PCA.

What does a contrast score EU1 = −0.9 say, and what does EU1 = 0 say?

EU1 contrasts the UK Biobank Irish reference group (X, at −1) with the UK Biobank Italy group (Y, at +1). −0.9 is close to −1, the score of a typical member of X: on this contrast the person resembles the Irish reference far more than the Italian one. 0 is halfway: for example a person with one parent from each population, or a person from a population that lies between them on this contrast. The score alone cannot tell these apart, which is why GrafAnc combines several scores.

Why is GrafAnc's result for a person the same whether they are analyzed alone or with 10,000 others, while their PC scores in step 7 are not?

GrafAnc's distances use only the person's genotypes and fixed reference allele frequencies. PCA computes its axes from the relationship matrix of everyone in the file, so adding or removing people changes the axes and every person's scores.
