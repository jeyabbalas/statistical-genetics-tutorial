QC step 6: principal component analysis

Do some people differ from the rest of the sample in their genome-wide pattern of genotypes? PLINK's --pca finds the directions along which people's genotypes at the LD-pruned, common SNPs vary most, the principal components (PCs), and gives each person a score on each of them. Plots of the first PCs show groups of genetically similar people, gradients between them, and people far from everyone else. Such patterns reflect genetic ancestry, but also relatives, regions of strong LD and technical differences between batches of samples. This page runs the practical's command, reads its two output files, shows why the SNPs are pruned first, and explains what clusters and outliers do and do not mean. How PCA works is explained on Population structure · PCA.

Command: plink --pca Writes: .eigenvec and .eigenval ≈ 40 min
Mental model

Each person becomes a point on a few axes of genetic variation; the axes describe this sample, not people in general

PLINK standardizes everyone's genotypes at the pruned, common SNPs, measures how similar every pair of people is, and finds the few directions that capture most of that similarity: the principal components. Each person gets a score on each PC (.eigenvec); each PC gets an eigenvalue, the amount of variation it captures (.eigenval). A plot of PC1 against PC2 shows the main structure, and the scree plot shows how many PCs carry it. Clusters, gradients and outliers are then explained with reference samples (steps 7 and 8), relatedness (step 4) and sample quality (steps 1 and 5), and the top PCs are kept as covariates for the association analysis of Session 2.

  • PCs summarize the people analyzed. Who is in the data sets the axes; adding or removing people moves everyone (what clusters mean).
  • Prune first. A region of strong LD can take over a component on its own (pruning).
  • A cluster is not a race. It is a group of people with similar genotypes at these SNPs; any label comes from reference samples, not from the plot (what clusters mean).
  • Outliers need an explanation. A person far from everyone may differ in genetic ancestry from the rest of the sample, or may be a poor sample (outliers).
  • The PCs are used again. The top PCs become covariates that adjust association tests for ancestry (covariates).

What the step finds Core

People who share more recent ancestry share more allele copies, so their genotypes agree more often, across the whole genome. PCA looks for directions in which many SNPs vary together across people. What lies behind such a direction can be one of several things:

Why it matters. Allele frequencies differ between populations, and so can the share of cases, because disease risk, recruitment and diagnosis differ between groups. A SNP whose frequency differs between the groups then appears associated with the disease because of the grouping, not because of the SNP: confounding by ancestry, called population stratification. Including the top PCs as covariates in the association model adjusts for it, which is why the PCs are computed before the association analysis of Session 2.1,2 For quality control itself, PCA shows samples unlike everyone else and technical structure, such as a batch that forms its own cluster. One quality-control review also tested, within each population subgroup, whether case–control status was associated with the first two PCs, a direct check for this kind of confounding.3

What --pca computes Core

--pca works on the fileset left after --extract and --maf: \(n\) people and \(m\) SNPs. The derivation, with a worked example, is on Population structure · Applied to genotypes; in brief, PLINK first standardizes the genotypes and computes a relationship matrix:2,4

\[ \begin{gathered} z_{ij} = \frac{g_{ij} - 2\hat{p}_j}{\sqrt{2\hat{p}_j(1 - \hat{p}_j)}} \\[6pt] \mathbf{K} = \tfrac{1}{m}\, \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top} \end{gathered} \]
\(g_{ij}\)
Genotype of person \(i\) at SNP \(j\): copies of allele 1, 0, 1 or 2.
\(\hat{p}_j\)
Frequency of allele 1 at SNP \(j\) among the people analyzed.
\(z_{ij}\)
Standardized genotype, the entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\) of the \(n \times m\) matrix \(\mathbf{Z}\).
\(m\)
Number of SNPs: here the pruned autosomal SNPs with a minor allele frequency of at least 5%.
\(\mathbf{K}\)
The \(n \times n\) relationship matrix; PLINK calls it the variance-standardized relationship matrix.

Each SNP is centered, so that its average over people is 0, and scaled, so that its variance is about 1 whatever its allele frequency. Entry \((i, i')\) of \(\mathbf{K}\) is the average over SNPs of \(z_{ij} z_{i'j}\): large and positive for two people whose genotypes agree at many SNPs more than the allele frequencies predict, near 0 for two unrelated people from one population, and near 1 on the diagonal.

PLINK changes one thing: it averages each entry over the SNPs at which it can be computed, so that a missing call is skipped rather than counted. An off-diagonal entry is averaged over the SNPs called in both people, and a diagonal entry over the SNPs called in that person. The diagonal gets no other special treatment: each diagonal entry is the person's average of \(z_{ij}^2\), close to 1 for people whose parents are not related. PLINK's documentation writes it as 1 plus \(\hat{F}_1\), the first of the three estimates of a person's inbreeding coefficient that PLINK's --ibc computes.13 The principal components are the eigenvectors of \(\mathbf{K}\):

\[ \mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{u}_k = \ell_k\,\mathbf{u}_k, \qquad \text{share of variance of PC}\,k \approx \frac{\ell_k}{\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{K}} \approx \frac{\ell_k}{n} \]
\(\mathbf{u}_k\)
The \(k\)-th eigenvector: \(n\) numbers, one per person, whose squares add up to 1. Column \(k + 2\) of the .eigenvec file.
\(\ell_k\)
Its eigenvalue, line \(k\) of the .eigenval file; \(\ell_1 \ge \ell_2 \ge \dots\)
\(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{K}\)
The trace of \(\mathbf{K}\), the sum of its diagonal entries; close to \(n\) because each diagonal entry is close to 1.
\(n\)
Number of people.

Among all ways of giving each person one number, with the squares adding up to 1, \(\mathbf{u}_1\) is the one that agrees best with the relationship matrix: the quantity \(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{u}\) is largest for \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_1\), and its largest value is \(\ell_1\). \(\mathbf{u}_2\) is the best among the ways perpendicular to \(\mathbf{u}_1\), and so on. All the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{K}\) add up to its trace, so \(\ell_k / n\) is approximately the share of all the variation among people that PC \(k\) captures. The 20 eigenvalues PLINK reports are only part of the trace; dividing by their sum inflates every share.

Three properties of these numbers matter whenever PCs are plotted or compared:

  • Scale. Every column of .eigenvec has length 1, so with \(n\) people a typical value is of the order of \(1/\sqrt{n}\), whatever the PC. How much a PC matters is in its eigenvalue, not in the spread of its column. Multiplying column \(k\) by \(\sqrt{\ell_k}\) restores the relative sizes.
  • Sign. If \(\mathbf{u}_k\) is an eigenvector, so is \(-\mathbf{u}_k\). Two runs, or two programs, can give mirror images of the same plot, so compare PCs only up to a change of sign.
  • Origin. Every column of \(\mathbf{Z}\) has mean 0, so the scores of all people on a PC add up to 0 (approximately, because PLINK averages each entry over the SNPs called in both people): the origin is the average person of this sample, and the largest group sits nearest to it. Adding or removing people moves everyone's coordinates (Population structure · Centering).

The .eigenvec and .eigenval files

.eigenvec
One line per person, separated by spaces, with no header line (PLINK 1.9 adds one only with the modifier header). Columns: FID, IID, then PC1 to PC20. Read into R with fread, the columns are named V1, V2, V3, …, so PC1 is V3 and PC2 is V4; that is why the practical writes rename(PC1 = V3, PC2 = V4).2,5
.eigenval
20 lines: the eigenvalues \(\ell_1, \ldots, \ell_{20}\), largest first.
Not in the files
The share of variance (compute \(\ell_k / n\)); the SNP weights of each PC (the modifier var-wts writes them; the lab's PLINK does not support it, and the widget in Pruning uses correlations, which are approximately proportional to the weights).

  2. --bfile PREFIX: the fileset left after step 1's missingness filters.
  3. --extract FILE: only the SNPs in step 3's pruned list, so that no region of strong LD contributes many SNPs carrying the same information (pruning).

  6. --out PREFIX: writes PREFIX.eigenvec, PREFIX.eigenval and PREFIX.log; the same prefix as the .genome and .het files.

The warning about monomorphic variants. PLINK 1.9.0 prints "Warning: Monomorphic variant(s) present; this implies GRM-destabilizing very rare variants are also present. You should almost certainly apply a MAF filter and rerun this calculation, instead of using results from the current run." A monomorphic SNP, one at which everyone in the sample carries the same allele, has \(\hat{p}_j = 0\) or 1, so the formula for \(z_{ij}\) would divide by 0; PLINK lets such a SNP add 0 to the sum behind every entry. The warning's real concern is SNPs that are rare without being monomorphic: their \(\sqrt{2\hat{p}_j(1 - \hat{p}_j)}\) is small, so the few people who carry the rare allele get very large values of \(z_{ij}\), and a handful of such SNPs can dominate \(\mathbf{K}\). Here --maf 0.05 has already removed every SNP with a minor allele frequency below 5%, so no SNP is monomorphic or very rare, and the results can be used. The warning appears anyway because of how PLINK 1.9 builds the matrix: it processes SNPs 20 at a time and fills an incomplete last group with placeholder entries of frequency 0, which its check counts as monomorphic SNPs. So it warns whenever the number of SNPs is not a multiple of 20, as with the toy data's ? SNPs, PennCATH's 112,669 and step 7's merged sets. The lab's PLINK warns under the same rule. PLINK 1.9 versions released before 23 September 2026 do not print the warning at all.13

Why the SNPs are pruned first Core

PCA treats every SNP as a separate column and knows nothing about where the SNPs lie. Hundreds of SNPs in one region of strong LD carry nearly the same information, so they act like one SNP counted hundreds of times, and a component can end up describing that single region. The practical puts it this way: "regions of strong or long-range LD can disproportionately influence individual components".5 A quality-control review gives a real example. In a lung cancer study of people of European ancestry, PCA on all 545,000 autosomal SNPs with a missing-call rate below 5% separated the US and Italian participants on the first two PCs, while the third PC split both into three groups. The groups matched people's genotypes at a cluster of highly correlated SNPs in 8p23, a region that contains a polymorphic inversion: a stretch of chromosome 8 that is reversed on some copies of the chromosome and not on others. PCA of other European samples had shown the same. The authors therefore recommend against adjusting for PCs that are highly correlated with such local SNP clusters, which could even hide true associations in those regions.3,6 Lists of long-range LD regions to remove before PCA exist,7 and Privé and colleagues detect such regions automatically from peaks of the SNP weights; they found that PC19 to PC40 of UK Biobank capture LD structure rather than population structure.8 Pruning (step 3) removes most of this redundancy. The widget runs the toy data's PCA with and without it.

The right panel gives, for every SNP, the squared correlation \(r^2\) between its genotypes and the people's scores on the chosen PC; the correlation is approximately proportional to the SNP's weight on the component, which real PLINK writes with var-wts. A component that reflects genome-wide ancestry gets small \(r^2\) from SNPs everywhere. A component made by one region of strong LD gets large \(r^2\) from that region, and its scatter plot sorts people into three groups: those carrying zero, one or two copies of one of the region's haplotypes. The readout says how much of the component's summed \(r^2\) the region carries. Components are perpendicular by construction, but the signals behind them need not be: when the region's signal and an ancestry signal are of similar size, two components can each carry part of both, and the three groups then run diagonally across the plot of the two. Switch to the pruned run to see whether the region's signal has gone.

What clusters mean, and what they do not Core

Slides Slides 104–105 · PCs summarize the people in the data

Slide 104 explains why the PCA of Europeans on slide 103 looks like a map. Its authors used a person only "if all observed grandparents originated from a single country" and excluded everyone else, which removes the people who would fall between countries; the slide calls the picture "(by design) too pretty".6 Slide 105 shows the opposite sampling. In a figure from a New York City biobank, reproduced by Lewis and colleagues, reference people from 87 populations form colored islands, and 31,705 biobank participants fill the space between and around them; the caption calls the islands "a by-product of sampling strategy".9,10

The slide's reminder, "PCs are just a summary of the data! Who is in the data? Who is not in the data?", applies to step 6 directly: the PCs of PennCATH, or of the toy data, describe the people in that file as the study recruited them. Clusters and gaps depend on who was recruited, and a person between clusters is not an error. Population structure · What PCs depend on decodes both slides in more detail.

The practical opens step 6 with the same caution: "PCA does not measure ancestry directly: the resulting components can reflect genetic ancestry and demographic history, but they can also be affected by technical artifacts."5 Three points follow from how PCA works.

  • The axes come from this sample. Each PC is a direction of variation among the people analyzed, and the origin is their average. A group that is a small share of the sample may not get a component of its own, and the same person can sit in a cluster in one study and between clusters in another.
  • A cluster is a group of people with similar genotypes at these SNPs, not a race, an ethnicity or a nationality. The practical's terminology note: genetic ancestry "refers to patterns of genetic similarity inferred relative to reference samples or statistical models. It is not interchangeable with self-identified race, ethnicity, nationality, or personal identity."5 Lecture 0 explains the distinction in Words matter, following the National Academies' 2023 report on population descriptors.11 A study-only PCA has no labels at all: names for its clusters come from comparison with reference samples (step 7) or from a reference-based tool (step 8).
  • A cluster can be technical. Samples genotyped in different batches, on different plates or arrays, or with DNA of different quality can separate on a PC. Coloring the plot by batch or plate, when they are recorded, tests this directly.

PennCATH. Its 1,401 people were drawn from consecutive patients undergoing cardiac catheterization at one university hospital, and all were recorded as white (The data). Its study-only PCA shows the variation among these people; it cannot show how they relate to other populations. Step 7 adds that by merging the data with 1000 Genomes, and the practical reports there that the PennCATH people "lie predominantly within or very close to the cluster formed by 1KG samples carrying the EUR reference label", a statement about genetic similarity to a reference, "not a categorical determination that every participant is 'European'".5

Outliers: flag, explain, then decide Core

An outlier is a person far from everyone else on at least one of the top PCs. PLINK's documentation calls its --pca "a simple port of GCTA's --pca flag" and points to EIGENSOFT for "automatic outlier removal".2 EIGENSOFT's smartpca by default removes anyone more than 6 standard deviations from the mean along any of the top 10 PCs and repeats the PCA, up to 5 times.12 The widget above applies one pass of this rule to PLINK's PCs. Privé and colleagues instead use each person's distances to their nearest neighbors on the PCs, which flags people in sparsely populated parts of the plot, not only people far from the mean.8

What to do with a flagged person depends on why they are far out:

  • A poor sample. A high missing-call rate (step 1), relatedness to many people (step 4) or F far from 0 (step 5) points to DNA problems. Remove the sample.
  • A relative. A family can form its own component. Compute the PCs on unrelated people (one of each close pair from step 4) and project the relatives onto them (Population structure · LD and relatives).
  • A person whose genetic ancestry differs from most of the sample. This is not an error. The practical's exercise says not to exclude a sample "solely because its GrafAnc label differs from the majority" and lists defensible options: "retaining all samples with appropriate population-structure adjustment, conducting stratified analyses, or restricting an analysis to a more genetically homogeneous subset when that restriction is scientifically justified and prespecified".5 Removing people for their ancestry also narrows whom genetic research serves (Lecture 0 · Diversity in genetic studies).

In the toy data. The three actions match the people the widget's outlier rule flags. The duplicate pair (?) has ordinary missingness and F, and a PI_HAT near 1 in step 4. Step 4's decision removes one copy, and their component goes with it: a PCA run without one copy has no component that singles out the other. The two East-Asian-like people have ordinary missingness, no close relatives and slightly positive F, the pattern that genetic ancestry gives (step 5). They are correct samples at the edge of the sample's ancestry range: keep them, and let the PCs describe them.

Later: PCs as covariates Core

The association analysis of Session 2 includes the top PCs as covariates in each SNP's regression, so that the comparison between genotypes and disease is made among people with the same PC scores (Testing association · Principal components as covariates).1,2 Three choices made in this step carry over:

  • How many PCs. There is no single rule: the scree plot, the Tracy–Widom test, plots of each PC and the purpose are used together (Population structure · How many PCs?).
  • Which people define them. Unrelated people, with relatives projected onto their PCs, so that no family makes a component.
  • Which SNPs. Pruned SNPs, with long-range LD regions removed: a PC made by one region would adjust every association test for that region's genotypes.

In R Core

The practical reads the .eigenvec file and plots PC1 against PC2 and PC2 against PC3.5 The code below does the same, adds the scree plot with approximate shares of variance, colors the people by the phenotype code of the .fam file, and lists people more than 6 SD from the mean on any of the first 10 PCs. library(tidyverse)
library(data.table)

analysis_dir <- Sys.getenv("analysis_dir")
pca <- fread(file.path(analysis_dir, "penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.LD0.3.eigenvec"))
eigenval <- scan(file.path(analysis_dir, "penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.LD0.3.eigenval"))

pca %>% rename(PC1 = V3, PC2 = V4) %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = PC1, y = PC2)) +
  geom_point() +
  labs(title = "PC1 vs. PC2") +
  theme_classic()

pca %>% rename(PC2 = V4, PC3 = V5) %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = PC2, y = PC3)) +
  geom_point() +
  labs(title = "PC2 vs. PC3") +
  theme_classic()

# Scree plot; eigenvalue / number of people approximates the share of variance
tibble(PC = seq_along(eigenval), eigenvalue = eigenval, share = eigenval / nrow(pca)) %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = PC, y = eigenvalue)) +
  geom_line() + geom_point() +
  labs(x = "Principal component", y = "Eigenvalue") +
  theme_classic()

# Color by the phenotype code in column 6 of the .fam file
fam <- fread(file.path(analysis_dir, "penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.fam"))
pca %>%
  inner_join(select(fam, V1, V2, pheno = V6), by = c("V1", "V2")) %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = V3, y = V4, color = factor(pheno))) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.6) +
  coord_equal() +
  labs(x = "PC1", y = "PC2", color = "Phenotype code") +
  theme_classic()

# More than 6 SD from the mean on any of PC1-PC10 (one pass)
pca %>%
  mutate(across(V3:V12, ~ (.x - mean(.x)) / sd(.x))) %>%
  filter(if_any(V3:V12, ~ abs(.x) > 6)) %>%
  select(FID = V1, IID = V2)

  • fread() on a file without a header names the columns V1, V2, …; pandas would need header=None. scan() reads a file of numbers into a vector, like np.loadtxt.
  • rename(PC1 = V3) gives column V3 the name PC1 (pandas df.rename(columns={"V3": "PC1"})).
  • tibble() builds a small table from vectors, like pd.DataFrame({...}); seq_along(x) is 1, 2, …, up to the length of x.
  • inner_join(x, y, by = …) keeps people found in both tables (pd.merge(..., how="inner")); factor(pheno) makes ggplot2 color by category rather than on a continuous scale.
  • mutate(across(V3:V12, ~ …)) applies the same formula to columns V3 to V12; if_any() keeps a row if the condition holds in any of them (in pandas, (z.abs() > 6).any(axis=1)).
  • coord_equal() draws both axes on the same scale, so that distances on the plot are not distorted.

Pitfalls Core

Pitfall Reading clusters as races or ethnic groups

A PC plot shows genetic similarity among the people analyzed. Labels such as "European" come from reference samples and describe similarity to those samples; they are not identities, and race and ethnicity are not read off a PC plot.

Pitfall Comparing PCs across runs as if they were fixed

Signs can flip, the order of PCs with similar eigenvalues can swap, and adding or removing people moves the origin. Compare runs by the pattern, allowing for sign changes, not by the values.

Pitfall Shares of variance from the 20 reported eigenvalues

The eigenvalues in .eigenval are the 20 largest of \(n\). Divide by \(n\) (the trace of \(\mathbf{K}\), approximately), not by the sum of the 20, which makes every share look larger.

Pitfall PCs from unpruned SNPs or with relatives included

A region of strong LD or a large family can make a component of its own, and using that component as a covariate adjusts the association tests for something other than ancestry.

Key points
  • --pca standardizes the genotypes of the pruned SNPs with minor allele frequency of at least 5%, builds the relationship matrix \(\mathbf{K}\), and writes its top 20 eigenvectors (.eigenvec, one line per person, no header) and eigenvalues (.eigenval).
  • Each PC column has length 1; its importance is in its eigenvalue, and \(\ell_k / n\) approximates its share of the variance. Signs are arbitrary and the origin is the sample's average person.
  • Clusters, gradients and outliers can reflect genetic ancestry, relatives, regions of strong LD or technical batches; pruning removes the LD effect, and steps 1, 4, 5, 7 and 8 explain the rest.
  • PCs describe the people analyzed. A cluster is a group of genetically similar people, not a race or an ethnicity; labels come from reference samples.
  • Flag outliers, explain each one, and decide by the analysis plan; the top PCs, computed on unrelated people from pruned SNPs, become covariates in Session 2.

Check your understanding

Why does the practical run --pca on pruned SNPs with --maf 0.05, rather than on all SNPs?

Pruning removes SNPs in strong LD with each other. Without it, a region of strong LD contributes many SNPs that carry the same information, and a component can describe that region (people's genotypes at an inversion, for example) instead of genome-wide ancestry. The MAF filter removes low-frequency variants, which the practical says "can generate sample-specific structure": a rare allele carried by a few people makes those few people look alike at that SNP, which says little about broad population structure.

In a study of 1,380 people, the first eigenvalue is 12.4 and the 20 eigenvalues add up to 60. What share of the variance does PC1 capture?

About 12.4 / 1,380 ≈ 0.9%, because the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{K}\) add up to its trace, which is close to the number of people. Dividing by the sum of the 20 reported eigenvalues would give 12.4 / 60 ≈ 21%, a large overstatement: the other 1,360 eigenvalues are left out.

You rerun the PCA after removing three people, and PC1 now points the other way: the people who were on the right are on the left. Is something wrong?

No. If \(\mathbf{u}\) is an eigenvector, so is \(-\mathbf{u}\), so the sign of each PC is arbitrary. Removing people also changes the sample's average and therefore moves the origin and everyone's coordinates a little. Compare the pattern, allowing for a change of sign.

In the unpruned PCA, PC2 splits people into three horizontal stripes, and almost all SNPs correlated with PC2 lie in a 4 Mb region of chromosome 8. What is going on, and what do you do?

PC2 is describing one region, not ancestry: most likely an inversion or another region of strong LD, where the three stripes are people carrying zero, one or two copies of one of its haplotypes. Use the pruned SNPs, or exclude the region before the PCA, and check that the component disappears. Using such a PC as a covariate would adjust every association test for the genotypes in that region, and could hide a true association there.

A study has 950 people of one genetic ancestry and 50 of another. On PC1, the large group sits near 0 and the small group far from it. Why?

The scores on every PC add up to approximately 0 over the sample, because each SNP column was centered. With the groups at their means, \(950\,\bar{x}_1 + 50\,\bar{x}_2 \approx 0\), so \(\bar{x}_1 \approx -\bar{x}_2/19\): the large group's mean is 19 times closer to 0. The origin is the average person of this sample, not a fixed point.

A colleague labels the clusters on a study-only PC plot "White", "Black" and "Asian". What is wrong with that, and what can be said instead?

A study-only PCA has no labels: it shows groups of people with similar genotypes at these SNPs, nothing about how they identify or are identified. Race and ethnicity are social categories, not properties read off genotypes. What can be said comes from comparison with reference samples (step 7) or a reference-based tool (step 8), for example that a cluster is genetically most similar to the 1000 Genomes samples labeled EUR, together with the caveat that this is a statement about genetic similarity relative to that reference.
