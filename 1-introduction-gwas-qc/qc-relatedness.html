QC step 4: relatedness and identity by descent

Is anyone in the study twice, and are any two people close relatives? Two people share a stretch of DNA identical by descent (IBD) when both inherited it from the same recent ancestor. PLINK's --genome compares every pair of people at the LD-pruned SNPs from step 3, counts the SNPs at which their alleles match, and turns those counts into estimates of how much of their genomes the two share IBD: Z0, Z1, Z2 and their summary PI_HAT. This page explains that estimate, what duplicates, relatives, contaminated DNA and genetic ancestry do to it, and how to decide whom to remove.

Command: plink --genome Writes: .genome, one row per pair of people ≈ 50 min
Mental model

Relatedness is a statistic per pair: count shared alleles, estimate shared descent, flag the pairs that are too close

For every pair of people, PLINK counts the SNPs at which the two share 0, 1 or 2 alleles, compares those counts with what the allele frequencies predict for each level of sharing by descent, and estimates the fractions of the genome they share IBD (Z0, Z1, Z2) and their summary PI_HAT. Pairs far from the cloud of unrelated pairs are duplicates or relatives: one member of each is removed, or the relatedness is modeled later.

  • Pairs, not people. Duplicates, relatives and contaminated DNA make rows of the genotype matrix depend on each other; this is the one QC step that compares people two at a time (what the step finds).
  • Sharing by state is counted; sharing by descent is estimated. PLINK sees only matching alleles. The method of moments converts the counts into Z0, Z1, Z2 and PI_HAT (the statistic).
  • Every relationship has an address. In the plane of Z0 against Z1, duplicates, parent–child pairs, siblings and second- and third-degree relatives sit at known points, and unrelated pairs pile up at Z0 = 1 (expected values, the plot).
  • Ancestry and contamination imitate relatedness. Both raise PI_HAT for pairs that are not family (when PI_HAT misleads).
  • A threshold picks pairs; you pick people. A cut-off such as PI_HAT > 0.1875 flags pairs, and a rule (more missing calls, missing phenotype) chooses which member of each pair goes (threshold and action).

What the step finds Core

Why relatives matter for the association tests later. A GWAS regression treats each person as an independent observation. Relatives share alleles IBD and often share households; if a trait runs in families, a SNP that relatives happen to share can look associated with it, so test statistics are inflated and false positives increase. In well-designed studies of outbred populations the effect of a few unknown relatives is small, but it becomes serious when the sampling favors relatives or in founder populations that grew from a small number of people.2 Duplicates count one person twice, which makes estimates look more precise than they are.

Why it matters for quality control itself. A pair of samples with nearly identical genotypes but different study IDs is evidence of a sample mix-up unless the records show one person sampled twice or identical twins. In a lung cancer GWAS whose subjects were all expected to be unrelated, the relatedness analysis found 14 pairs of full siblings, 5 of half siblings, one parent–offspring pair and 36 duplicate pairs, including a pair of identical twins; two duplicate pairs could not be documented as coming from the same person and were removed.3 Relatives also affect later steps: a large family can form its own principal component in step 6, so principal components are often computed on unrelated people only. And a replication cohort, or the target sample of a polygenic score, must not contain the same people or their relatives as the discovery GWAS.4

The statistic: from shared alleles to Z0, Z1, Z2 and PI_HAT Core

PLINK cannot see which stretches of DNA two people inherited from a common ancestor. It sees their genotypes, and it reasons in four steps, which the subsections below follow: (1) count the SNPs at which the pair shares 0, 1 or 2 alleles by state; (2) work out, from each SNP's allele frequency, how often each count should occur if the pair shares 0, 1 or 2 copies IBD there; (3) choose the IBD fractions that make the expected counts equal the observed ones (the method of moments); (4) summarize the fractions in one number, PI_HAT. Only autosomal SNPs are used, and for each pair only the SNPs where both people have a genotype call.5,6

1 · Count the SNPs at which the pair shares 0, 1 or 2 alleles

Two alleles are identical by state (IBS) when they are the same base, whatever their origin. At a SNP each person carries two alleles, so a pair can share 0, 1 or 2 of them by state. With genotypes coded as the number of copies of allele 1 (0, 1 or 2), the count has a one-line formula:

\[ s_j = 2 - \lvert g_{\mathrm{A}j} - g_{\mathrm{B}j} \rvert \]
\(j\)
A SNP, \(1, \ldots, m\), counting only the autosomal SNPs at which both people have a genotype call.
\(g_{\mathrm{A}j}, g_{\mathrm{B}j}\)
Genotypes of person A and person B at SNP \(j\): the number of copies of allele 1, 0, 1 or 2.
\(s_j\)
Number of alleles the pair shares IBS at SNP \(j\): 0, 1 or 2.

Equal genotypes share both alleles (\(s_j = 2\)), including two heterozygotes, who both carry one copy of each allele. Genotypes that differ by one copy share one allele. Opposite homozygotes, 0 and 2, share none: \(s_j = 0\) is the only case in which the two people have no allele in common. Counting over SNPs gives \(m_0\), \(m_1\) and \(m_2\), the numbers of SNPs with \(s_j = 0\), 1 and 2, with \(m_0 + m_1 + m_2 = m\). PLINK writes them as IBS0, IBS1 and IBS2 when you ask for --genome full.

2 · What each level of sharing by descent predicts

Take one SNP at which the pair shares \(k\) copies IBD, where \(k\) is 0, 1 or 2. A copy shared IBD carries the same allele in both people. Every copy that is not shared is an independent draw from the population, carrying allele 1 with probability \(p\), the SNP's allele frequency. That is enough to compute the chance of each IBS count:

  • 2 copies IBD: both people have the same two copies, so the genotypes are equal and \(s_j = 2\) with probability 1 (apart from genotyping errors).
  • 1 copy IBD: each person's other copy is an independent draw. If the two draws are the same allele (probability \(p^2 + q^2\)) the genotypes are equal and \(s_j = 2\); otherwise (probability \(2pq\)) they differ by one copy and \(s_j = 1\). Sharing nothing by state is impossible.
  • 0 copies IBD: all four copies are independent draws, as for two unrelated people.
\[ \begin{aligned} e_{00} &= 2p^2q^2 \\ e_{01} &= 4pq\,(p^2 + q^2) \\ e_{02} &= p^4 + q^4 + 4p^2q^2 \\ e_{11} &= 2pq \\ e_{12} &= p^2 + q^2 \end{aligned} \]
\(e_{ki}\)
Probability that a pair sharing \(k\) copies IBD at a SNP shares \(i\) alleles IBS there. The first index is the IBD count (0, 1 or 2), the second the IBS count (0, 1 or 2).
\(p, q\)
Frequencies of allele 1 and allele 2 at the SNP, \(q = 1 - p\). PLINK estimates them from all the people in the file.
\(e_{10}, e_{20}, e_{21}\)
Not listed because they are 0: sharing a copy by descent rules out sharing fewer alleles by state. Similarly \(e_{22} = 1\).

Each line adds up the probabilities of the genotype pairs that give that outcome. \(e_{00}\): genotypes 2 and 0 (probability \(p^2 q^2\)) or 0 and 2 (\(q^2 p^2\)). \(e_{02}\): both 2 (\(p^4\)), both 0 (\(q^4\)) or both 1 (\((2pq)^2 = 4p^2q^2\)). \(e_{01}\) is what is left, \(1 - e_{00} - e_{02}\). With \(p = 0.5\): \(e_{00} = 0.125\), \(e_{01} = 0.5\), \(e_{02} = 0.375\), so unrelated people are opposite homozygotes at one such SNP in eight. With \(p = 0.1\): \(e_{00} = 0.016\) and \(e_{02} = 0.689\). At a SNP with a rare allele, unrelated people usually match by state, so a match says little, while an opposite homozygote is rare and very informative. PLINK computes each \(e_{ki}\) at every SNP and averages it over the SNPs; it also corrects for having estimated \(p\) from a finite number of people (it treats the alleles as drawn without replacement from the sample), which matters only in small samples.6,7

3 · Solve for the IBD fractions: the method of moments

Refresher: the method of moments. To estimate unknown parameters by the method of moments, write the expected values of statistics you can observe as functions of the parameters, set each expected value equal to its observed value, and solve. The allele frequency estimate of Lecture 0 is an example: among the \(2n\) allele copies of \(n\) people, the expected number of allele-1 copies is \(2np\); setting this equal to the observed count \(2n_{11} + n_{12}\) and solving gives \(\hat{p} = (2n_{11} + n_{12})/(2n)\).

Here the unknown parameters are the pair's IBD fractions \(k_0, k_1, k_2\), and the observed statistics are the fractions of SNPs with each IBS count. A SNP lies in a stretch shared 0 copies IBD with probability \(k_0\), and then shows IBS 0 with probability \(e_{00}\); adding over the three IBD states gives the expected fractions:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{E}\!\left[\frac{m_0}{m}\right] &= k_0\, e_{00} \\ \mathrm{E}\!\left[\frac{m_1}{m}\right] &= k_0\, e_{01} + k_1\, e_{11} \\ \mathrm{E}\!\left[\frac{m_2}{m}\right] &= k_0\, e_{02} + k_1\, e_{12} + k_2 \end{aligned} \]
\(m\)
Number of autosomal SNPs at which both people have a genotype call.
\(m_0, m_1, m_2\)
Numbers of those SNPs at which the pair shares 0, 1 or 2 alleles IBS (PLINK's IBS0, IBS1, IBS2).
\(\mathrm{E}[\,\cdot\,]\)
Expected value: the average over the random draws of the copies that are not shared IBD.
\(k_0, k_1, k_2\)
Fractions of the genome at which the pair shares 0, 1 or 2 copies IBD; unknown, each between 0 and 1, with \(k_0 + k_1 + k_2 = 1\).
\(e_{ki}\)
Expected IBS fractions given IBD, from the previous block, averaged over SNPs.

IBS 0 can arise only where nothing is shared IBD, IBS 1 where 0 or 1 copy is shared, and IBS 2 anywhere, so the three lines contain one, two and three unknowns. Each line is a mixture: the share of the genome in each IBD state times the IBS rate of that state. For an unrelated pair (\(k_0 = 1\)) the lines reduce to \(e_{00}\), \(e_{01}\) and \(e_{02}\); for a duplicate (\(k_2 = 1\)) to 0, 0 and 1.

Setting the observed fractions equal to these expected values and solving from the top line down gives PLINK's estimates:6,7

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{k}_0 &= \frac{m_0}{m\, e_{00}} \\[6pt] \hat{k}_1 &= \frac{m_1 - m\, \hat{k}_0\, e_{01}}{m\, e_{11}} \\[6pt] \hat{k}_2 &= 1 - \hat{k}_0 - \hat{k}_1 \end{aligned} \]
\(\hat{k}_0, \hat{k}_1, \hat{k}_2\)
Estimates of the IBD fractions. PLINK writes them as Z0, Z1 and Z2.
\(m_0, m_1, m\)
Observed counts of IBS-0 SNPs, IBS-1 SNPs and all SNPs used for the pair.
\(e_{00}, e_{01}, e_{11}\)
Expected IBS fractions given IBD, averaged over SNPs.

The first line has a single unknown because opposite homozygotes cannot occur where a copy is shared: \(\hat{k}_0\) is the observed number of opposite homozygotes divided by the number expected if the pair shared nothing anywhere. The second line subtracts the IBS-1 SNPs that the IBD-0 part of the genome produces, and divides what is left by the rate at which IBD-1 stretches produce IBS 1. The third uses the fact that the fractions add to 1 (PLINK computes \(\hat{k}_2\) from the IBS-2 line, which gives the same number). A worked example, simplified so that \(p = 0.5\) at every SNP (\(e_{00} = 0.125\), \(e_{01} = e_{11} = 0.5\)): a pair with \(m = 10{,}000\) SNPs, \(m_0 = 330\), \(m_1 = 3{,}700\) and \(m_2 = 5{,}970\) gets \(\hat{k}_0 = 330 / 1{,}250 = 0.264\), \(\hat{k}_1 = (3{,}700 - 1{,}320) / 5{,}000 = 0.476\) and \(\hat{k}_2 = 1 - 0.264 - 0.476 = 0.260\), close to the full-sibling values ¼, ½ and ¼.

When an estimate falls outside 0 to 1

Nothing in the three equations keeps the estimates between 0 and 1. For an unrelated pair, \(\hat{k}_0\) scatters around 1 and is above 1 about half the time, and \(\hat{k}_2\) scatters around 0. By default PLINK 1.9 clips the estimates, checking in this order:5,7

  1. If Z0 > 1, set Z0 = 1 and Z1 = Z2 = 0. Otherwise, if Z1 > 1 or Z2 > 1, set that one to 1 and the other two to 0.
  2. Otherwise, if Z0 < 0, set it to 0 and rescale Z1 and Z2 so that they add to 1.
  3. Then, if Z1 < 0, set it to 0 and rescale Z0 and Z2; then the same for Z2.

Two consequences show up in every real .genome file. Many unrelated pairs get exactly Z0 = 1 and PI_HAT = 0, so the distribution of PI_HAT has a spike at 0; the others get small positive values, and because negative values are cut off, the average PI_HAT of unrelated pairs is slightly above 0. And PI_HAT = 0 does not mean "certainly unrelated"; it means the pair shared no more alleles than unrelated people are expected to. The modifier --genome unbounded turns clipping off, which shows the raw spread of the estimates.

4 · Summarize: PI_HAT

\[ \hat{\pi} = \frac{\hat{k}_1}{2} + \hat{k}_2 \]
\(\hat{\pi}\)
Estimated proportion of the genome the pair shares IBD, from 0 to 1: PLINK's PI_HAT. Here \(\pi\) is a proportion, not the circle constant.
\(\hat{k}_1, \hat{k}_2\)
Z1 and Z2 after clipping.

Where the pair shares 2 copies IBD, each of their copies has a partner in the other person; where it shares 1, half of them do; where it shares 0, none do. Averaging over the genome gives \(\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{k}_1 + 1 \cdot \hat{k}_2\) (Lecture 0 derives this, together with the kinship coefficient \(\varphi = \pi/2\)). In the worked example, \(\hat{\pi} = 0.476/2 + 0.260 = 0.498\). Different relationships can have the same \(\pi\): a parent and child (\(k_1 = 1\)) and full siblings (\(k_0 = \tfrac{1}{4}\), \(k_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}\), \(k_2 = \tfrac{1}{4}\)) both have \(\pi = \tfrac{1}{2}\). Z0 tells them apart, which is why the practical plots Z0 against Z1 rather than looking at PI_HAT alone.

Real pairs scatter around these points for two reasons. First, the true sharing varies: two full siblings share about half their genomes on average, but the exact fraction depends on where the crossovers fell in the sperm and eggs that made them (Lecture 0's simulator shows this). One quality-control review drew its bands for full siblings at \(\pm 2\) standard deviations as 0.25 ± 0.08 for Z0 and 0.50 ± 0.10 for Z1.3 Second, the estimate has sampling error, which shrinks as the number of SNPs grows. The two together make the clouds of half siblings and first cousins touch: PI_HAT separates duplicates and first-degree relatives cleanly from everyone else, second-degree from third-degree relatives only roughly, and third-degree relatives from unrelated pairs least reliably, especially when genetic ancestry varies within the sample (below).

Two practical readings of the plane follow. A pair with Z0 near 0 shares at least one copy everywhere: a parent and child if Z1 is near 1, a duplicate if Z2 is near 1. A pair with PI_HAT near 0.5 but Z0 near 0.25 is a pair of full siblings. PLINK's nudge modifier moves estimates that fall below the dotted curve onto it.5

Run it in the browser Core

The practical runs --genome on the fileset left after step 1's filters, restricted to the SNPs that step 3 kept. Here is the command for the toy data, part by part (the same command with penncath in place of toy is the practical's):1

  1. plink: PLINK 1.9 (in the lab, its emulator).
  2. --bfile PREFIX: the input fileset, here the one written by step 1 after removing SNPs and then people with more than 1% missing calls (step 1).
  3. --extract FILE: keep only the SNPs listed in FILE, one ID per line; all others are ignored in this run. The list is .prune.in from step 3, SNPs with pairwise \(r^2\) of at most 0.3 within windows of 50 SNPs. The estimator treats SNPs as independent; the PLINK documentation warns that these calculations "are not LD-aware" and recommends LD pruning first.5
  4. --genome: compare every pair of people and write PREFIX.genome. It uses the autosomes only. Modifiers: full (add the IBS counts), gz (compress), rel-check (same-family pairs only), unbounded (no clipping), nudge; related flags --min and --max (keep pairs with PI_HAT in a range) and --read-freq (use allele frequencies from a file instead of from this sample).
  5. --out PREFIX: the prefix of the files written, PREFIX.genome and PREFIX.log. LD0.3 in the name records the \(r^2\) threshold of the pruning; steps 5 and 6 use the same prefix.

When PI_HAT misleads: ancestry, contamination and genotype quality Core

The estimator assumes three things: that every pair's unshared alleles are drawn with the same allele frequencies, the sample's; that genotypes are correct; and that SNPs are independent. Each failure produces a recognizable pattern.

Genetic ancestry: everyone in a group looks slightly related

PLINK computes \(p\) from the whole file. When the sample contains a group of people whose genetic ancestry, and therefore whose allele frequencies, differ from those of the rest, two unrelated people from that group are more alike than the sample's frequencies predict: they are opposite homozygotes less often than \(e_{00}\) says, so Z0 comes out below 1 and PI_HAT above 0. Two people from different groups are opposite homozygotes more often, so their Z0 exceeds 1 and is clipped to PI_HAT = 0. A calculation with made-up frequencies shows the size of the effect:

PairIBS 0 expectedPLINK's \(e_{00}\)Z0Z1PI_HAT
Both from group 1 (or both from group 2)0.1150.1250.920.080.04
One from each group0.1550.1251.24 → 1−0.24 → 00

Applied to every SNP of the genome (frequencies 0.1 apart from the pooled value in each direction, a large difference, of the size seen between continental groups), the same calculation gives unrelated pairs within a group an expected PI_HAT of about 0.06, and pairs across groups 0. That is well below 0.1875, but it moves the whole within-group cloud away from (1, 0), and with estimation noise on top some pairs can cross a threshold. Three remedies exist: estimate relatedness within groups of genetically similar people, give PLINK allele frequencies that fit the group with --read-freq, or use an estimator built to be robust to ancestry differences, such as KING (below) or PC-Relate.5,10,12 Do not remove people for this pattern: it is ancestry, which step 6 and steps 7–8 describe and the association analysis adjusts for.

Contamination: one sample related to everyone

A sample that mixes DNA from two people is called heterozygous at many SNPs where its true owner is homozygous (step 5 shows how). A heterozygous call can never be an opposite homozygote, so the mixed sample has too few IBS-0 SNPs with every other person: its Z0 falls below 1 and its PI_HAT rises with everyone at once. In one GWAS, two samples appeared related to nearly every other sample in the study, with kinship between the expected values of half siblings and first cousins; both had high heterozygosity, and their intensity data showed mixtures of two people's DNA.3 The signature is easy to find: count how many flagged pairs each person belongs to. One person in dozens of pairs is a contamination suspect, not a person with dozens of relatives; check their heterozygosity and missingness, remove them, and then look at relatedness again.

Genotyping errors and LD

A genotyping error at a SNP where a pair truly matches turns IBS 2 into IBS 1, or IBS 1 into IBS 0, which pulls duplicates and parent–child pairs slightly away from their expected points (duplicates with PI_HAT of 0.98 rather than 1). Low-quality samples with many errors can show weak relatedness to many people, like contaminated ones.3 SNPs in LD carry overlapping information, so without pruning a few long LD blocks count many times and the estimates become noisier; that is why the command uses --extract with the pruned list.

Why not lower. Third-degree relatives (expected PI_HAT 0.125) overlap with unrelated pairs once ancestry and estimation noise are added (above), and a single first-cousin pair adds little dependence to a study of thousands. Thresholds near 0.1875 or 0.2 remove the pairs that matter while keeping false alarms rare.

Duplicates. A pair with PI_HAT near 1 needs a decision of its own. If it is a planned laboratory replicate, keep the copy with the higher call rate and use the pair to estimate the genotyping error rate (the share of SNPs at which the two copies disagree). If it is not planned, compare the two records: same sex, same age, same phenotype? If the two copies carry different phenotypes, at least one label is wrong and it is not possible to know which, so the usual choice is to remove both.

Which member of a related pair to remove. The common rule keeps the person with fewer missing calls.13 Others are reasonable when argued in advance: keep the person whose phenotype is recorded, or, in a case–control study with few cases, keep the case. Check what the phenotype codes mean before basing a rule on them: in this copy of PennCATH they do not mean what the practical's description of column 6 suggests (The data · What column 6 holds), so read that section before using "keep the one with a recorded phenotype" or "keep the case". When one person is related to several others, removing that one person can resolve several pairs at once. Choosing the smallest set of people to remove is a hard combinatorial problem; PLINK 2's --king-cutoff uses a greedy algorithm for it.15

Remove or model. Removing one member per pair suits analyses that assume independent people, such as PLINK's logistic regression. The alternative is to keep everyone and use a method that models relatedness, a linear mixed model or a whole-genome regression such as REGENIE, which Session 2 uses (see GWAS analysis).4 Duplicates are removed either way.

Order of operations. Run this step after the missingness filters, because missing calls add noise; look at heterozygosity (step 5) before deciding, because a contaminated sample creates many false pairs; and settle relatedness before computing principal components (step 6), because a family can form its own component. The decision itself is applied once, at the end, when the cleaned fileset is made (the exercise).

Record. Write the removed people to a text file with two columns, family ID and within-family ID, which plink --remove FILE reads; keep a note of the threshold, the pairs, each person's PI_HAT and the reason for choosing that member. The QC report of the exercise has a row for this step.

In the toy data. At 0.1875 the widget's removal list starts with the contaminated sample, ?, which resolves all of its pairs at once (step 5 flags it as well). Whichever rule you choose, choose it before looking at the results and apply it to every pair.

KING kinship with PLINK 2 Optional

KING is a program, and an estimator of the kinship coefficient, designed to work without allele frequencies, so that population structure barely affects it.10 PLINK 2 implements its "robust" estimator as --make-king-table; PLINK 2 has no --genome.15 For a pair of people from the same population the estimator is:

\[ \hat{\varphi} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{het,het}} - 2N_{\mathrm{opp}}}{N_{\mathrm{het}}^{(\mathrm{A})} + N_{\mathrm{het}}^{(\mathrm{B})}} \]
\(\hat{\varphi}\)
Estimated kinship coefficient of persons A and B: 0.5 for duplicates, 0.25 for first-degree relatives, 0.125 for second-degree and 0.0625 for third-degree; half of PI_HAT.
\(N_{\mathrm{het,het}}\)
Number of SNPs at which both are heterozygous. The .kin0 file reports it as a proportion of the SNPs used, in the column HETHET.
\(N_{\mathrm{opp}}\)
Number of SNPs at which they are opposite homozygotes, IBS 0; reported as a proportion in the column IBS0.
\(N_{\mathrm{het}}^{(\mathrm{A})}, N_{\mathrm{het}}^{(\mathrm{B})}\)
Numbers of SNPs at which A, and B, are heterozygous. All counts are over SNPs genotyped in both.

For two unrelated people drawn from one population, at a SNP with allele frequency \(p\), both are heterozygous with probability \((2pq)^2 = 4p^2q^2\) and they are opposite homozygotes with probability \(2p^2q^2\), so the numerator has expected value \(4p^2q^2 - 2 \cdot 2p^2q^2 = 0\) at every SNP, whatever \(p\) is. No allele frequency is needed, and ancestry differences between pairs do not shift the estimate away from 0. For a duplicate, \(N_{\mathrm{opp}} = 0\) and \(N_{\mathrm{het,het}} = N_{\mathrm{het}}^{(\mathrm{A})} = N_{\mathrm{het}}^{(\mathrm{B})}\), so \(\hat{\varphi} = \tfrac{1}{2}\). For unrelated people from two populations with frequencies \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), the expected numerator is \(-2(p_1 - p_2)^2\) per SNP, so their estimate is negative. PLINK 2 uses KING's between-family version for every pair. It divides by twice the smaller of the two heterozygote counts and subtracts a term that grows with the difference between them; for two people with equal heterozygote counts it gives the same value as the formula above. It is meant for pairs whose heterozygosity differs, as it does between people from different populations.10,15

Pitfalls Core

Pitfall PI_HAT is twice the kinship coefficient

A threshold copied from KING, PLINK 2 or UK Biobank (kinship 0.0884 for second degree) applied to PI_HAT removes third-degree relatives too; one copied the other way (PI_HAT 0.1875 applied to kinship) keeps first-degree relatives. Check which quantity a threshold refers to.

Pitfall Reading PI_HAT alone

A parent–child pair and a pair of full siblings both have PI_HAT near 0.5; a contaminated sample and a half sibling can both have PI_HAT near 0.2. Look at Z0 and Z1, and count how many pairs each person is in.

Pitfall Removing an ancestry group as "relatives"

Many pairs with small positive PI_HAT among people of one genetic ancestry group are an artifact of using the whole sample's allele frequencies. Removing them shrinks the diversity of the study and does not fix anything; use a robust estimator or estimate within groups, and handle ancestry with principal components.

Pitfall Summarizing a filtered file

After --min 0.05, the .genome file holds only the closest pairs, so summary(PI_HAT) and the scatter plot describe them, not the sample. Also do not run --genome on all SNPs instead of the pruned set: SNPs in LD are not independent and the estimates become noisier.

Key points
  • Relatedness is a statistic per pair: \(n\) people give \(n(n-1)/2\) pairs, 951,510 for the 1,380 PennCATH people.
  • PLINK counts the SNPs at which a pair shares 0, 1 or 2 alleles by state and uses the method of moments, with the expected IBS fractions for each IBD state, to estimate Z0, Z1, Z2; PI_HAT = Z1/2 + Z2.
  • Estimates outside 0–1 are clipped, so many unrelated pairs have PI_HAT exactly 0 and the rest small positive values.
  • Duplicates sit at Z0 = 0, Z1 = 0; parent–child at (0, 1); full siblings at (0.25, 0.5); second-degree relatives at (0.5, 0.5); third-degree at (0.75, 0.25); unrelated at (1, 0). Z0 separates parent–child pairs from siblings.
  • Ancestry differences raise PI_HAT within groups; contamination raises it with everyone; neither is family relatedness.
  • Common rules flag PI_HAT > 0.1875 (or 0.2) and remove the member with more missing calls, or keep everyone and use a mixed model; duplicates are always resolved.

Check your understanding

A pair has \(m = 10{,}000\) SNPs, all with \(p = 0.5\), and \(m_0 = 0\), \(m_1 = 5{,}020\), \(m_2 = 4{,}980\). Compute Z0, Z1, Z2 and PI_HAT as PLINK would. What is the relationship?

\(\hat{k}_0 = 0 / (10{,}000 \times 0.125) = 0\). \(\hat{k}_1 = (5{,}020 - 0) / (10{,}000 \times 0.5) = 1.004\), which is above 1, so PLINK sets Z1 = 1 and Z0 = Z2 = 0, and PI_HAT = 0.5. No opposite homozygotes and one allele shared by state at about half the SNPs: a parent and child, who share exactly one copy IBD everywhere. Full siblings would also have PI_HAT near 0.5 but Z0 near 0.25, because they share nothing IBD on about a quarter of the genome, where opposite homozygotes occur.

Why is a SNP at which two people are opposite homozygotes (IBS 0) more informative about relatedness than a SNP at which they match (IBS 2), especially when the allele is rare?

IBS 0 is impossible wherever the pair shares a copy IBD, so every IBS-0 SNP is direct evidence of a stretch shared 0 copies; that is why Z0 is simply the observed IBS-0 count over the count expected for unrelated people. A match (IBS 2) happens for unrelated people too, and for a rare allele almost always: with \(p = 0.1\), \(e_{02} = 0.689\), so two unrelated people match at about 69% of such SNPs. A match therefore carries little information, while an opposite homozygote at such a SNP is rare (\(e_{00} = 0.016\)) and, when seen, says a lot.

A study reports kinship coefficients from KING and removes pairs above 0.0884. Which PI_HAT threshold is that, and how does it compare with Anderson et al.'s 0.1875?

Kinship is half the proportion IBD (\(\varphi = \pi/2\)), so 0.0884 corresponds to PI_HAT = 0.177. Both cut-offs lie between the second-degree expectation (PI_HAT 0.25, kinship 0.125) and the third-degree expectation (0.125, kinship 0.0625): KING's is the midpoint on a logarithmic scale (\(\sqrt{0.125 \times 0.0625} \approx 0.0884\)), Anderson's the arithmetic midpoint, (0.25 + 0.125)/2 = 0.1875. Both remove second-degree and closer relatives.

After running --genome you find one person in 40 of the 45 pairs with PI_HAT above 0.1875, all with PI_HAT between 0.15 and 0.25. What is the most likely explanation, and what do you check next?

One person cannot have 40 second-degree relatives in an ordinary study. A sample that appears related to many people at once is the signature of DNA contamination (or of a very low-quality sample): the mixture is called heterozygous at many SNPs, so it is rarely an opposite homozygote with anyone, and its Z0 falls below 1 with everyone. Check that person's heterozygosity (step 5: a contaminated sample has a high heterozygosity rate) and missingness (step 1). Remove the sample, then re-examine the remaining five pairs.

Two samples have PI_HAT = 0.99, but one is recorded as a case and the other as a control, with different study IDs. What do you do?

The genotypes say the two samples come from one person (or identical twins), but the records disagree, so at least one sample was mislabeled, mixed up or enrolled twice. Without a way to tell which record is right, the usual decision is to remove both, and to report the mix-up to the laboratory, since other samples from the same plate may also be affected. If the records can be checked (same sex, age and recruitment site?) and one is clearly wrong, the other may be kept.

A sample mixes two groups whose allele frequencies differ. Why do unrelated people within a group get PI_HAT above 0, while pairs across groups get exactly 0?

PLINK computes \(e_{00}\) and the other expectations from the allele frequencies of the whole file. Two people from the same group carry alleles drawn from their group's frequencies, so they are opposite homozygotes less often than the pooled frequencies predict: Z0 = \(m_0/(m e_{00})\) comes out below 1 and PI_HAT above 0 (about 0.04 in the one-SNP example on this page with frequencies 0.4 and 0.6). Two people from different groups are opposite homozygotes more often than predicted, so Z0 exceeds 1, and clipping sets Z0 = 1 and PI_HAT = 0.
