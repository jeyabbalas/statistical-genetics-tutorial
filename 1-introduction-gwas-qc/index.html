Genetic epidemiology, GWAS and quality control

Session 1 of the workshop asks how differences in DNA between people relate to differences in their health, and answers with one kind of study: the genome-wide association study (GWAS). A GWAS measures hundreds of thousands of positions in the genome in thousands of people, tests each position for a link with a trait, and only works if the measurements are cleaned first. This lecture covers the field, the population-genetic ideas the analysis depends on, the GWAS from design to use, and a hands-on practical that cleans a real dataset with PLINK, here also runnable in your browser.

Mental model 1 · The matrix

One matrix holds the whole lecture

A GWAS stores its genotypes as a matrix with one row per person and one column per SNP, next to a column of phenotypes and a few columns of covariates. Almost everything in Lecture 1 is a statistic computed on that matrix: on one column, on neighboring columns, on one row, on pairs of rows, on all rows together, or, for the association test itself, on each column paired with the phenotype.

  • Why build it (Part A). Genetic epidemiology asks whether, how much and why genotype differences between people go with differences in their health. Which people fill the rows, and which traits fill the phenotype column, are scientific and social choices.
  • Columns (Part B). One column gives an allele frequency and a Hardy–Weinberg test; neighboring columns are correlated by linkage disequilibrium.
  • Rows (Part B). Rows differ systematically by ancestry; principal components of the matrix summarize that population structure.
  • One test per column (Part C). A regression of the phenotype on each column, adjusted for covariates, gives an effect estimate and a p-value; a GWAS is millions of these.
  • Cleaning rows and columns (Part D). Quality control removes columns and rows whose values cannot be trusted, and finds related people and ancestry groups before any test is run.
Mental model 2 · The pipeline

A GWAS is eight stages, and quality control is the fourth

A GWAS goes from a question to people and measurements, to a genotype matrix, to a cleaned matrix, to imputed genotypes, to one test per SNP, to a list of associated regions that is then interpreted and used. Errors made early cannot be fixed by a better test later, which is why the practical spends a whole session on stage 4.

The practical, two ways Core

The workshop's practical cleans the genotypes of the PennCATH study (1,401 people and 861,473 SNPs) with PLINK 1.9, makes plots in R, and assigns genetic ancestry with GrafAnc. The workshop runs it on NIH's Biowulf computer cluster. This site gives you two other ways to do it:

In your browser, on toy data

Each step page embeds a terminal. Type the same commands as in the practical, with the file name toy in place of penncath; a JavaScript version of PLINK 1.9 runs them on a small simulated study and writes output files with PLINK's names and columns. Widgets plot those files, and an optional R console runs the practical's R code on them. Nothing to install.

On your computer or a cloud machine, on the real data

Set up your computer installs PLINK, R and GrafAnc on macOS, Linux or Windows, or uses a cloud service, and downloads the PennCATH files. Each step page then gives the real commands and R code, and says what to look for in the output.

The toy dataset was built so that every quality-control step has something to find: SNPs and people with many missing calls, genotyping errors that break Hardy–Weinberg proportions, a duplicate and several relatives, a contaminated sample, people of several ancestries, and SNPs coded on the opposite DNA strand from the reference panel. The description of the toy dataset lists each one.

After this lecture you can… Core

  • Define epidemiology and genetic epidemiology, and describe three ways genetic findings have changed medicine or public health (page 1).
  • Place a study in the map of the field, and explain why a GWAS of a behavior can reflect social structure (pages 2–3).
  • Read a SNP's name, position and alleles in a .bim file, code genotypes as 0/1/2, and recognize strand problems (page 4).
  • Estimate an allele frequency with its standard error, and test Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium by chi-square and exact tests (page 5).
  • Compute D, D′ and r² from haplotype frequencies, estimate them from genotypes, and explain why LD differs between populations (page 6).
  • Explain what principal components of a genotype matrix show and what they do not (page 7).
  • Describe the stages of a GWAS, compute its power, and read a Manhattan plot and a Q–Q plot (pages 8–9).
  • Explain how GWAS results feed polygenic scores, Mendelian randomization and drug discovery, and name their limits (page 10).
  • Run the eight quality-control steps with PLINK, read every output file, and justify each threshold (pages 11–23).
