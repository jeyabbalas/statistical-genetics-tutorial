Testing association and reading GWAS results

With clean genotypes and a phenotype in hand, a GWAS fits one regression per SNP, about a million times, and keeps the SNPs whose p-value clears 5 × 10⁻⁸. This page follows the analysis half of the session’s reading: the test itself, the threshold, the plots that show the results and their biases, the corrections for ancestry and relatedness, combining and replicating studies, and the work that turns a significant locus into a causal variant, a gene and a pathway.

Reading: Testing for associations, Accounting for false discovery, meta-analysis, Results, Figs. 2–3 ≈ 75 min Shows up in the data as: one row per SNP with BETA or OR, SE and P; Manhattan and Q–Q plots
Mental model

One regression per SNP, then four steps that decide which rows to believe

For each SNP the GWAS regresses the phenotype on the genotype and the covariates, and keeps three numbers: the estimated effect \(\hat{\beta}\), its standard error and the p-value of \(z = \hat{\beta}/\SE(\hat{\beta})\). One row per SNP makes the summary statistics. Everything after that asks which rows to believe: which pass a threshold set for a million tests, which are free of confounding, which hold up in other people, and which variant in a block of correlated SNPs is the cause.

  • Test. Linear regression for a quantitative trait, logistic regression for a disease; the coefficient of the genotype, its standard error and \(z\) give the p-value and, for a disease, the odds ratio.
  • Threshold. About a million independent tests set the bar at p < 5 × 10⁻⁸; Manhattan and Q–Q plots show the results, and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) and LD score regression measure inflation.
  • Bias. Ancestry and relatedness create associations of their own; principal components and linear mixed models (BOLT-LMM, SAIGE, REGENIE) remove them.
  • Combine. Cohorts share summary statistics, a meta-analysis weights them by inverse variance, and independent replication confirms the findings.
  • Narrow down. LD makes each locus a block of associated SNPs; conditional analysis and Bayesian fine-mapping give each SNP a posterior inclusion probability and each locus a credible set.
  • Interpret. Annotation, molecular QTLs and colocalization, chromatin contacts, experiments and gene-set tests link variants to genes, cell types and pathways (Fig. 3 of the reading).

One regression per SNP Core

Step e tests each SNP separately. For person \(i\) the genotype \(x_i\) counts the copies of the effect allele (0, 1 or 2; an imputed dosage between 0 and 2 for imputed SNPs), and the phenotype \(y_i\) is a measurement or a 0/1 disease status. The reading notes that the theory behind the test is the biometrical model, in which each allele adds to the trait1. Coding the genotype as a count of alleles builds that in: the additive model assumes that two copies have twice the effect of one. Other codings exist (dominant: 0 vs 1 or 2 copies; recessive: 0 or 1 vs 2 copies; genotypic: two separate effects), but the additive test is the default because it has good power for most patterns of effect.

Quantitative traits: linear regression

A regression line fitted by least squares chooses the intercept and slope that make the sum of squared vertical distances between the points and the line as small as possible. Its slope is the regression coefficient: the change in \(y\) per unit change in \(x\). In a GWAS the slope is the effect of one more copy of the effect allele, and the covariates (age, sex, principal components of ancestry, genotyping batch) enter the same model so that the slope is estimated holding them fixed1.

\[ \begin{aligned} y_i ={}& \alpha_0 + \beta x_i \\ &+ \mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \varepsilon_i \end{aligned} \]
\(y_i\)
Trait value of person \(i\), for \(i = 1, \dots, N\) people, in trait units (for example mmol/L, or standard deviations after standardizing).
\(\alpha_0\)
Intercept: the expected trait value for a person with 0 copies and all covariates at 0.
\(x_i\)
Genotype of person \(i\) at the tested SNP: copies of the effect allele, 0, 1 or 2 (or a dosage from 0 to 2).
\(\beta\)
Effect of the SNP: change in the expected trait value per copy of the effect allele. The test asks whether \(\beta = 0\).
\(\mathbf{w}_i, \boldsymbol{\alpha}\)
Covariate values of person \(i\) (a vector, such as age, sex and 10 principal components) and their coefficients.
\(\varepsilon_i\)
Residual: everything else that moves person \(i\)’s trait, assumed independent between people with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2\).

The model draws a straight line through the mean trait values of the three genotype groups. If the means are 0.00, 0.10 and 0.20 standard deviations for 0, 1 and 2 copies, \(\beta = 0.10\). Covariates matter when they differ between genotype groups: if people with 2 copies were on average older and age raised the trait, leaving age out would credit the SNP with the effect of age. The same model is fitted at every SNP; only \(x_i\) changes, so a GWAS of a million SNPs fits a million such lines.

\[ \begin{gathered} \hat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \\[4pt] \SE(\hat{\beta}) = \frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2}} \end{gathered} \]
\(\hat{\beta}\)
Least-squares estimate of \(\beta\) (written without covariates; with covariates, \(x\) and \(y\) are first replaced by their residuals after regression on the covariates, and the formulas are the same).
\(\bar{x}, \bar{y}\)
Means of the genotype and of the trait over the \(N\) people.
\(\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2\)
Spread of the genotypes: about \(N \cdot 2p(1-p)\) for effect-allele frequency \(p\).
\(\hat{\sigma}\)
Estimated residual standard deviation: the typical distance of a point from the fitted line.
\(\SE(\hat{\beta})\)
Standard error of \(\hat{\beta}\): the standard deviation of the estimate over repeated samples of \(N\) people.

The slope is the covariance of genotype and trait divided by the variance of the genotype. Its standard error shrinks as the genotypes spread out: a SNP with \(p = 0.3\) in 2,000 people has \(\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \approx 2{,}000 \times 0.42 = 840\), so for a standardized trait (\(\hat{\sigma} \approx 1\)) \(\SE(\hat{\beta}) \approx 1/\sqrt{840} = 0.035\). Four times as many people halve the standard error; a rare SNP, with little spread, has a large one.

\[ z = \frac{\hat{\beta}}{\SE(\hat{\beta})} \]
\(z\)
The Wald statistic, or z-score: the estimate measured in standard errors. If \(\beta = 0\) it follows the standard normal distribution (mean 0, standard deviation 1), and \(z^2\) follows a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom.
\(\hat{\beta}, \SE(\hat{\beta})\)
The estimate and its standard error from the formulas above (or from logistic regression, below).

Without an effect, \(\hat{\beta}\) scatters around 0 with standard deviation \(\SE(\hat{\beta})\), so \(z\) rarely leaves \(\pm 2\). The two-sided p-value is the probability that a standard normal variable is at least as far from 0 as the observed \(z\): \(|z| = 1.96\) gives p = 0.05, \(|z| = 3.29\) gives p = 0.001 and \(|z| = 5.45\) gives p = 5 × 10⁻⁸. With an effect, \(z\) is centered at about \(\sqrt{\lambda}\), the square root of the noncentrality parameter of page 8: for \(\beta = 0.1\), \(p = 0.3\) and 2,000 people, \(\sqrt{\lambda} \approx 0.1/0.035 = 2.9\). For a quantitative trait PLINK reports the t statistic, which is the same number and has almost the same distribution when \(N\) is in the thousands.

Diseases: logistic regression and the odds ratio

A 0/1 outcome cannot be fitted with a straight line, because predictions would leave the range 0–1. Logistic regression models the log of the odds of being a case, \(\logit P = \ln(P/(1-P))\), as a straight line in the genotype. Its coefficients are estimated by maximum likelihood (the values under which the observed cases and controls are most probable), its standard errors come from the curvature of the likelihood, and the Wald \(z\) is formed as above1.

\[ \begin{gathered} \logit P(y_i = 1) = \alpha_0 + \beta x_i + \mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha} \\[4pt] \widehat{\mathrm{OR}} = e^{\hat{\beta}} \\ \text{95\% CI: } e^{\hat{\beta} \pm 1.96\,\SE(\hat{\beta})} \end{gathered} \]
\(P(y_i = 1)\)
Probability that person \(i\) is a case, given genotype and covariates.
\(\logit\)
The log-odds, \(\ln(P/(1-P))\): 0 for \(P = 0.5\), negative below, positive above.
\(\beta\)
Change in the log-odds of disease per copy of the effect allele.
\(\widehat{\mathrm{OR}}\)
Estimated odds ratio per copy: the factor by which one more copy multiplies the odds of disease.
\(\text{95\% CI}\)
95% confidence interval of the odds ratio, built on the log scale where \(\hat{\beta}\) is close to normal.

Because the model is linear in the log-odds, each copy of the allele multiplies the odds by the same factor \(e^{\beta}\): with \(\mathrm{OR} = 1.3\), heterozygotes have 1.3 times and homozygotes \(1.3^2 = 1.69\) times the odds of people with 0 copies. Worked number from the widget below: \(\hat{\beta} = 0.293\) and \(\SE = 0.075\) give \(\widehat{\mathrm{OR}} = e^{0.293} = 1.34\), with 95% CI \(e^{0.293 \pm 0.147}\), from 1.16 to 1.55, and \(z = 3.9\). The odds ratio estimated in a case–control sample estimates the population odds ratio even though cases are over-represented; the intercept absorbs the sampling (page 8).

A million tests: thresholds, Manhattan and Q–Q plots Core

At a significance level of 0.05, one test in 20 of a SNP with no effect is significant by chance. A GWAS of a million SNPs would therefore report about 50,000 false positives. The Bonferroni correction divides the level by the number of tests \(m\): if each test uses \(0.05/m\), the probability of at least one false positive among all \(m\) tests (the family-wise error rate) stays below 0.05. Because SNPs in LD give correlated tests, the relevant \(m\) is the effective number of independent tests. Using HapMap data, Pe’er et al. estimated that the common variants of the genome amount to about one million independent tests in people of European ancestry and about twice that number in people of African ancestry4, which gives the conventional threshold \(0.05/10^6\): p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ (Lecture 0 · the effective number of tests). The reading describes this threshold as a false discovery rate1; strictly, Bonferroni controls the family-wise error rate, a stricter quantity than the false discovery rate (the expected share of false positives among the significant results).

The threshold depends on what is tested and in whom. Rare variants are mostly not in LD with common ones, so each adds close to one independent test: for sequencing studies of more than 2,000 people, Pulit et al. recommended about p < 5 × 10⁻⁹ in samples of European, Asian or admixed ancestry and p < 1 × 10⁻⁹ in African samples, whose genomes have shorter LD5. A looser suggestive threshold, often 1 × 10⁻⁵, flags SNPs for follow-up but is not evidence of association.

Manhattan plots (Fig. 2a)

A Manhattan plot shows every SNP’s p-value against its position. Its height scale is −log₁₀ p: each step of 1 is a factor of 10 in p. The reading’s example is a meta-analysis of body mass index in about 700,000 people of European ancestry1, 6. Tools such as R, FUMA and LocusZoom draw these plots1, 7, 8. Lecture 0 has an interactive Manhattan plot of a simulated LDL-cholesterol GWAS with hover labels and zoom.

Q–Q plots and the genomic inflation factor (Fig. 2b)

A quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plot compares the distribution of the observed p-values with the uniform distribution that p-values follow when no SNP has an effect (Lecture 0 · QQ plots). The reading’s caption gives the rule for reading it: true effects make the points leave the diagonal at the right side of the plot, while population stratification makes the deviation start close to the origin. A very polygenic trait studied in a large sample also lifts the points early, so the two causes can be hard to tell apart by eye1. Two numbers summarize the inflation.

\[ \lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = \frac{\median(z_j^2)}{0.4549} \]
\(z_j^2\)
Chi-square statistic (1 degree of freedom) of SNP \(j\), for all \(m\) tested SNPs.
\(\median(z_j^2)\)
The middle value of the \(m\) statistics.
\(0.4549\)
The median of a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom: \(z^2 \le 0.4549\) exactly when \(|z| \le 0.674\), which has probability 1/2.
\(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\)
Genomic inflation factor: 1 when the bulk of the tests behaves as if no SNP had an effect.

A median ignores the few hundred strongest SNPs, so true hits alone barely move \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\); a bias that multiplies every statistic by a factor \(c\) multiplies the median, and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\), by \(c\). Genomic control divides every statistic by \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) to remove such a uniform inflation9. In the figure the blue scan has \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = 1.13\) without any confounding: 9% of its SNPs sit in the eight associated regions, and the others were given a slight inflation (1.04) to mimic many tiny polygenic effects. Multiplying every statistic by 1.25 gives the stratified version, 1.42. Real polygenic traits spread small effects over the whole genome, and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) then grows with \(N\) even without confounding10, so a large biobank GWAS can have \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) well above 1 and still be unbiased.

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[z_j^2] ={}& \frac{N h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}}}{M}\,\ell_j \\ &+ N a + 1 \end{aligned} \]
\(\E[z_j^2]\)
Expected chi-square statistic of SNP \(j\).
\(N\)
Number of people in the GWAS.
\(h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}}\)
SNP-heritability: the share of trait variance explained by the additive effects of the \(M\) SNPs, between 0 and 1.
\(M\)
Number of SNPs whose effects make up \(h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}}\).
\(\ell_j\)
LD score of SNP \(j\): the sum of its \(r^2\) with all SNPs within 1 cM, itself included, computed in a reference panel of matching ancestry.
\(N a\)
Inflation from confounding (stratification, cryptic relatedness), the same for every SNP whatever its LD score.

Polygenic signal reaches a SNP through every variant it tags, so it grows with \(\ell_j\); confounding does not. LD score regression fits this line to all SNPs: the slope estimates \(N h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}}/M\) and the intercept \(1 + Na\) measures confounding10. Worked numbers: with \(N = 100{,}000\), \(h^2_{\mathrm{SNP}} = 0.3\) and \(M = 10^6\), the slope is 0.03, so a SNP with \(\ell_j = 50\) has expected statistic \(1 + 1.5 = 2.5\) without any confounding. An intercept of 1.02 with a mean statistic of 1.40 means that \((1.02 - 1)/(1.40 - 1) = 5\%\) of the inflation comes from confounding and 95% from polygenic signal. Lecture 0 derives the formula (Lecture 0 · LD scores).

Confounding by ancestry and relatedness Core

A confounder is a variable that affects both the exposure and the outcome. In a GWAS the exposure is a genotype, and ancestry is the classic confounder: allele frequencies differ between populations (page 7), and so can the trait, for reasons that have nothing to do with the SNP, such as diet, culture or how the study recruited people. If a sample mixes two such populations, every SNP whose frequency differs between them is associated with the trait. This is population stratification; the chopsticks example of page 8 is an extreme case. The reading adds that uncorrected structure overestimates SNP-heritability, biases polygenic scores and can bias Mendelian randomization1.

Try itPopulation stratification: false positives from ancestry, and their removal by a principal component

What to notice. None of the 1,000 simulated SNPs affects the trait. With the defaults (two populations in equal shares, \(F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.02\), a trait difference of 0.5 standard deviations, 1,000 people) the unadjusted scan has \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} \approx 2.2\) and about 170 SNPs with p < 0.05 instead of the 50 expected by chance; adding PC1 as a covariate brings \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) back to about 1.0. Set the trait difference to 0 and the inflation disappears: ancestry confounds only when it is related to both the genotypes and the trait. Raise \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) to 0.05 and the trait difference to 1, and about 85 SNPs pass 5 × 10⁻⁸ without adjustment, none with it. Choose 2,000 people and the unadjusted \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) rises to about 3.3: stratification bias, unlike chance, does not shrink with sample size; it grows.

Principal components as covariates

The first principal components of the genotype matrix capture its main axes of ancestry (page 7). Adding the leading components (typically 10–20) as covariates removes the part of each SNP’s association that is explained by ancestry along those axes11. The reading describes an iterative use: compute components to find and remove outliers, recompute them, then include them in the regression1. Components capture broad ancestry well, but not fine-scale structure or relatedness, and residual stratification can survive them.

Linear mixed models

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{x}_s \beta_s + \mathbf{g} + \mathbf{e} \\[4pt] \mathbf{g} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma^2_{\mathrm{A}} \boldsymbol{\psi}) \\ \mathbf{e} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma^2_{\mathrm{e}} \mathbf{I}) \end{gathered} \]
\(\mathbf{y}\)
Vector of the \(N\) people’s trait values.
\(\mathbf{W}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}\)
Covariate matrix (\(N\) rows, one column per covariate plus a column of 1s for the intercept) and its coefficients: fixed effects.
\(\mathbf{x}_s, \beta_s\)
Genotypes of the \(N\) people at the tested SNP \(s\), and the SNP’s effect, also a fixed effect.
\(\mathbf{g}\)
Random effect: each person’s polygenic background, the summed effects of all other SNPs.
\(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{A}}\)
Additive genetic variance of the trait captured by the SNPs.
\(\boldsymbol{\psi}\)
Genetic relationship matrix (\(N \times N\)): entry \((i, k)\) is the genome-wide genetic similarity of persons \(i\) and \(k\), about 0.5 for full siblings and near 0 for unrelated people.
\(\mathbf{e}, \sigma^2_{\mathrm{e}}, \mathbf{I}\)
Residuals, their variance, and the identity matrix: residuals are independent between people.

The model says that the traits of two people are correlated in proportion to their genetic similarity: \(\Cov(y_i, y_k) = \sigma^2_{\mathrm{A}} \psi_{ik}\) for \(i \ne k\). Relatives have large \(\psi_{ik}\), and people of the same ancestry have many small positive entries, so the model absorbs both close relatedness and population structure without removing anyone. Because the polygenic background is modeled, the residual variance is smaller and the test of \(\beta_s\) is more powerful1, 12. If SNP \(s\) itself is part of \(\boldsymbol{\psi}\), the model partly explains the SNP’s effect away; leave-one-chromosome-out (LOCO) analysis builds \(\boldsymbol{\psi}\), or the polygenic prediction, from the other 21 chromosomes12, 13. These are equations (1)–(3) of the reading.

Fitting an \(N \times N\) matrix for every SNP is slow for biobanks, and a series of programs made mixed models fast enough. GEMMA fits the exact model14. BOLT-LMM uses a Bayesian prior that lets a few SNPs have larger effects and iterative algorithms that avoid building \(\boldsymbol{\psi}\), which increases power in large cohorts13. fastGWA uses a sparse relationship matrix that keeps only the entries of relatives15. REGENIE first fits a ridge regression of the trait on blocks of SNPs from the whole genome and turns it into LOCO predictions (step 1), then tests each SNP with that prediction as an offset (step 2)16.

Binary traits add a difficulty. In a biobank, a disease may have 1 case per 100 people or fewer, and for SNPs of low frequency the normal approximation behind the Wald and score tests then gives p-values that are far too small. SAIGE fits a logistic mixed model and computes p-values with the saddle-point approximation, which stays accurate when cases and controls are very unbalanced17; REGENIE offers the same approximation or Firth’s penalized logistic regression16. In ascertained case–control samples, adjusting for covariates that strongly predict disease can lower power rather than raise it3.

Pitfall A Q–Q plot that hugs the diagonal does not prove there is no confounding

Principal components remove the confounding along the axes they capture. Structure at a finer scale, or recent relatedness, can leave biases too small to see in \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) but large enough to matter when thousands of SNPs are summed, as in a polygenic score or a test for selection. Within-family designs, which compare siblings or transmitted and untransmitted alleles, are immune to this (page 8; page 10).

Summary statistics, meta-analysis and replication Core

Summary statistics: the output of a GWAS

The rows written by the association software, one per variant, are the summary statistics. They hold no individual-level data, so they can be shared and combined far more easily than genotypes. The reading lists the minimum content: variant identifiers, positions with their genome build, the alleles and strand, the effect size and its standard error, the p-value, the test statistic, the minor allele frequency and the sample size1. The GWAS Catalog’s standard format (GWAS-SSF) makes eight columns mandatory, in a fixed order, and encourages seven more18:

Each row answers the same questions: which variant (chromosome, position and build), which allele was counted (effect_allele), how large the effect is and how precisely it is known (odds_ratio or beta, standard_error), and how surprising it would be without an effect (p_value). The standard error is what a meta-analysis needs; the allele columns are what makes two studies comparable, because the same variant can be reported with the other allele as effect allele and an inverted odds ratio (Lecture 0 · strands in SNP data). A full set has one row per tested variant: about 10 million rows for an imputed GWAS, roughly 1 GB as plain text before compression.

Meta-analysis

Most GWAS findings come from consortia, such as the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium, GIANT (body size) and the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium, that combine many cohorts1. The reading lists the steps: every cohort follows the same analysis plan with harmonized phenotypes, reports its results in a standard format, at least two analysts check each cohort’s results against a predefined quality-control protocol (with pipelines such as RICOPILI or EasyQC), and only then are the summary statistics combined1, 19. METAL and GWAMA are the common programs; METAL can weight cohorts by sample size or by inverse variance20, 21.

\[ \begin{gathered} \hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IVW}} = \frac{\sum_k w_k \hat{\beta}_k}{\sum_k w_k}, \quad w_k = \frac{1}{\SE_k^2} \\[4pt] \SE(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IVW}}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_k w_k}} \end{gathered} \]
\(k\)
Index of the cohorts, \(k = 1, \dots, K\).
\(\hat{\beta}_k, \SE_k\)
Cohort \(k\)’s estimate (here the log odds ratio for the same effect allele) and its standard error.
\(w_k\)
Weight of cohort \(k\): the inverse of its sampling variance.
\(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IVW}}\)
Inverse-variance-weighted (fixed-effect) estimate of the common effect.

Each cohort counts in proportion to its precision, and precisions add: in the figure the weights sum to 2,348, so \(\SE(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IVW}}) = 1/\sqrt{2{,}348} = 0.021\), smaller than the 0.035 of the largest cohort. The combined log odds ratio is 0.096 (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.06–1.15), \(z = 4.6\) and p ≈ 4 × 10⁻⁶: convincing evidence of a direction, but still short of 5 × 10⁻⁸, so more cohorts are needed. The fixed-effect model assumes one true effect shared by all cohorts; Lecture 0 shows why these weights give the most precise unbiased average (Lecture 0 · meta-analysis).

\[ \begin{gathered} Q = \sum_k w_k \big(\hat{\beta}_k - \hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IVW}}\big)^2 \\[4pt] I^2 = \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{Q - (K - 1)}{Q}\right) \end{gathered} \]
\(Q\)
Cochran’s heterogeneity statistic: the weighted sum of squared deviations of the cohort estimates from the combined one. Without heterogeneity it follows a chi-square distribution with \(K - 1\) degrees of freedom.
\(K - 1\)
The expected value of \(Q\) when all cohorts share one true effect.
\(I^2\)
Share of the variation between cohort estimates that reflects true differences in effect rather than chance, from 0 to 122.

Each term is a cohort’s squared distance from the combined estimate measured in its own standard errors, so with no true differences each term is about 1 on average and \(Q \approx K - 1\). In the figure \(Q = 3.9\) on 5 degrees of freedom, less than its expectation, so \(I^2 = 0\). Had \(Q\) been 15, \(I^2 = (15 - 5)/15 = 67\%\): two-thirds of the spread would be real. A random-effects meta-analysis then lets the true effect vary between cohorts, which widens the confidence interval1. Heterogeneity can come from differences in phenotype definition, ancestry (LD and allele frequencies), environment or analysis.

Replication

An association is accepted when it replicates: the same allele has an effect in the same direction, of compatible size, in people who were not part of the discovery1. The replication sample must be independent, with no shared or related people, and large enough to have power for the effect that is likely to be true, which is usually smaller than the discovery estimate because of the winner’s curse (page 10). The reading asks that effect sizes and their standard errors be compared, especially when different software analyzed the two samples, and that replication be planned from the start1. When discovery and replication samples are both available, analyzing them jointly is more powerful than requiring significance in each23.

In the data Combining files means aligning alleles first

Before two sets of summary statistics are combined, each variant must be matched on chromosome, position and build, and the effect alleles aligned: if one file counts C and the other G at a C/G SNP, the effect must be flipped (\(\hat{\beta} \to -\hat{\beta}\), \(\mathrm{OR} \to 1/\mathrm{OR}\)) or, for strand-ambiguous SNPs, resolved with allele frequencies or dropped. The Session 2 practical meta-analyzes breast-cancer summary statistics from three ancestry strata with METASOFT (Lecture 0 · meta-analysis).

From a locus to causal variants: fine-mapping Core

Every tower in a Manhattan plot contains many significant SNPs, most of them significant only because they are in LD with the causal variant; whether a correlated SNP passes the threshold depends on its LD with the causal variant and on the strength of the causal association1. The reading calls these clusters genomic risk loci. Three steps reduce a locus to a short list.

Lead variants and clumping. The lead variant is the most significant SNP of a locus. Clumping groups the significant SNPs around each lead variant by LD and distance, giving one row per signal (Lecture 0 · pruning and clumping). The lead variant is often not the causal one: when a locus holds two signals, their combination can make a neighboring SNP the most significant, and differences in imputation quality between SNPs in LD make the test statistics fluctuate1.

Conditional analysis. Adding the lead variant to the regression as a covariate asks whether any other SNP in the locus is still associated once the lead variant’s effect is accounted for. Repeating this, adding the most significant remaining SNP each time until none passes the threshold, is stepwise conditional analysis; it finds secondary signals but depends on the order of selection1. GCTA-COJO does the same from summary statistics and an LD reference panel24.

Bayesian fine-mapping. CAVIAR, FINEMAP, PAINTOR and SuSiE give each SNP a posterior probability of being causal, starting from a prior probability and updating it with the association statistics and the LD between SNPs25, 26, 27, 28. The simplest version assumes one causal variant per locus and uses Wakefield’s approximate Bayes factor29, 30.

\[ \begin{gathered} \ln \mathrm{ABF}_j = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(1 - r_j) + \tfrac{1}{2} r_j z_j^2 \\[4pt] r_j = \frac{W}{V_j + W} \end{gathered} \]
\(z_j\)
Wald statistic of SNP \(j\) in the locus, from the summary statistics.
\(V_j\)
Sampling variance of SNP \(j\)’s effect estimate, \(\SE_j^2\).
\(W\)
Prior variance of a true effect: how large causal effects are expected to be (for log odds ratios often \(0.2^2 = 0.04\)).
\(r_j\)
Share of the estimate’s variance that comes from the true effect, between 0 and 1; close to 1 in a large study, where \(V_j\) is small.
\(\mathrm{ABF}_j\)
Approximate Bayes factor: how many times more probable the data are if SNP \(j\) is the causal variant than if the locus has no effect.

The Bayes factor grows exponentially with \(z_j^2/2\), so small differences in \(z\) become large differences in support: \(z = 8\) against \(z = 7.5\) gives a ratio of \(e^{(64 - 56.25)/2} = e^{3.9} \approx 48\). The first term, \(\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(1 - r_j)\), penalizes SNPs whose estimates are imprecise. SNPs in perfect LD have the same \(z\) and therefore the same Bayes factor: the data cannot choose between them.

\[ \mathrm{PIP}_j = \frac{\mathrm{ABF}_j}{\sum_{k} \mathrm{ABF}_k} \]
\(\mathrm{PIP}_j\)
Posterior inclusion probability of SNP \(j\): the probability that it is the causal variant, given the data and the assumptions.
\(\sum_k \mathrm{ABF}_k\)
Sum of the Bayes factors of all SNPs in the locus.

Under the assumptions that the locus has exactly one causal variant and that every SNP is a priori equally likely to be it, Bayes’ rule makes each SNP’s posterior probability its share of the total Bayes factor; the PIPs sum to 1. Two SNPs in perfect LD and far ahead of the rest get 0.5 each. A 95% credible set is built by adding SNPs in order of decreasing PIP until their PIPs sum to at least 0.95: it contains the causal variant with probability 0.95 if the assumptions hold30. Annotations such as “in a coding exon” or “in an enhancer” can raise a SNP’s prior instead of using equal priors27.

Try itFine-map a locus: posterior inclusion probabilities and the 95% credible set

What to notice. In the default sample the lead SNP is SNP 12 (p ≈ 4 × 10⁻¹²), it has a PIP of 0.73, and the 95% credible set holds SNPs 11, 12 and 13. Tick “Reveal the causal SNP”: it is SNP 12. Now raise the LD between SNP 12 and SNP 13 to 0.99: the lead SNP becomes SNP 13 and SNP 12’s PIP falls to 0.35. Press “New sample” many times: the lead SNP is not the causal one in about 45% of samples, yet the credible set contains the causal SNP in about 99%. Set the LD back to 0.9 and lower the signal to an expected \(z\) of 4: the credible set grows to five or more SNPs, and the lead SNP is the causal one only about half the time. LD limits what statistics alone can resolve; data with different LD (other ancestries) or functional evidence are needed to break the tie.

Real fine-mapping is less tidy. Credible sets from different Bayesian methods often disagree, especially at loci with several independent signals, and power to find the right set falls as the number of signals grows1, 28. In 67,852 people genotyped densely at 94 inflammatory bowel disease loci, Huang et al. pinned 18 associations to a single causal variant with more than 95% certainty and 27 more with more than 50% certainty31. Resolution improves with denser variants, from imputation panels built from whole genomes (gnomAD and TOPMed held more than 10,000 and 90,000 genomes when the reading was written1), and with meta-analyses across ancestries, whose different LD patterns narrow each signal1 (Lecture 0 · LD across ancestries). Some regions need special methods: the HLA region on chromosome 6, with its extreme diversity and LD, is imputed with dedicated reference panels and software such as SNP2HLA and HIBAG32, 33 (Lecture 0 · the MHC), and structural variants and short tandem repeats are not always captured by current sequencing1.

From variants to genes and pathways Core

A credible set is a list of variants, not a mechanism. The reading describes the full dissection of a locus as finding the immediate effect of the causal variant (on a protein or on an enhancer), the gene or genes that mediate the association, the pathways downstream, and the tissue, cell type and cell state in which all of this happens; at the time, this was known for only a few loci, such as FTO and SORT11. Fig. 3 of the reading arranges the approaches as five questions.

Which gene?

Coding variants. For the 2–3% of GWAS loci fine-mapped to protein-changing variants, annotation tools such as ANNOVAR and the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) report the effect on the protein1, 34, 35. Most fine-mapped variants lie outside coding sequence and have unknown regulatory effects; the causal gene is often the nearest one, but not always1.

Molecular QTLs and colocalization. A molecular quantitative trait locus (molQTL) is a variant associated with a molecular measurement: gene expression (eQTL), splicing (sQTL), chromatin accessibility or DNA methylation. In its 2020 analysis of release V8, the GTEx Consortium mapped eQTLs and sQTLs from 15,201 RNA-sequencing samples of 49 tissues from 838 donors36 (later releases are larger; Lecture 0's genes page gives the V10 numbers); eQTLGen mapped eQTLs in blood from 31,684 people and found cis-eQTLs (near the gene) for 88% of genes37; the eQTL Catalogue reprocesses many published datasets uniformly38. Because any SNP in LD with a causal eQTL is itself associated with expression, an overlap of a GWAS hit and an eQTL is not enough: colocalization tests whether the two signals share one causal variant39. Transcriptome-wide association studies go one step further, predicting each gene’s expression from genotypes and testing the prediction against the trait (page 1 shows PrediXcan and FUSION)1. Molecular QTL catalogs lack many tissues, cell types and molecular traits, so these gene assignments are less certain than those of coding variants1.

Chromatin contacts and experiments. An enhancer can regulate a gene hundreds of kilobases away by folding the DNA into a loop. Chromosome conformation capture methods (3C, 4C, 5C and genome-wide Hi-C) measure which stretches of DNA touch, and can link an enhancer variant to its target promoter1, 40. Massively parallel reporter assays test thousands of DNA sequences, each carrying one allele, for their ability to drive expression in a single experiment41, and CRISPR editing introduces a variant, or disables an enhancer, in cells. These experiments are not confounded by LD, but cultured cells do not always behave like cells in the body1.

Which pathways and cell types?

The many loci of a polygenic trait converge on fewer biological processes. Gene-set analysis asks whether the genes near associated SNPs are enriched in a predefined set, such as a pathway, a tissue’s highly expressed genes or a protein network; MAGMA and DEPICT are common tools, and the result depends on how the sets were defined1, 42, 43. Trans-eQTLs, which link a variant to genes far away or on other chromosomes, and large perturbation screens followed by cellular phenotyping add further evidence1, 37. All of this depends on context: data from bulk tissue average over cell types and can hide a signal from a rare one, so single-cell and cell-type-specific data are expected to sharpen interpretation1.

Story Two loci followed all the way

SORT1 and LDL cholesterol. GWAS found a locus on chromosome 1p13 strongly associated with LDL cholesterol and with myocardial infarction. Musunuru et al. showed that a common noncoding SNP there, rs12740374, creates a binding site for the transcription factor C/EBP and changes how much of the gene SORT1 the liver expresses, and, by lowering and raising Sort1 in mouse liver, that its protein changes blood LDL cholesterol by changing how much lipoprotein the liver secretes44.

FTO and obesity. The FTO region holds the strongest common genetic association with obesity, but in fat-cell precursors FTO itself turned out not to be the target. Claussnitzer et al. traced the signal to the SNP rs1421085, whose risk allele disrupts a binding site for the repressor ARID5B, activates an enhancer in adipocyte precursor cells and doubles expression of two other genes, IRX3 and IRX5, shifting developing fat cells from energy-burning to energy-storing45.

Key points
  • Each SNP gets one regression: linear for a quantitative trait, logistic for a disease, with covariates. It reports \(\hat{\beta}\) (or the odds ratio \(e^{\hat{\beta}}\)), its standard error and the p-value of \(z = \hat{\beta}/\SE(\hat{\beta})\).
  • About a million effective tests in European-ancestry genomes set the threshold at p < 5 × 10⁻⁸; sequencing studies and African-ancestry samples need stricter thresholds.
  • Manhattan plots show where the signals are; Q–Q plots, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) and the LD score regression intercept show whether the bulk of the tests is inflated, and whether by confounding or by polygenic signal.
  • Ancestry and relatedness are confounders. Principal components and linear mixed models (with LOCO, and with saddle-point or Firth corrections for unbalanced binary traits) remove most of their effect.
  • Summary statistics, one row per variant, are the shareable output; inverse-variance meta-analysis combines cohorts, \(Q\) and \(I^2\) measure heterogeneity, and independent replication confirms findings.
  • A significant locus is a block of correlated SNPs. Conditional analysis finds independent signals; Bayesian fine-mapping assigns posterior inclusion probabilities and credible sets; molecular QTLs, colocalization, chromatin maps, experiments and gene-set tests link variants to genes and pathways.

Check your understanding

A SNP with effect-allele frequency 0.2 is tested in 5,000 people for a standardized quantitative trait. Roughly what standard error do you expect for \(\hat{\beta}\), and how large must the true effect be for \(z\) to be centered at the genome-wide critical value 5.45?

\(\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \approx N \cdot 2p(1-p) = 5{,}000 \times 0.32 = 1{,}600\), so \(\SE(\hat{\beta}) \approx 1/\sqrt{1{,}600} = 0.025\). The expected \(z\) is \(\beta/0.025\), which equals 5.45 when \(\beta \approx 0.136\) standard deviations per allele. Such an effect would be detected only half the time; 80% power needs \(\beta/0.025 \approx 6.29\), or \(\beta \approx 0.157\).

A logistic regression reports \(\hat{\beta} = -0.105\) with standard error 0.025 for allele T. What are the odds ratio and its 95% confidence interval, and what would a study counting the other allele report?

\(\widehat{\mathrm{OR}} = e^{-0.105} = 0.90\), with 95% CI \(e^{-0.105 \pm 0.049}\), from 0.86 to 0.95; \(z = -4.2\). Counting the other allele changes the sign of \(\hat{\beta}\) to \(+0.105\), so the odds ratio becomes \(1/0.90 = 1.11\) (CI 1.05 to 1.17), with the same standard error and p-value. Summary statistics must therefore always say which allele is the effect allele.

A GWAS of 400,000 biobank participants has \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = 1.25\). Is the study confounded? What would you compute to find out?

Not necessarily. For a polygenic trait, true signal spread over thousands of SNPs raises the median statistic, and the more so the larger \(N\) is. LD score regression separates the two: if the intercept is close to 1 (say 1.02) while the mean \(\chi^2\) is well above it, most of the inflation is polygenic signal; an intercept well above 1 points to confounding or cryptic relatedness that the principal components and mixed model did not remove.

Why does a linear mixed model let a GWAS keep relatives in the analysis, and why does it use leave-one-chromosome-out (LOCO)?

The random effect with covariance \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{A}}\boldsymbol{\psi}\) models the extra similarity of relatives’ traits, so their correlated residuals no longer inflate the test; removing relatives would instead throw away data. If the tested SNP, or SNPs in LD with it, also contribute to \(\boldsymbol{\psi}\) or to the polygenic prediction, the random effect absorbs part of the SNP’s own effect and the test loses power. LOCO builds the background from the chromosomes other than the one being tested.

Three cohorts report log odds ratios 0.12, 0.08 and 0.20 with standard errors 0.04, 0.02 and 0.10. What is the fixed-effect estimate and its standard error?

The weights are \(1/0.04^2 = 625\), \(1/0.02^2 = 2{,}500\) and \(1/0.10^2 = 100\), summing to 3,225. \(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IVW}} = (625 \times 0.12 + 2{,}500 \times 0.08 + 100 \times 0.20)/3{,}225 = 295/3{,}225 = 0.091\), and \(\SE = 1/\sqrt{3{,}225} = 0.018\), so \(z = 5.1\). The second cohort dominates because its standard error is half that of the first.

Two SNPs in a locus have \(r^2 = 1\) with each other and the strongest associations. What can fine-mapping say about which one is causal, and what could break the tie?

With perfect LD the two SNPs have the same \(z\) and the same Bayes factor, so with equal priors each gets a posterior inclusion probability of about 0.5 and both are in the credible set. Statistics on this sample cannot separate them. A population in which the two SNPs are less correlated (a multi-ancestry analysis), a functional prior (one SNP in an enhancer active in a relevant cell type, the other not), or an experiment such as a reporter assay or a CRISPR edit can.

A GWAS lead SNP is also the strongest eQTL for a nearby gene in liver. Is that enough to name the gene as causal?

No. Any SNP in LD with a causal eQTL is associated with expression, so the GWAS signal and the eQTL signal could come from two different causal variants that happen to be correlated. A colocalization analysis tests whether one variant explains both; even then the eQTL might act in a tissue that is irrelevant to the disease, or the variant might affect several genes. Evidence from the right tissue and an experimental perturbation make the case stronger.
