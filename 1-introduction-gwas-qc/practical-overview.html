The practical: quality control of GWAS data with PLINK

A genome-wide association study (GWAS) tests hundreds of thousands of SNPs, positions where people's DNA differs by one letter, for a link with a disease. Before any test, the analyst checks that the genotype calls and the people are what the files claim; this is quality control (QC). Session 1's practical runs eight QC steps on 1,401 people from the PennCATH study of coronary artery disease, with PLINK, R and GrafAnc. This page explains why QC comes first, describes the real data and the lab's toy data, maps the eight steps and the files they pass along, and shows how to work through them on this site.

Mental model

Quality control is eight checks, each the same five-stage loop

Each QC step computes one statistic for every SNP or every person, looks at its distribution, chooses a threshold, removes or flags what lies beyond it, and records the decision. The practical applies this loop eight times, and the files each step writes are what the next step reads.

  • Why first. A small genotyping error that differs between cases and controls produces an association as convincing as a real one, and the larger the study, the more convincing it becomes.
  • The data. PennCATH: 1,401 people who had coronary angiography, genotyped at 861,473 SNPs, distributed as a PLINK fileset.
  • The toy data. A small simulated study with the same file format, used by the lab on every step page.
  • Eight steps. Missingness, Hardy–Weinberg, LD pruning, relatedness, heterozygosity, PCA, reference PCA and GrafAnc: each a statistic, a threshold and an action.
  • A chain of files. Each output name records the filters already applied, so the file names tell you where you are in the pipeline.
  • How to work through it. In the browser on the toy data, or on your own computer with the real data; the commands are the same.

Why quality control comes before association testing Core

A GWAS compares, SNP by SNP, how often each allele occurs in people with the disease (cases) and without it (controls), or how a measured trait changes with the number of copies of an allele. The test takes the genotype calls at face value. Genotype calling, described in Lecture 0 · Genotype calling, makes errors that are invisible one call at a time: a degraded DNA sample gives many missing calls; a SNP whose intensity clusters overlap is miscalled in some people; a sample contaminated with another person's DNA looks heterozygous at too many SNPs; the same person sampled twice looks like two people. Relatives, and people of different ancestry, violate the assumption that participants are independent draws from one population. QC looks for the statistical trace of each of these problems in the whole genotype matrix and removes or flags the SNPs and people responsible.1,2

Errors do damage in two ways. Random errors that hit cases and controls alike blur the genotypes and cost power. Errors that differ between cases and controls, called differential errors, are worse: they create allele-frequency differences that the test cannot tell apart from a real effect. In the words of the GENEVA consortium's report on GWAS quality control, "even small sources of systematic or random error can cause spurious results or obscure real effects".3

How a small bias becomes a significant result

Take a SNP with no real association and an allele frequency \(\bar{p}\) in everyone. Suppose a genotyping problem that affects only the cases, for example cases processed in a different laboratory, shifts their measured allele frequency by \(\Delta\). With \(n\) cases and \(n\) controls, the allelic test statistic is approximately

\[ z^2 = \frac{n\,\Delta^2}{\bar{p}\,(1 - \bar{p})} \]
\(z^2\)
The allelic test statistic: the square of \(z\), the difference between the case and control allele frequencies divided by its standard error. It is large when the frequencies differ by more than chance allows; the p-value is the probability that a standard normal variable lies further from 0 than \(z\). The formula gives the statistic's value when each group's estimated frequency equals its expected value; sampling noise moves the statistic of any one study around it.
\(n\)
The number of people in each group (equal numbers of cases and controls); each person contributes two allele copies.
\(\bar{p}\)
The allele frequency, the same in cases and controls because the SNP has no real effect; between 0 and 1.
\(\Delta\)
The error in the cases' measured allele frequency caused by the genotyping problem, as a difference in frequency (0.01 = one percentage point).

Each group's allele frequency is estimated from \(2n\) allele copies, with variance \(\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})/(2n)\); the difference between the two estimates has variance \(\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})/n\), and \(z\) is \(\Delta\) divided by its square root. The bias \(\Delta\) does not shrink as the study grows, but the standard error does, as \(1/\sqrt{n}\), so \(z^2\) grows in proportion to \(n\). If the error hits cases and controls equally, \(\Delta = 0\) and there is no false signal at all, only noise. With \(\bar{p} = 0.30\) and an error of one percentage point (\(\Delta = 0.01\)):

An error of one percentage point is undetectable at 1,000 people per group and reaches the genome-wide significance threshold (a p-value below 5 × 10⁻⁸) at about 62,400. Conversely, with 500 people per group the error would have to be about 11 percentage points to reach that threshold, large enough for QC to catch. Large studies therefore need cleaner data, not less clean, and the most dangerous errors are those that line up with case–control status: different laboratories, arrays, plates or DNA sources for cases and controls.

Story Two laboratories, one inflated test

In 2005 David Clayton and colleagues in Cambridge genotyped 6,322 SNPs in 816 people with type 1 diabetes and 877 population controls from Great Britain. Across all SNPs the association statistics were inflated by 11.2%, more than chance allows. Population structure, differences in ancestry between cases and controls, explained part of it. The rest came from "differential bias in genotype scoring between case and control DNA samples, which originated from two laboratories, causing false-positive associations".4 Genotype scoring means genotype calling: the case DNA and the control DNA came from different laboratories, and the calls differed subtly between the two sets.

Story One weak sample can change its neighbors' genotypes

Genotype calling is often done one plate (batch) of samples at a time, and the calling algorithm learns where the intensity clusters lie from the samples it is given. In the Genetics of Kidneys in Diabetes (GoKinD) study, Pluzhnikov and colleagues showed that "even a few DNA samples of marginally acceptable quality can profoundly alter the allele calls for other samples on the plate". The result was a bias in allele frequencies between plates, which can produce false associations when cases and controls are not randomized to plates. A standard battery of QC checks was not enough to detect it; the authors detected and corrected it with additional information, including the raw signal intensities.5

Story A longevity signature that came from the arrays

In July 2010 a GWAS of 1,055 centenarians and 1,267 controls reported a model of 150 SNPs that predicted exceptional longevity with 77% accuracy.6 Other geneticists soon pointed to how the samples had been genotyped: the first 90% of the centenarians on Illumina's HumanCNV370 array, the last 10% on its newer 610-Quad array, and the controls on four different arrays. When the mix of arrays differs between cases and controls, any SNP that one array reads differently looks like an association, and critics said that this had happened for the two most strongly associated SNPs.7 The paper was retracted in 2011, after the authors found that "technical errors in the Illumina 610 array and an inadequate quality control protocol introduced false positive" SNPs.8 A reanalysis of 801 centenarians and 914 genetically matched controls, with stricter quality control, was published in 2012.9

Published QC protocols turn these lessons into routine checks on samples (missing calls, heterozygosity, sex, relatedness, ancestry) and on SNPs (missing calls, Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, allele frequency, differential missingness), run before association testing, with thresholds chosen and reported.1,2 The practical runs the core of such a protocol; what it leaves out is listed at the end of this page.

The data: 1,401 people from the PennCATH study Core

PennCATH is a hospital-based study of genes and biomarkers for coronary artery disease (CAD) at the University of Pennsylvania. Between July 1998 and March 2003 it recruited 3,815 consecutive patients undergoing cardiac catheterization.10 During catheterization a thin tube is passed through a blood vessel to the heart and contrast dye is injected; X-ray images of the coronary arteries (coronary angiography) then show how much each vessel is narrowed. The narrowing, called stenosis, is given as the percentage by which the vessel's inner diameter is reduced. From these patients the investigators drew a nested case–control sample of 1,401 patients, all recorded as white, for a GWAS:10

  • Cases (933): at least one coronary vessel narrowed by 50% or more, at an early age: men 60 or younger, women 65 or younger.
  • Controls (468): less than 10% narrowing in every vessel, in men older than 40 and women older than 45.

Defining cases and controls from angiograms, rather than from a diagnosis in a medical record, measures coronary atherosclerosis directly; Lecture 0 discusses why this matters in Case definitions. PennCATH was one of the discovery studies of a 2011 GWAS of coronary artery disease and myocardial infarction.10 Its genotypes, measured on the Affymetrix Genome-Wide Human SNP Array 6.0, were later released for teaching with a tutorial paper on GWAS analysis.11

Populations and allele frequencies

Each simulated person belongs to a population, and each population has its own allele frequency at every SNP: the share of its allele copies that carry a given allele. The frequencies come from a tree of populations. An ancestral population splits into an African branch and an out-of-Africa branch; the out-of-Africa branch splits into a West Eurasian branch, which gives the European and South Asian populations, and an East Asian branch. The tips of the tree are named after 1000 Genomes populations: CEU, GBR and TSI (European), YRI and LWK (African), CHB, JPT and CHS (East Asian), and GIH, PJL and BEB (South Asian).

Along each branch the allele frequencies change at random. In real populations this change is genetic drift, which comes from each generation passing on only a finite sample of its parents' alleles. The simulation draws each population's frequency from its parent population's with the Balding–Nichols model: from a beta distribution whose mean is the parent's frequency \(p\) and whose variance is \(F\,p(1-p)\), where \(F\) is the amount of drift on that branch (a different quantity from the F of step 5). A larger \(F\) moves frequencies further from the parent's.

How far apart two populations' allele frequencies are is summarized by the fixation index, \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\): the fraction of the variance in allele frequency that lies between the populations rather than within them. It is 0 when the frequencies are identical and 1 when each population carries only one allele, a different one in each (Population structure · Measuring the differences). The drifts were chosen so that \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) between the simulated African, European and East Asian groups is close to values reported for the 1000 Genomes super-populations, while the three European-like populations differ only slightly from each other.

Some populations are mixtures. The South Asian branch takes part of its frequencies from a branch related to East Asians, following a model of South Asian history in which a West-Eurasian-related population mixed with a deeply diverged one. This places South-Asian-like people about halfway between European-like and East-Asian-like people; in real 1000 Genomes data, South Asians lie closer to Europeans. The admixed study people carry stretches of chromosome inherited from an African-like and a European-like population (admixture). Most of them descend from several generations of mixing and have different African-like shares; the first-generation admixed person has one parent from each population. The panel's admixed American populations are built the same way, from European-like, African-like and Native-American-like branches.

Genotyping arrays carry SNPs that were first discovered in a limited set of people and populations. Array SNPs therefore tend to be common in several populations, and their allele frequencies are shifted toward intermediate values: this is SNP ascertainment bias.16 The simulation imitates it by keeping a candidate SNP only if its minor allele frequency is high enough, both averaged over the African, European and East Asian branches and in the European branch. So the toy study has few SNPs with MAF below 0.05 and no monomorphic SNPs.

Haplotypes and linkage disequilibrium

Alleles at nearby SNPs are correlated because they are inherited together on the same stretch of chromosome. This correlation is linkage disequilibrium (LD); step 3 lists a subset of SNPs with little LD between them, for the steps that need nearly independent SNPs. To create it, part of the SNPs sit in blocks a few hundred kilobases (kb) long, with neighboring SNPs a few kb apart; the other SNPs are isolated, far from any other SNP. Without the blocks, SNPs spread evenly over the genome would hardly be correlated, and step 3 would have little to do.

Each block starts from a small set of founder haplotypes: ancestral versions of that stretch of chromosome, each with its own string of alleles. Their alleles come from coalescent trees, genealogies of the founder haplotypes that change along the block. A SNP is a mutation on one branch of the local tree, so every founder below that branch carries the new allele. Each simulated chromosome copy is then built from segments copied from the founders, in the way of the Li–Stephens model: it copies one founder for a stretch, then switches to another, choosing founders with its population's drifted founder frequencies. The switches come at shorter distances in African-like populations, so LD extends over shorter distances there, as in real data (LD differs between populations).

One region imitates a large inversion, a segment of chromosome that occurs in two orientations. Chromosome copies with different orientations do not recombine within the segment, so most of its SNPs track the orientation and stay correlated over millions of bases. Such long-range LD regions can take over principal components unless the SNPs are pruned (Long-range LD regions).

Relatives

Relatives are made by simulated meiosis, the cell division that makes eggs and sperm. A parent passes on one copy of each chromosome, assembled from the parent's own two copies: at each crossover, placed at random along the chromosome, the transmitted copy switches from one of the parent's copies to the other. The simulation records, for every allele, which chromosome copy of the family's oldest simulated generation it came from. So for any two people it knows exactly the share of the genome they inherited from the same ancestral copy, their identity by descent (IBD), which is the quantity step 4 estimates. A relative's meioses were redrawn until the realized IBD was typical of the relationship rather than unusually high or low.

Two other kinds of planted people come from the same records. A duplicate is one simulated person entered twice under different IDs, with independent missing calls and errors. Inbreeding comes from parents who are related to each other: their child carries, in parts of the genome, two copies of the same ancestral stretch, which lowers the child's heterozygosity. Parents and grandparents are simulated but not included, and the parent columns of toy.fam are 0 for everyone, so relatives are hidden, as in PennCATH.

Genotype calls

Genotype calling turns the array's two intensity signals at a SNP into a genotype (Lecture 0 · Genotype calling). The simulation starts from each person's true genotypes and adds four kinds of problem.

Contamination. A sample's DNA can be mixed with another person's. The simulation models the array signal of such a mixture through the B allele frequency, the share of a SNP's signal that comes from allele B, which sits near 0, 0.5 and 1 for the genotypes AA, AB and BB:

\[ b = (1-\alpha)\,\frac{g_1}{2} + \alpha\,\frac{g_2}{2} + \varepsilon \]
\(b\)
The B allele frequency that the array measures for the mixed sample at one SNP; between 0 and 1, apart from noise.
\(\alpha\)
The contaminating person's share of the DNA in the sample; between 0 and 1, one value per sample.
\(g_1\)
The number of copies of allele B in the genotype of the person the sample belongs to: 0, 1 or 2.
\(g_2\)
The same count for the contaminating person.
\(\varepsilon\)
Measurement noise: a normal variable with mean 0 and a small standard deviation, drawn for each call.

Each person contributes their own B allele frequency, \(g/2\), in proportion to their share of the DNA. With \(\alpha = 0\) the sample gives \(g_1/2\) plus noise, a clean call, and wherever the two people have the same genotype the mixture gives the same value. Take for illustration \(\alpha = 0.3\) and a caller that calls any \(b\) between 0.2 and 0.8 heterozygous. A person who is AA (\(g_1 = 0\)) mixed with a BB contaminant (\(g_2 = 2\)) gives \(b \approx 0.3\) and is called AB; mixed with an AB contaminant, \(b \approx 0.15\), still AA. A heterozygous person stays heterozygous whatever the contaminant carries (\(b \approx 0.35\), \(0.5\) or \(0.65\)). So, at this \(\alpha\), contamination adds heterozygous calls at the SNPs where the contaminant carries the opposite homozygote and leaves the other calls unchanged.

The extra heterozygous calls are what step 5 detects. They have a second effect: the contaminated sample almost never carries the homozygote opposite to anyone else's, which step 4 reads as relatedness to everyone (Contamination: one sample related to everyone).

Miscalled clusters. At a few SNPs the simulated caller draws a cluster boundary in the wrong place. Either most true heterozygotes are called as the common homozygote, giving too few heterozygotes, or most common homozygotes are called heterozygous, giving too many; the second imitates a probe that also binds a second place in the genome, where everyone carries the minor allele. Either way the genotype counts no longer fit the Hardy–Weinberg proportions for the SNP's allele frequency, which is what step 2 tests (What a genotyping error does to the counts). These SNPs were chosen so that the distortion is strong in the controls, whose rows step 2 reads.

Random errors. Every call has a small chance of being replaced by a wrong genotype, and the chance is larger for degraded samples.

Missing calls. Whether a call is missing depends on both the person and the SNP:

\[ m_{ij} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\mu + u_i + v_j)}} \]
\(m_{ij}\)
The probability that the call of person \(i\) at SNP \(j\) is missing; between 0 and 1.
\(e\)
The base of the natural logarithm, about 2.718.
\(\mu\)
The base rate on the log-odds scale, \(\log(r/(1-r))\) for a missing rate \(r\) of a typical person at a typical SNP; one value for the whole study.
\(u_i\)
Person \(i\)'s offset, one for each person in the study: small and random for most people, large and positive for samples simulated as degraded (poor DNA), and moderately positive for samples simulated as low quality.
\(v_j\)
SNP \(j\)'s offset, one for each SNP: small and random for most SNPs, and large and positive for SNPs simulated with a high missing rate, as if their assay worked poorly.

The function \(1/(1+e^{-x})\) is the logistic function of logistic regression: it turns any number into a probability. The person and SNP offsets add on the log-odds scale, so a raised person offset costs far more calls at a poor SNP than at a good one. Take illustrative values, not the simulation's: a base rate of 0.1%, a weak sample with a person offset of 2, and poor SNPs with a SNP offset of 3. An ordinary person misses 0.1% of calls at ordinary SNPs and 2.0% at poor SNPs; the weak sample misses 0.73% and 12.9%. If a tenth of the SNPs were poor, the weak sample's overall missing rate would be \(0.9 \times 0.73\% + 0.1 \times 12.9\% = 1.95\%\), above the 1% threshold. After --geno 0.01 removes the poor SNPs, which miss 2% or more of their calls, it is 0.73%. This is why step 1 filters SNPs first (Why the order matters).

Phenotype and the causal SNP

Each person was first set as a case, a control or missing. One SNP was then made causal. Its effect is given by penetrance, the probability of disease given the genotype: the same for zero or one copy of the risk allele, and many times higher for two (a recessive model). The effect is far larger than any real CAD variant's, on purpose, so that a study of ? cases shows it. Each case's genotypes in the causal SNP's block were drawn by rejection sampling: a set of genotypes is drawn for the person's population and kept with probability equal to its penetrance, or else drawn again; a control's draw is kept with probability one minus the penetrance. This gives cases more risk alleles than controls, in exactly the proportions the model implies. Relatives inherit their genotypes by meiosis, so their status was drawn from their own genotype instead. Missing phenotypes were assigned without regard to genotype.

One consequence matters for step 2. Cases are enriched for people with two risk alleles, so among the cases the causal SNP has too few heterozygotes for its allele frequency, a departure from Hardy–Weinberg proportions caused by a real effect, while the controls are in equilibrium. This is why step 2 reads the controls' rows (Why QC tests the controls).

The reference panel

The panel's people are drawn from the same model as the study, with the same tree and founders, so the study's and the panel's allele frequencies agree. Its SNPs are common SNPs thinned so that no two nearby SNPs are strongly correlated, as in a file of markers chosen for PCA, and few of them lie in the inversion-like region. Some are A/T or C/G SNPs, which are strand-ambiguous: their two alleles are complementary bases, so the letters cannot show which strand a file uses. The study's .bim file, for its part, writes some of its SNPs on the reverse strand, each allele replaced by its complement, as data from some arrays do. Step 7 has to repair this before the two files can be merged (What goes wrong when two files meet).

Each step reads the file the last one wrote Core

PLINK names every file it writes after the --out prefix plus an extension that says what the file is, and the practical builds its prefixes so that a file's name records its history:

  • Each filter adds a suffix. penncath becomes penncath.geno0.01 after the SNP filter and penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01 after the person filter. A name that contains geno0.01.mind0.01 was made from data that passed step 1.
  • .LD0.3 marks analyses on the pruned SNPs. The relatedness, heterozygosity and PCA outputs carry it because they were computed only on the SNPs in .prune.in (pruned at r² 0.3).
  • The extension says which statistic. .imiss/.lmiss (missingness per person/per SNP), .hwe, .genome, .het, .eigenvec/.eigenval; their columns are explained on each step's page and in the PLINK file format reference.17
  • Only filesets are inputs to PLINK. The tables (.hwe, .het, …) are read by R for plots and by the reader; lists such as .prune.in and -merge.missnp go back into PLINK through --extract and --flip.

Because every step after step 1 reads the same fileset, the steps can be run in any order once step 1 is done, provided step 3 comes before steps 4–6. That is also why each step page can recreate what it needs from scratch in the lab.

What the practical does not do Optional

The practical itself says that its steps "illustrate core GWAS QC concepts but are not an exhaustive production QC protocol".13 Checks that a full protocol usually adds:1,2,3

  • Sex check. Compares recorded sex with sex estimated from X-chromosome heterozygosity (--check-sex) to find mix-ups of samples. Impossible here: the PennCATH files have no X chromosome.
  • Differential missingness. Tests whether a SNP's missing calls are more common in cases than in controls (--test-missing), one signature of differential errors like those in the stories above.
  • Batch and plate effects. Compares allele frequencies and missingness between genotyping batches or plates; needs batch information, which is not part of these files.5
  • Minor allele frequency filter. Removes rare SNPs (--maf), whose calls are less reliable and whose tests have little power; the practical applies --maf 0.05 only to the PCAs of steps 6 and 7.
  • Mendelian errors. In family data, genotypes impossible given the parents' genotypes (--mendel); PennCATH has no families.
  • Contamination estimates. Methods that estimate the fraction of another person's DNA in a sample, from array data alone or together with sequence reads, and detect contamination as low as 1%.18
  • Sample identity and duplicates against known genotypes from another assay, allele and genome-build harmonization before merging with other data, comparison of allele frequencies with an external reference, and, after imputation, post-imputation QC of imputation quality scores.
In the reading Uffelmann et al. 2021 · Experimentation › Data processing

The reading places quality control between genotyping and imputation. Its Figure 1, panel c, draws the stage as a plot of principal components in which "your data" sit among reference populations, the picture that step 7 makes. The text states that "generating reliable results from GWAS requires careful quality control" and gives example steps: removing rare or monomorphic variants, variants out of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium and SNPs missing in a fraction of people (steps 1 and 2), and comparing self-reported sex with sex inferred from the X and Y chromosomes (the sex check above).19 Its Table 1 lists PLINK/PLINK2 for these filters. Its section on population stratification describes PCA used first to find and exclude outliers and then to supply covariates for the association model; step 6 is the first of these uses.

Key points
  • Genotype errors that differ between cases and controls create false associations whose test statistic grows in proportion to sample size; QC exists to find them before association testing.
  • Every QC step is the same loop: compute a statistic per SNP or person, look at its distribution, choose a threshold, remove or flag, and record the decision.
  • The practical's eight steps: missingness (remove SNPs, then people, above 1%), Hardy–Weinberg in controls, LD pruning (r² 0.3), relatedness (PI_HAT), heterozygosity (±3 SD or |F| > 0.1), PCA, PCA with 1000 Genomes, and GrafAnc.
  • Only step 1 removes data; the other steps flag or list, and the exercise turns flags into removals.
  • File names record history: each filter adds a suffix, .LD0.3 marks analyses on pruned SNPs, and all later steps read penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.
  • The lab runs the same commands on a small simulated toy study whose problems were planted and recorded, so every answer can be checked; on the toy files its PLINK writes the same files as real PLINK 1.9.0. PennCATH's QC results come from running PLINK yourself; the step pages quote some of them so that you can check yours.

Check your understanding

Why does the practical remove SNPs with too many missing calls before it removes people?

A SNP that failed on the array is missing in many people at once. If people were filtered first, those bad SNPs would add to every person's missing-call count and could push good samples over the 1% threshold. Removing bad SNPs first means the person filter measures the quality of each DNA sample, not of the array's worst probes.

A genotyping problem shifts the cases' allele frequency by 0.005 at a SNP with \(\bar{p} = 0.2\) and no real effect. What is \(z^2\) with 50,000 people per group, and with 500,000?

\(z^2 = n\Delta^2 / (\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})) = 50{,}000 \times 0.005^2 / 0.16 = 7.8\), a p-value of about 0.005: suggestive but far from genome-wide significance. With 500,000 per group, \(z^2 = 78\), far beyond the threshold of 29.7. The error is the same; only the sample size changed.

Why does step 2 judge Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium in the controls rather than in everyone?

A SNP that truly affects the disease changes genotype frequencies among cases, who are selected for having the disease, so it can depart from Hardy–Weinberg proportions in cases for a real biological reason. Controls are closer to a sample of the population, so a departure there points to a calling problem rather than an association.

What does the name penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.LD0.3.het tell you about how the file was made?

It is a heterozygosity table (.het) computed on the fileset that passed both missingness filters of step 1 (geno0.01.mind0.01), using only the LD-pruned SNPs from step 3 (LD0.3, pruned at r² 0.3).

After running all eight steps, how many people and SNPs has the practical removed beyond step 1?

None from penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01, the fileset the exercise starts from. Steps 2–8 compute statistics, lists and plots; they flag SNPs (Hardy–Weinberg), pairs of people (relatedness) and people (heterozygosity, PCA, ancestry) but do not remove them. Step 7's --geno 0.1 drops SNPs only from the merged copy used for its PCA. The exercise asks you to choose thresholds and build the cleaned fileset with options such as --hwe and --remove, and the practical notes that a well-justified analysis may remove no one on some criteria.

What can the toy study teach that PennCATH cannot, and the other way round?

The toy study's problems were placed on purpose and are recorded, so you can check whether a step found them and see what each threshold catches; it is also small enough to run in a browser. PennCATH has real array errors that no one listed in advance, real ancestry and real size, so its results are the ones that resemble a real analysis, and you get them by running PLINK yourself. The two also differ in ways that change the numbers: --geno 0.01 removes about a third of PennCATH's SNPs but a small share of the toy's.
