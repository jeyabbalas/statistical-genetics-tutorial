Exercise: build a quality-controlled dataset

Steps 1–8 computed a statistic for every SNP, person or pair of people, but only step 1 removed anything. The practical's exercise asks you to turn the other statistics into decisions: choose a Hardy–Weinberg threshold, rules for heterozygosity and relatedness, and what to do with genetic-ancestry information; then make a cleaned fileset with PLINK. On this page you do that on the toy data, with shell tools for the lists and one PLINK command for the result, and you record a QC report that someone else could check line by line.

The practical's exercise plink --remove · --hwe · --make-bed Writes: toy.qc.bed, .bim, .fam ≈ 90 min
Mental model

Eight diagnoses, two kinds of filter, one command, one report

Each of the eight steps left one statistic per SNP, per person or per pair of people. The exercise fixes a rule for each statistic, turns the rules into two kinds of filter (a list of people for --remove and a p-value threshold for --hwe), applies both in one PLINK command to the fileset that step 1 left, and records what each rule removed, so that another analyst can reproduce every number.

  • Everything in one terminal. This page's lab starts from the raw toy data; its suggested commands are the practical's steps 1–8 (Task 1).
  • Rules first, results later. Each threshold is chosen from the QC statistics alone, before any association test, and justified (Tasks 2–5).
  • Lists of IDs, then one command. awk writes the IDs that fail into text files; plink --remove … --hwe … --make-bed applies them in PLINK's fixed order (Task 6).
  • A report someone else can check. Every count in it comes from a log line or a file, and a widget reads them from your files (Task 7).
  • What changes if you change the order. Four short experiments show why the practical's order and choices matter (what-if questions).

Tasks 2–5: choose the rules and list who fails Core

The practical's exercise, in its own words:1

1. Choose a HWE p-value threshold and justify your choice.
2. Choose and apply a threshold for excluding samples based on heterozygosity, and separately for relatedness. Note: a well-justified analysis may conclude that no samples need to be excluded on one or both of these criteria. 3. Optional: Consider whether the study design or analysis plan calls for flagging samples whose genetic-structure patterns differ from the main cohort. Do not exclude a sample solely because its GrafAnc label differs from the majority. […]
4. Use PLINK to produce a QC-filtered dataset (--hwe, --remove, etc. as appropriate) based on the criteria covered in this workshop.

Each task below states the decision, shows how to look at the statistic with the shell, and hides one worked solution, the one this page uses from then on. Try your own first. Whatever you choose, fix it from the QC statistics alone, before any association test: a threshold adjusted after seeing which SNPs are associated with the disease can be tuned, knowingly or not, toward the hoped-for result.

Task 2: a Hardy–Weinberg threshold

--hwe T removes every SNP whose exact-test p-value is below T. For case–control data PLINK 1.9 tests only the people it treats as controls, those coded 1 in column 6 of the .fam file (the UNAFF rows of the .hwe file),3 because, in the practical's words, "a genuine disease association can cause HWE departures among cases".1 What code 1 means in the real PennCATH files is less simple than "no disease": The data · What column 6 holds. Published thresholds range from 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻¹² (step 2 · Choose a threshold); the practical's own question counts SNPs below 10⁻⁸. Look at the controls' smallest p-values and count how many SNPs a few candidate thresholds would remove.

A solution
bash 
$ awk '$3 == "UNAFF"' ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.hwe | sort -g -k9,9 | head -5
$ awk '$3 == "UNAFF" && $9 < 1e-8' ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.hwe | wc -l
$ awk '$3 == "UNAFF" && $9 < 1e-6' ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.hwe | wc -l

In the .hwe file column 3 is TEST and column 9 is P. An awk pattern with no action prints the lines for which it is true, so the first command keeps the controls' rows and sort -g -k9,9 sorts them by p-value (-g understands numbers such as 3.1e-09); the other two count rows below 10⁻⁸ and 10⁻⁶. Look at the extreme rows: is the departure a shortfall or an excess of heterozygotes (O(HET) against E(HET))?

Choice used from here on: --hwe 1e-6 in controls, the threshold Marees et al. recommend for controls.5 It removes SNPs with gross departures, which serious genotyping errors produce, while genuine associations "can be expected to deviate slightly from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium", in the words of the PLINK documentation, and rarely reach so small a p-value.3 The filter is applied in Task 6, together with the removal of people, so the test is computed on the controls who remain. PLINK's documentation recommends the mid-p version of the test (the midp modifier; step 2 · The --hwe filter); this page uses the plain exact test, the one in the .hwe file, so that this count and Task 6's log measure the same thing.

In the lab, ? UNAFF rows are below 10⁻⁸ and ? below 10⁻⁶. They are the same SNPs, the ? genotyping artifacts planted in the toy data (?): ? have too few heterozygotes, because most of their heterozygous genotypes were called as homozygous, and ? (?) have too many. Below 10⁻³ there are ? rows: a looser threshold starts to remove SNPs that nobody planted, whose departures are chance.

Task 3: heterozygosity

Step 5's .het file gives, for each person, the number of pruned SNPs with a genotype and how many of them are homozygous. The practical turns them into a heterozygosity rate and flags people far from the average;1 the F column is a second measure that compares the observed number of homozygous genotypes with the number expected from the sample's allele frequencies.2 Why each departure arises, and why the two measures flag different people: step 5.

\[ h_i = \frac{N_i - O_i}{N_i} \] \[ F_i = \frac{O_i - E_i}{N_i - E_i} \]
\(N_i\)
Column N(NM): the number of pruned SNPs at which person \(i\) has a genotype.
\(O_i\)
Column O(HOM): how many of those genotypes are homozygous.
\(E_i\)
Column E(HOM): the number of homozygous genotypes expected for a person drawn at random from the sample, computed from the allele frequencies.
\(h_i\)
The heterozygosity rate: the fraction of the person's genotypes that are heterozygous.
\(F_i\)
Column F: the excess of homozygous genotypes over the expected number, as a fraction of the most it could be.

A sample that mixes DNA from two people shows extra heterozygous calls, so \(h_i\) is high and \(F_i\) negative; a person whose parents are related carries long homozygous stretches, so \(h_i\) is low and \(F_i\) positive. \(F_i\) is about 0 for a person whose genotypes look like a random draw from the sample's allele frequencies, which is why a person whose genetic ancestry differs from most of the sample can have a large \(|F_i|\) without any technical problem.

The practical's rule flags a person when \(h_i\) lies more than 3 standard deviations from the mean of all people in the file. Some pipelines use a fixed cut on \(F_i\) instead, such as \(|F_i| > 0.1\) or 0.2.1 Compute both lists and compare them.

A solution
bash 
$ awk 'FNR == 1 { next }
     NR == FNR { r = ($5 - $3) / $5; s += r; ss += r * r; n++; next }
     { m = s / n; sd = sqrt((ss - n * m * m) / (n - 1)); r = ($5 - $3) / $5
       if (r < m - 3 * sd || r > m + 3 * sd) print $1, $2 }' \
    ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.LD0.3.het ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.LD0.3.het \
    > ${analysis_dir}/fail_het.txt
$ cat ${analysis_dir}/fail_het.txt
$ awk 'NR > 1 && ($6 > 0.1 || $6 < -0.1) { print $1, $2, $6 }' ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.LD0.3.het

The first program reads the same file twice, because the mean and standard deviation must be known before any person can be judged. FNR == 1 { next } skips the header line of each pass (FNR counts lines within the current file). During the first pass NR == FNR is true (NR counts lines across all files), and the program adds up \(h_i\) (r) and its square. During the second pass it computes the mean m and the standard deviation sd, with the \(n - 1\) denominator that R's sd() uses, and prints the family and individual IDs of people outside \(m \pm 3\,\mathrm{sd}\). The output, one person per line with the IDs exactly as in the .fam file, is the format --remove reads. The last command lists the people with \(|F| > 0.1\), with their F.

Choice used from here on: the practical's ±3 SD rule, written to fail_het.txt. The \(|F|\) list is kept for comparison: a person flagged only by \(F\) may be one whose genetic ancestry differs from most of the sample, which is a reason to look at steps 6–8, not a technical failure.

In the lab, the ±3 SD rule flags ? people (?): the sample planted as contaminated (?, a high rate and a negative F), the person planted as the child of two relatives (?, a low rate and a positive F), and a person whom the simulation made admixed, with one European-like and one African-like parent. That person's extra heterozygosity comes from their genetic ancestry, not from a fault in the sample, yet the rule removes them: step 7 places them between the EUR and AFR clusters, which explains the rate, so keeping them is also defensible. The \(|F| > 0.1\) list has ? people (?): the same people and an East-Asian-like person whose F is just above 0.1, again because of ancestry. Neither rule can tell the causes apart; the results of steps 6–8 can.

Task 4: relatedness

Analyses that assume unrelated people need one member of each closely related pair removed; mixed models can keep both (step 4 · Choose a threshold). A common rule removes one member of every pair with PI_HAT > 0.1875, halfway between the values expected for second- and third-degree relatives, and keeps the member with fewer missing calls.4 Two refinements matter. A person who appears in many pairs may be a contaminated sample rather than a relative of many people, so check heterozygosity first; and a pair whose member already fails Task 3 needs no second removal, because that person goes anyway. A pair with PI_HAT near 1 is a duplicate or identical twins: compare the two records before choosing.

A solution
bash 
$ awk 'NR == FNR { if (FNR > 1) fmiss[$1 " " $2] = $6; next }
     FNR > 1 && $10 > 0.1875 { print $1, $2, $3, $4, $10, fmiss[$1 " " $2], fmiss[$3 " " $4] }' \
    ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.imiss ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.LD0.3.genome \
    > ${analysis_dir}/related_pairs.txt
$ cat ${analysis_dir}/related_pairs.txt
$ awk '{ n[$1 " " $2]++; n[$3 " " $4]++ } END { for (k in n) print n[k], k }' ${analysis_dir}/related_pairs.txt | sort -k1,1nr
$ awk 'FILENAME ~ /fail_het/ { het[$1 " " $2] = 1; next }
     !(($1 " " $2) in het) && !(($3 " " $4) in het) { print ($6 >= $7 ? $1 " " $2 : $3 " " $4) }' \
    ${analysis_dir}/fail_het.txt ${analysis_dir}/related_pairs.txt | sort -u > ${analysis_dir}/fail_related.txt
$ cat ${analysis_dir}/fail_related.txt

The first program reads toy.geno0.01.imiss into an array fmiss, keyed by "family ID, space, individual ID", with each person's F_MISS (column 6). Then, for each pair in the .genome file with PI_HAT (column 10) above 0.1875, it prints the four IDs, PI_HAT and both people's missing fractions. The second counts how many listed pairs each person is in (END runs after the last line), largest first. The third skips pairs that contain a heterozygosity failure and, for each other pair, prints the member with the larger missing fraction (the first member when they are equal, as on the step 4 page); sort -u removes repeats, so a person in two pairs is listed once.

The third program recognizes its first file by name (FILENAME ~ /fail_het/), not by the usual NR == FNR. The reason: if fail_het.txt is empty, which is a legitimate outcome of Task 3, awk reads no line of it, NR == FNR is still true for every line of the second file, and every pair would be swallowed into the array and none printed.

Choice used from here on: PI_HAT > 0.1875, remove the member with more missing calls, skip pairs that contain a heterozygosity failure. If one person is in several pairs, removing that person alone may resolve them all; look at the second command's output and decide before using the pair-by-pair list.

In the lab, related_pairs.txt lists ? pairs, and the second command shows why there are so many: one person, the contaminated sample ?, is in all of them but ?. Its extra heterozygous calls make it look related to most people (step 4), and it fails Task 3, so the third command skips its pairs. The others are the ? planted pairs: a duplicate (?, PI_HAT ?), a parent and child (?, ?), full siblings (?, ?) and half siblings, a second-degree pair (?, ?). The rule removes the member of each pair with more missing calls, so fail_related.txt has ? lines.

Task 5 (optional): genetic-similarity flags Optional

Steps 6–8 described the toy study's genetic structure. The practical makes any action on it optional and rules out one: excluding a person "solely because its GrafAnc label differs from the majority". Defensible options it lists are keeping everyone and adjusting for structure, stratified analyses, or restricting the analysis to a more genetically homogeneous subset "when that restriction is scientifically justified and prespecified".1 A flag list records who would be affected by such a choice without removing anyone. GrafAnc's groups are algorithmic assignments relative to its reference populations, not race, ethnicity or identity (step 8 · Labels are not identities).

A solution
bash 
$ awk -F'\t' 'NR == 1 { for (i = 1; i <= NF; i++) col[$i] = i; next }
     { print int($col["AncGroupID"] / 100) }' ${analysis_dir}/toy_grafanc_out.txt | sort | uniq -c
$ awk 'NR == FNR { if (FNR == 1) { for (i = 1; i <= NF; i++) col[$i] = i }
                 else if (int($col["AncGroupID"] / 100) != 3) { s = $col["Sample"]; gsub(/"/, "", s); flag[s] = 1 }
                 next }
     { k = $4; gsub(/"/, "", k); if (k in flag) print $1, $2 }' \
    ${analysis_dir}/toy_grafanc_out.txt ${analysis_dir}/toy.updateIDs.txt > ${analysis_dir}/flag_ancestry.txt
$ cat ${analysis_dir}/flag_ancestry.txt

Both programs find columns by name: on the header line they store each column's position in col, so $col["AncGroupID"] is the group code whatever its position. The first prints the continental digit of every person's group (int() drops the decimals) and counts each digit: 1 AFR, 2 MEN, 3 EUR, 4 SAS, 5 EAS, 6 AMR, 7 OCN, 8 MIX in the practical's coding.1 The second marks every person whose digit is not 3 and translates GrafAnc's sample name back to the original IDs. GrafAnc names people by the new individual ID from step 8 (such as "10002_1"), while the removal lists and the final command use the step 1 fileset's IDs; toy.updateIDs.txt holds both, the new individual ID in column 4. Quotation marks are removed on both sides before matching, so the match does not depend on whether the program kept them.

Choice used from here on: keep everyone, and keep flag_ancestry.txt as a record for a sensitivity analysis and for the PCs that Session 2 uses as covariates. The flag rule is one choice among several: a rule on Pe, or on step 7's PCs, would flag different people.

In the lab, the first command counts ? people with digit 3 (EUR), ? with 1 (AFR), ? with 5 (EAS), ? with 4 (SAS) and ? with 6 (AMR); the lab's GrafAnc gives continental codes only (step 8 · The lab's GrafAnc). flag_ancestry.txt lists everyone outside EUR, all of them people the simulation drew from non-European populations or made admixed, and nobody else. Task 6: make the cleaned fileset Core

The people who fail go into one list, and one PLINK command applies the list and the Hardy–Weinberg threshold to the fileset that step 1 left:

bash 
$ cat ${analysis_dir}/fail_het.txt ${analysis_dir}/fail_related.txt \
    | sort -u > ${analysis_dir}/toy.remove.txt
$ wc -l ${analysis_dir}/fail_het.txt ${analysis_dir}/fail_related.txt \
    ${analysis_dir}/toy.remove.txt
$ plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 --remove ${analysis_dir}/toy.remove.txt \
      --hwe 1e-6 --make-bed --out ${analysis_dir}/toy.qc
plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 --remove ${analysis_dir}/toy.remove.txt --hwe 1e-6 --make-bed --out ${analysis_dir}/toy.qc
  1. plink — PLINK 1.9.
  2. --bfile PREFIX — the step 1 output, with all its SNPs. Not the pruned SNP set, which served steps 4–6; not the uniqueIDs fileset, whose individual IDs differ from those in the lists; not the merged file, which contains the reference people.
  3. --remove FILE — drop the people listed in FILE, one per line, family ID then individual ID. PLINK compares the IDs as text, so "10002" and 10002 are different IDs. The log reports --remove: N people remaining.3
  4. --hwe 1e-6 — drop SNPs whose exact-test p-value in controls is below 10⁻⁶. The log reports --hwe: N variants removed due to Hardy-Weinberg exact test.
  5. --make-bed — write the result as a new fileset. Without it the filters would be applied to this run only and nothing would be saved.
  6. --out PREFIX — the cleaned fileset, toy.qc.bed, .bim and .fam, and its log.

Order of operations. PLINK 1.9 applies its filters in a fixed order, whatever their order on the command line, and --remove comes before --hwe.2 The Hardy–Weinberg test is therefore computed on the controls who remain, so a contaminated control that is being removed cannot push a SNP below the threshold. That is also why the number of SNPs in the log can differ slightly from the count in Task 2, which used all controls.

Check the result. Read the three log lines and count the lines of the new files:

bash 
$ grep -E 'remaining|removed due to|pass filters' ${analysis_dir}/toy.qc.log
$ wc -l ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.fam ${analysis_dir}/toy.qc.fam ${analysis_dir}/toy.qc.bim

The number of people remaining should be the step 1 count minus the number of lines of toy.remove.txt. If it equals the step 1 count, no ID in the list matched anyone, almost always because of quotation marks or the wrong set of IDs (Pitfalls). The last log line, … variants and … people pass filters and QC., gives the size of the cleaned fileset, which the two wc -l counts confirm.

Of the step 1 fileset's ? people and ? SNPs, toy.qc therefore keeps ? people (? removed) and ? SNPs (? removed by --hwe). Task 7: the QC report Core

A QC report lists, for each step, the statistic, the rule, what the rule removed or flagged, and where each number comes from (a log line or a file), so that another analyst can rerun the commands and get the same numbers. The widget below builds that table from the files in this page's lab: it reads the PLINK logs, the lists and the .fam and .bim files you made, and redraws whenever a command writes one of them. It also checks that the numbers are consistent with each other.

Try itThe QC report, read from your files

What to notice. Rows fill in as files appear: run Task 1 (or press Run steps 1–8), then Tasks 2–6 by pasting the solutions into the terminal or typing your own. Only three rows remove anything: step 1's two filters and the final command (people from your list, SNPs by --hwe). Steps 3, 6 and 7 never remove anyone, and step 8 at most flags. Step 1's removals come from line counts, not logs: the practical reuses the prefix toy.geno0.01 for --missing and toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 for --hardy and --indep-pairwise, and each PLINK run overwrites the .log of the same name, so the filters' own log lines are gone by the end of Task 1. A report should cite a source that still exists. The checks under the table compare numbers that must agree, such as the people remaining after --remove and the lines of toy.qc.fam. Change a rule, rerun the final command, and watch which rows change.

The table under the checks compares your counts with the toy data's reference values, computed for this site by running the practical's commands with the lab's emulators (window.TOY_CARD; the same numbers appear in the toy-data paragraphs of the step pages). Real PLINK 1.9.0 writes the same files from the same commands. For steps 1–8 a difference means that a command differs from the practical's, or that a file was overwritten. For the final command the reference is this page's worked solution: ? heterozygosity failures plus one member of each of the ? related pairs removed, and the ? planted SNPs removed by --hwe 1e-6. A difference there is expected whenever your rules differ. What the new functions do: readLines(path) reads a text file into a vector with one element per line, and length() counts them, like len(open(path).readlines()). sapply(x, f) applies f to each element and simplifies the result to a vector, like a list comprehension. lag(x) (dplyr) shifts a column down by one row, so lag(people) - people is the number removed at each stage, like df.people.shift() - df.people; the first row has nothing before it and gets NA. grep(pattern, x, value = TRUE) returns the elements of x that match, like the shell's grep.

What if you filter people before SNPs: --mind 0.01 first, then --geno 0.01?
bash 
$ plink --bfile ${input_dir}/toy --mind 0.01 --make-bed --out ${analysis_dir}/toy.mind0.01
$ plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/toy.mind0.01 --geno 0.01 --make-bed --out ${analysis_dir}/toy.mind0.01.geno0.01
$ grep -h 'removed due to missing' ${analysis_dir}/toy.mind0.01.log ${analysis_dir}/toy.mind0.01.geno0.01.log
$ wc -l ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.fam ${analysis_dir}/toy.mind0.01.geno0.01.fam
$ wc -l ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.bim ${analysis_dir}/toy.mind0.01.geno0.01.bim

A few bad SNPs with many missing calls raise every person's missing fraction. Filtered first, they are gone before people are judged, so --mind removes only people whose DNA is genuinely poor. In the reversed order the bad SNPs still count, more people cross 1%, and those people are lost although their data on the good SNPs may be fine. The grep -h line prints the reversed order's two log lines without file names. The practical's order cannot be read the same way, because its later commands reuse the prefixes toy.geno0.01 and toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 and overwrite those logs; the two wc -l lines compare the people and the SNPs that each order keeps. A single command with both flags, plink --bfile ${input_dir}/toy --geno 0.01 --mind 0.01 …, does not follow the practical's order either: PLINK applies --mind before --geno, so it matches the reversed order.2 More on this, with a widget: step 1 · Order of the filters.

In the lab, --mind 0.01 on the raw data removes ? people, against ? in the practical's order. Besides the planted degraded samples it removes the planted low-quality samples, whose missing calls sit mostly in the bad SNPs, and an ordinary person just above 1%. The wc -l lines show that the reversed order keeps fewer people and slightly more SNPs: the people it removed carried some of the missing calls that pushed SNPs over 1%.

What if you test Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium in everyone (ALL rows) instead of controls?
bash 
$ awk '$3 == "ALL" && $9 < 1e-6 { print $2 }' ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.hwe > ${analysis_dir}/hwe_all.txt
$ awk '$3 == "UNAFF" && $9 < 1e-6 { print $2 }' ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.hwe > ${analysis_dir}/hwe_unaff.txt
$ wc -l ${analysis_dir}/hwe_all.txt ${analysis_dir}/hwe_unaff.txt
$ awk 'FILENAME ~ /hwe_unaff/ { u[$1] = 1; next } !($1 in u)' ${analysis_dir}/hwe_unaff.txt ${analysis_dir}/hwe_all.txt

The last command prints the SNPs that fail in everyone but not in controls. On your own computer grep -v -w -F -f ${analysis_dir}/hwe_unaff.txt ${analysis_dir}/hwe_all.txt does the same (-f reads the patterns from a file, -F takes them literally, -w matches whole words, -v keeps the lines that match none); the lab's grep has no -f. For each, look at its three rows with grep -w SNP_ID on the .hwe file: a departure that shows in the AFF and ALL rows but not in UNAFF is the pattern of a true association with the disease, because selecting people with the disease changes their genotype frequencies at an associated SNP. Testing everyone (--hwe 1e-6 include-nonctrl) would remove that SNP and with it a real finding.3 In real data, remember which people PLINK calls controls (The data · What column 6 holds).

In the lab, the last command prints ?, the SNP that the simulation made causal: the risk allele raises the chance of being a case. Its P is ? in the UNAFF row, ? in the AFF row and ? in the ALL row: the controls are in equilibrium, and only the people with the disease depart from it.

What if you skip LD pruning before the PCA?
bash 
$ plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 --maf 0.05 --pca --out ${analysis_dir}/toy.noprune
$ head -3 ${analysis_dir}/toy.noprune.eigenval ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.LD0.3.eigenval

Without pruning, a region of strong LD contributes many SNPs that carry the same information. PCA finds directions of large shared variation, so a top PC can end up describing the genotypes in that one region rather than genome-wide ancestry, and its eigenvalue is inflated. Compare the first eigenvalues of the two runs, then plot the PCs of both (step 6 has the plots; the theory is in Population structure · LD and relatives). The same logic is why relatedness and heterozygosity use pruned SNPs.

In the lab, the first eigenvalue changes little and the second grows a lot. The cause is planted: a region of ? SNPs on chromosome ?, from ? to ? Mb, built like the 8p23.1 inversion, with two kinds of haplotype that never recombine with each other. Without pruning, PC? sorts people by how many inverted copies they carry (correlation ?). Pruning keeps ? of the region's ? SNPs left after step 1, and the region's strongest link is then with PC? (correlation ?).

Pitfalls Common mistakes in the exercise
  • IDs that do not match. --remove compares IDs as text. PennCATH's and the toy data's family IDs include quotation marks; a list without them, or one with GrafAnc's new IDs, removes nobody, and PLINK does not warn. Check the people remaining line every time.
  • Writing lists from R. fread() strips the quotation marks by default. Read with fread(path, quote = "") and write with fwrite(x, path, sep = " ", quote = FALSE, col.names = FALSE), or build lists with awk from PLINK's own output.
  • NR == FNR with a file that may be empty. The idiom means "the first file" only if that file has at least one line; an empty first list makes the program treat the second file as the first. Recognize the file by name (FILENAME ~ /…/).
  • The wrong input fileset. The final command starts from toy.geno0.01.mind0.01: not the pruned SNPs, not the uniqueIDs fileset, not the merged file with the reference people.
  • Removing more people than needed. Removing both members of a related pair, or a partner of a person who is removed anyway, loses data for no gain.
  • Forgetting --make-bed. Without it the filters apply to that one run and nothing is written.
  • Choosing rules after seeing association results. Fix every threshold from the QC statistics, write it in the report, and keep it.
Key points
  • Only step 1 of the practical removes anything; the exercise turns the other steps' statistics into rules, chosen and justified before any association test.
  • People who fail go into a list of family and individual IDs, built with awk from PLINK's own output; SNPs are filtered by --hwe in controls.
  • One command, plink --bfile … --remove … --hwe … --make-bed, makes the cleaned fileset; PLINK applies --remove before --hwe, so the test runs on the people who remain.
  • Genetic-ancestry results are flagged, not used to remove people, unless a restriction was scientifically justified and prespecified.
  • A QC report gives each step's rule, what it removed and the log line or file behind each number; checking that the numbers add up catches silent mistakes such as IDs that match nobody.

Why does the final command start from toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 and not from toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.uniqueIDs, which step 8 made?

The removal lists hold the IDs of the step 1 fileset, written from the .het, .genome and .imiss files, which all use those IDs. The uniqueIDs fileset has new individual IDs (such as "10002_1"), so none of the listed IDs would match and nobody would be removed. The new IDs exist only because GrafAnc needs unique individual IDs.

A person fails the heterozygosity rule and is also in three pairs with PI_HAT above 0.1875. What do you remove, and why?

Remove that person and skip the three pairs. A sample contaminated with a second person's DNA has extra heterozygous calls and shares alleles with many people, so it produces spurious "relatives"; once it is removed, those pairs no longer exist. Removing its partners as well would lose three people whose data may be fine. Check afterwards that no genuine pair is left unresolved.

Why put --remove and --hwe in the same command instead of running --hwe first and --remove in a second command?

PLINK applies --remove before --hwe within one run, so the Hardy–Weinberg test uses only the controls who remain. With --hwe first, people who are about to be removed, such as a contaminated control, would still take part in the test and could push SNPs below the threshold. The order of operations is fixed; the order of the flags on the command line does not change it.

A colleague's final log says "--remove: 1380 people remaining." for PennCATH, although their list has 12 lines. What went wrong?

No ID in the list matched: 1,380 is the number of people before the removal (after step 1). Likely causes are a list written from R without the quotation marks that PennCATH's family IDs carry, a list built from GrafAnc's output with the new individual IDs, or the columns in the wrong order. Compare the first line of the list with the matching line of the .fam file, character by character.

The practical says a well-justified analysis may remove nobody for heterozygosity. When is that the right conclusion?

When no person is far from the rest, or when the people who are far from the rest are explained by genetic ancestry rather than by a technical problem: for example, people whose heterozygosity is high because their ancestry differs from the sample's majority, whose F is shifted for the same reason, and whose missingness and relatedness are normal. Such people are kept and the analysis adjusts for structure; removing them would discard valid data and narrow the study's population.
