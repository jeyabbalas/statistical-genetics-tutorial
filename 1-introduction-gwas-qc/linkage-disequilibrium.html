Linkage disequilibrium: from genotypes to haplotypes to r²

Nearby SNPs are correlated because chromosome copies are passed on in long pieces. This correlation, linkage disequilibrium (LD), is defined on haplotypes, the combinations of alleles on one chromosome copy, but a genotyping array measures each SNP separately and never reports haplotypes. This page shows how haplotype frequencies are estimated from genotype counts with the EM algorithm, how D, D′ and r² are computed from them, why LD fades with distance and differs between populations, and how PLINK, LD matrices, heatmaps and reference panels report it.

Mental model

LD lives in a 2 × 2 table of haplotype frequencies that genotype data only let you estimate

For two SNPs, LD is a property of the 2 × 2 table of haplotype frequencies. Genotype data give only a 3 × 3 table of genotype counts, so the haplotype table is estimated (by EM), summarized by one number, D, and rescaled into D′ and r². Mutation, recombination and population history set the values, which is why LD decays with distance and differs between populations.

  • Origin. A new allele arises on one haplotype, so a new SNP starts with three haplotypes next to an older one, and only recombination (or a repeat mutation) creates the fourth.
  • Genotypes to haplotypes. The 3 × 3 table fixes everyone's haplotypes except the double heterozygotes; EM splits them in proportion to the current haplotype frequencies until the estimates stop changing.
  • Measures. Once the allele frequencies are fixed, the table has one free number, D; D′ divides it by its largest possible value, r² by the allele-frequency variances.
  • Decay. Each generation keeps a fraction 1 − θ of D, and Haldane's map function turns map distance into θ, so LD fades with distance at a rate set by the local recombination rate.
  • Populations. A larger, older population has had more recombination and less drift, so LD is shorter in samples of African ancestry: more tag SNPs are needed, imputation is harder and fine-mapping is sharper.
  • In the data. PLINK reports r² of allele counts for pairs of SNPs or whole matrices (D′ on request); heatmaps draw the matrix; reference panels and LDlink supply LD when you have no genotypes; LD pruning uses it in QC step 3.

Where LD comes from: one new allele, three haplotypes, then recombination Core

Throughout this page, SNP 1 has alleles A and a and SNP 2 has alleles G and g, as on the slides. A chromosome copy carries one of four combinations of them, the haplotypes AG, Ag, aG and ag. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) is a non-random association between the alleles at the two SNPs across the chromosome copies of a population: among copies that carry A, the share that carries G differs from the share among copies that carry a. The reading defines it as "the non-independent association of two alleles in a population"1. Slide 85 adds that it is "typically measured using r²", the squared correlation defined in the measures section below, and lists why it matters: it sets the number of independent tests in a genome scan, lets a few SNPs stand for many (tag SNPs), makes imputation possible and records the history of genetic variation. Lecture 0 · LD in GWAS covers those uses; this page is about where the association comes from and how it is estimated and measured.

Every SNP began as a single mutation on a single chromosome copy, and that copy already carried alleles at the SNPs around it (slides 109–111). The figure below follows two SNPs through that history.

The figure carries three ideas that the rest of the page uses.

  • A new SNP starts in complete LD with its neighbors. At stage 3 the table has a zero cell. The measures section shows that any table with a zero cell has \(|D'| = 1\), whatever the allele frequencies. Slide 121 reads \(|D'| = 1\) as "evidence for no recombination" between the two SNPs.
  • Only recombination (or a repeat mutation) makes the fourth haplotype. If each SNP arose by a single mutation and the same mutation never happened twice, seeing all four haplotypes in a sample proves that at least one crossover happened between the SNPs in the history of that sample. This is the four-gamete test2.
  • After that, the association erodes slowly. Recombination keeps reshuffling alleles at the two SNPs, and drift keeps changing the haplotype frequencies; the closer the SNPs, the rarer the crossovers between them and the longer the association lasts (the decay section).

Slide 112 shows the long-run result: present-day chromosome copies are mosaics of pieces of ancestral copies, and copies that share an ancestral piece around a site share its alleles. How long the shared pieces are depends on the recombination rate, the mutation rate, the size of the population and natural selection3. Alleles at very close SNPs usually sit on the same ancestral piece, so their combinations reflect single ancestral haplotypes; alleles further apart have been reshuffled by recombination. Lecture 0 · How LD arises shows how fast the shared pieces shrink.

LD between unlinked loci: gametic disequilibrium

The name "linkage disequilibrium" suggests that the two loci must be linked, that is, close enough on one chromosome that crossovers between them happen in fewer than half of meioses (recombination fraction \(\theta < 1/2\); Lecture 0 · Linkage). Slide 115 separates the two conditions. Condition A, that the alleles at the two loci are not independently distributed, is gametic disequilibrium; condition B, that the loci are linked, is linkage. The usual LD measures check only condition A, so they measure gametic disequilibrium. Association without linkage does occur: if a sample mixes people from two populations whose allele frequencies differ at two loci on different chromosomes, the alleles at the two loci are associated across the pooled copies (see the population-structure page). For unlinked loci \(\theta = 1/2\), so such an association halves in every generation of random mating (the decay section) and soon disappears; association between close loci lasts much longer. LD is in practice computed for SNPs on the same chromosome, where the two terms mean the same thing.

Slides Slide 115 · A comment about the definition of LD

The slide's point in one line: D, D′ and r² measure association between alleles on the same gamete (an egg or sperm cell, which carries one chromosome copy), whatever caused it; linkage is what makes that association last. "Often people say LD when they mean gametic disequilibrium", and for SNPs on one chromosome nothing is lost by doing so.

From genotypes to haplotypes: estimating haplotype frequencies Core

Every LD measure is computed from the frequencies of the four haplotypes. A genotyping array does not report haplotypes, so the frequencies have to be estimated. This section defines them (slide 113), shows which haplotypes a table of genotypes fixes and which it leaves open (slides 114 and 117), and derives the algorithm that estimates them.

Haplotypes and haplotype frequencies

Slide 113 defines haplotypes as "the ordered arrangements of alleles at specific loci along the same chromosome", that is, "phased genotypes", the alleles as they were passed down from each parent. Its example has three loci. The chromosome copy from parent 1 carries A, T and A; the copy from parent 2 carries A, C and C. The genotypes are AA, CT and AC, and they do not say whether the T travels with the first A or the second, or with A or C at the third locus.

For two SNPs, the haplotype frequency \(p_{\mathrm{AG}}\) is the fraction of chromosome copies in the population that carry A at SNP 1 and G at SNP 2; \(p_{\mathrm{Ag}}\), \(p_{\mathrm{aG}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{ag}}\) are defined the same way, and the four add up to 1. Phase is not observed: the 3 × 3 genotype table

An array measures each SNP with its own probes and reports, for each person, an unordered pair of alleles per SNP (Lecture 0 · From samples to genotypes). A person who is heterozygous at both SNPs carries A on one copy and a on the other, and G on one copy and g on the other, but the data do not say whether A sits on the same copy as G or as g. Which alleles share a copy is the person's phase (Lecture 0 · Haplotypes and phase). Slide 114 puts it as "genotype data typically does not include information about phase of alleles". Long-range single-molecule sequencing can read phase directly, because one DNA molecule comes from one chromosome copy, but it is "experimentally challenging", so haplotypes are usually inferred from genotypes.

For a pair of SNPs, each person falls into one of nine genotype combinations, and the data are a 3 × 3 table of counts of people. The next table shows what each cell implies about the person's two haplotypes.

A person who is homozygous at either SNP has known haplotypes: an AA person carries A on both copies, so the G and g at SNP 2 can only pair with A. Only the center cell, the double heterozygotes (Aa and Gg), is ambiguous: each such person carries either AG and ag, or Ag and aG. This splits the data into two parts:

  • Known haplotype counts \(c_{\mathrm{AG}}, c_{\mathrm{Ag}}, c_{\mathrm{aG}}, c_{\mathrm{ag}}\): the copies of each haplotype carried by people outside the center cell (two copies from a person in a corner, one from a person on an edge).
  • The number of double heterozygotes \(m\): each contributes one AG and one ag copy, or one Ag and one aG copy. The known copies and the double heterozygotes' copies add up to \(2n\).

Slide 117 annotates its table with two remarks. "Low counts implies low numbers of aG haps": the people outside the center cell carry no aG copy at all, and that is the evidence that the frequency of aG is low. "Only ambiguous haplotypes. Why?" points at the 5 in the center: these are the only people whose haplotypes are not fixed, because everyone else is homozygous at one of the two SNPs. The estimation problem is therefore one number per double heterozygote: the probability that the person carries AG + ag rather than Ag + aG.

The EM algorithm, step by step

Likelihood, in one paragraph. A likelihood is the probability of the observed data under a model, read as a function of the model's unknown parameters. Here the parameters are the four haplotype frequencies and the model is random mating: each person's two chromosome copies are two independent draws from the population's haplotypes (Hardy–Weinberg proportions for haplotypes). Under this model a person with haplotypes AG and AG has probability \(p_{\mathrm{AG}}^2\), a person with AG and Ag has probability \(2p_{\mathrm{AG}}p_{\mathrm{Ag}}\) (the 2 counts the two ways the copies can come from the two parents), and a double heterozygote has probability \(2p_{\mathrm{AG}}p_{\mathrm{ag}} + 2p_{\mathrm{Ag}}p_{\mathrm{aG}}\), the sum over the two possible phases. Multiplying over people gives the likelihood; its logarithm turns the product into a sum. The maximum-likelihood estimate is the set of frequencies that makes the observed table most probable, the same principle the previous page uses for allele frequencies.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(p) &= \sum_{h} c_h \ln p_h + m \ln P_{\mathrm{dh}} \\ P_{\mathrm{dh}} &= 2p_{\mathrm{AG}}\,p_{\mathrm{ag}} + 2p_{\mathrm{Ag}}\,p_{\mathrm{aG}} \end{aligned} \]
\(\ell(p)\)
Log-likelihood (natural logarithm) of the four haplotype frequencies \(p = (p_{\mathrm{AG}}, p_{\mathrm{Ag}}, p_{\mathrm{aG}}, p_{\mathrm{ag}})\), leaving out a constant (a \(\ln 2\) for each person heterozygous at exactly one SNP) that does not depend on \(p\).
\(h\)
One of the four haplotypes AG, Ag, aG, ag; the sum runs over all four.
\(c_h\)
Known count of haplotype \(h\): its copies among people who are not double heterozygotes (0 to \(2n\)).
\(p_h\)
Frequency of haplotype \(h\) in the population (0 to 1; the four add up to 1).
\(m\)
Number of double heterozygotes (people who are Aa and Gg).
\(P_{\mathrm{dh}}\)
Probability that a person is a double heterozygote: AG with ag, or Ag with aG, each in either order.

Without double heterozygotes the log-likelihood would be \(\sum_h c_h \ln p_h\), which is largest at \(\hat{p}_h = c_h / \sum_h c_h\): each haplotype's share of the copies, a count. The term \(m \ln P_{\mathrm{dh}}\) mixes two phases, so setting the derivatives to zero gives a cubic equation in one frequency5 instead of a formula. For slide 117, \(\ell = -14.744\) at the no-LD starting point used below and \(-12.123\) at the final estimate: the final frequencies make the observed table \(e^{2.621} \approx 14\) times more probable.

The EM algorithm (expectation–maximization) climbs to the maximum without solving the cubic6,7. It alternates two steps that are each easy.

E-step. Given the current frequencies, compute for each double heterozygote the probability of each phase, and from it the expected number of copies of each haplotype.

\[ w = \frac{p_{\mathrm{AG}}\,p_{\mathrm{ag}}}{p_{\mathrm{AG}}\,p_{\mathrm{ag}} + p_{\mathrm{Ag}}\,p_{\mathrm{aG}}} \]
\(w\)
Probability, under the current frequencies, that a double heterozygote carries AG + ag rather than Ag + aG (0 to 1). The same for every double heterozygote.
\(p_{\mathrm{AG}}, \ldots, p_{\mathrm{ag}}\)
The current estimates of the four haplotype frequencies.

This is the probability of one phase divided by the probability of either, \(2p_{\mathrm{AG}}p_{\mathrm{ag}} / P_{\mathrm{dh}}\); the factors of 2 cancel. If AG and ag are common and Ag or aG is rare, \(w\) is close to 1. With no LD, \(p_{\mathrm{AG}}p_{\mathrm{ag}} = p_{\mathrm{Ag}}p_{\mathrm{aG}}\) (the measures section shows that their difference is \(D\)), so \(w = 0.5\). The expected counts are then \(c_{\mathrm{AG}} + mw\) for AG and \(c_{\mathrm{ag}} + mw\) for ag, and \(c_{\mathrm{Ag}} + m(1 - w)\) and \(c_{\mathrm{aG}} + m(1 - w)\) for Ag and aG.

M-step. Treat the expected counts as if they had been observed and estimate the frequencies by counting.

\[ \begin{aligned} p_{\mathrm{AG}}^{(t+1)} &= \frac{c_{\mathrm{AG}} + m\,w^{(t)}}{2n} \\[4pt] p_{\mathrm{Ag}}^{(t+1)} &= \frac{c_{\mathrm{Ag}} + m\,(1 - w^{(t)})}{2n} \end{aligned} \]
\(t\)
Iteration number; \(t = 0\) is the starting guess.
\(w^{(t)}\)
The E-step probability computed from the frequencies of iteration \(t\).
\(2n\)
Number of chromosome copies in the sample (\(n\) people).
\(p_{\mathrm{AG}}^{(t+1)}\)
The updated estimate; \(p_{\mathrm{ag}}\) is updated like \(p_{\mathrm{AG}}\) (with \(c_{\mathrm{ag}}\) and \(w\)), \(p_{\mathrm{aG}}\) like \(p_{\mathrm{Ag}}\) (with \(c_{\mathrm{aG}}\) and \(1 - w\)).

Each double heterozygote is split between its two possible pairs in proportion to their current probabilities, and the frequencies are recounted. Every double heterozygote carries exactly one A and one G whichever phase it has, so from the first update on, the allele frequencies equal the sample allele frequencies and only \(D\) moves. Each iteration can only raise \(\ell\) or leave it unchanged, a general property of EM6. With \(m = 0\) the M-step gives the counting answer at once.

The slide 117 example, iteration by iteration. The sample allele frequencies are \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{A}} = 10/16 = 0.625\) and \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{G}} = 8/16 = 0.5\). Start from no LD, each haplotype frequency equal to the product of its allele frequencies: \(p^{(0)}_{\mathrm{AG}} = 0.625 \times 0.5 = 0.3125\), and likewise 0.3125 for Ag and 0.1875 for aG and ag. Then \(w^{(0)} = 0.5\), the expected AG count is \(3 + 5 \times 0.5 = 5.5\), and \(p^{(1)}_{\mathrm{AG}} = 5.5/16 = 0.34375\). Two things in the table are worth following.

  • Why \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{aG}}\) goes to zero. No known copy is aG (\(c_{\mathrm{aG}} = 0\)), so aG gets copies only from the double heterozygotes' second option: \(p^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{aG}} = m(1 - w^{(t)})/2n\). And \(1 - w\) is proportional to \(p_{\mathrm{aG}}\) itself. Once \(p_{\mathrm{aG}}\) is small, \(1 - w \approx p_{\mathrm{Ag}}p_{\mathrm{aG}}/(p_{\mathrm{AG}}p_{\mathrm{ag}})\), so each iteration multiplies \(p_{\mathrm{aG}}\) by about \((m/2n)\,p_{\mathrm{Ag}}/(p_{\mathrm{AG}}p_{\mathrm{ag}}) = (5/16) \times 0.125/(0.5 \times 0.375) \approx 0.21\). The estimate approaches 0 but reaches it only in the limit; software stops when the estimates change by less than a set tolerance. The limit, 8 AG, 2 Ag, 0 aG and 6 ag copies out of 16, is the table on the right of slide 117: all five double heterozygotes are assigned AG + ag.
  • The maximum sits on the edge. The log-likelihood keeps rising until \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{aG}} = 0\), the edge of the allowed range, so \(\hat{D}\) ends at the largest value the allele frequencies allow. That is why \(|\hat{D}'| = 1\) in slide 124's worked example.

Several maxima. Once the allele frequencies are fixed, the log-likelihood is a function of one number, \(p_{\mathrm{AG}}\) (equivalently \(D\)). For most tables it has one peak. When most people are double heterozygotes it can have two peaks with a valley between them, and EM climbs to whichever peak is uphill from its start. Its stationary points are the real roots of the cubic equation mentioned above5,8. PLINK 1.9 solves the cubic directly instead of iterating, uses the maximum-likelihood solution for --r2 dprime, and its single-pair command --ld prints all biologically possible solutions when there is more than one9. A program that iterates can guard against the wrong peak by starting EM from several points and keeping the result with the highest log-likelihood.

More than two SNPs. The same E- and M-steps work for haplotypes of many SNPs7, but the number of possible haplotypes doubles with each added SNP, so whole-genome phasing uses other methods that build each person's haplotypes from haplotypes common in the rest of the sample (Lecture 0 · Phasing). All of them, like two-SNP EM, rely on people sharing haplotypes, and they work better as the sample grows.

Measures of LD: D, D′ and r² Core

With the haplotype frequencies estimated, the LD measures are short formulas. This section derives them in the order of slides 118–124 and computes them for the slide 117 table. Lecture 0 · Measuring LD introduces the same measures with a calculator; here the emphasis is on why each formula has its form and how it behaves.

D: one free number in the 2 × 2 table

Fix the allele frequencies, the margins of the 2 × 2 table. Then the four cells have only one degree of freedom left: if \(p_{\mathrm{AG}}\) goes up by some amount, \(p_{\mathrm{Ag}}\) must go down by the same amount to keep the column total \(p_{\mathrm{A}}\), \(p_{\mathrm{aG}}\) must go down to keep the row total \(p_{\mathrm{G}}\), and \(p_{\mathrm{ag}}\) must go up. Slide 118 names that one free number \(d\) (also written \(D\), or \(\delta\) on slide 120) and measures it from the cell values expected under independence:

\[ \begin{aligned} p_{\mathrm{AG}} &= p_{\mathrm{A}}\,p_{\mathrm{G}} + D \\ p_{\mathrm{Ag}} &= p_{\mathrm{A}}\,p_{\mathrm{g}} - D \\ p_{\mathrm{aG}} &= p_{\mathrm{a}}\,p_{\mathrm{G}} - D \\ p_{\mathrm{ag}} &= p_{\mathrm{a}}\,p_{\mathrm{g}} + D \end{aligned} \]
\(p_{\mathrm{AG}}, \ldots, p_{\mathrm{ag}}\)
Haplotype frequencies (0 to 1).
\(p_{\mathrm{A}}, p_{\mathrm{a}}\)
Allele frequencies at SNP 1, \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = 1 - p_{\mathrm{A}}\).
\(p_{\mathrm{G}}, p_{\mathrm{g}}\)
Allele frequencies at SNP 2, \(p_{\mathrm{g}} = 1 - p_{\mathrm{G}}\).
\(D\)
The LD coefficient: the excess of AG (and ag) copies over what independence predicts, as a fraction of all copies. Positive, negative or zero.

If the alleles were independent, each cell would be the product of its margins and \(D = 0\); the SNPs are then said to be in linkage equilibrium. Every cell must stay between 0 and 1, which bounds \(D\): \(D_{\max} = \min(p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{g}},\, p_{\mathrm{a}}p_{\mathrm{G}})\), reached when Ag or aG hits zero, and \(D_{\min} = -\min(p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{G}},\, p_{\mathrm{a}}p_{\mathrm{g}})\), reached when AG or ag hits zero. For slide 124's table, \(p_{\mathrm{A}} = 0.625\) and \(p_{\mathrm{G}} = 0.5\), so \(D\) can range from \(-0.1875\) to \(0.1875\), and the estimate \(\hat{D} = 0.5 - 0.3125 = 0.1875\) is at its maximum.

Two equivalent forms are used on the slides. From the first line, \(D = p_{\mathrm{AG}} - p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{G}}\). Multiplying out the four lines gives \(D = p_{\mathrm{AG}}\,p_{\mathrm{ag}} - p_{\mathrm{Ag}}\,p_{\mathrm{aG}}\) (slide 120): the product of the "coupling" diagonal minus the product of the other diagonal. The second form needs no allele frequencies, which is why the counting formulas below use it.

Why \(D\) measures dependence (slide 119). Two random variables are independent if knowing one gives no information about the other. For a chromosome copy drawn at random from the population, "carries A" and "carries G" are independent when \(\Pr(\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{G}) = \Pr(\mathrm{A})\Pr(\mathrm{G})\), which is \(p_{\mathrm{AG}} = p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{G}}\), that is \(D = 0\). Conditioning shows what a nonzero \(D\) does:

\[ \Pr(\mathrm{A} \mid \mathrm{G}) = \frac{p_{\mathrm{AG}}}{p_{\mathrm{G}}} = p_{\mathrm{A}} + \frac{D}{p_{\mathrm{G}}} \]
\(\Pr(\mathrm{A} \mid \mathrm{G})\)
Probability that a chromosome copy carries A, among copies that carry G.
\(p_{\mathrm{A}}\)
Probability that a copy carries A, among all copies.
\(D / p_{\mathrm{G}}\)
How much learning "G" changes the probability of A.

With \(D = 0\) the allele at SNP 2 tells nothing about SNP 1. In slide 124's table, \(\Pr(\mathrm{A} \mid \mathrm{G}) = 0.5/0.5 = 1\) against \(p_{\mathrm{A}} = 0.625\): every copy with G carries A.

Why \(D\) alone is hard to read (slide 120). "δ depends on allele frequencies and may be positive or negative". Its sign depends only on which allele of each SNP is called A or G: renaming a as A turns \(D\) into \(-D\). Its size is limited by the allele frequencies: for two SNPs with allele frequencies 0.5, \(D\) can reach 0.25, but when A and G both have frequency 0.05, \(D\) cannot exceed \(0.05 \times 0.95 = 0.0475\). The same \(D = 0.04\) is then close to the maximum for the rare pair and modest for the common pair. D′ and r² remove these dependences in two different ways.

D′ and r²

\[ D' = \begin{cases} D / D_{\max}, & D > 0 \\ D / |D_{\min}|, & D < 0 \end{cases} \]
\(D'\)
D prime: \(D\) as a fraction of the most extreme value it could take in the same direction; from −1 to 1, and \(|D'|\) from 0 to 1. PLINK reports \(|D'|\) unless asked for the signed value.
\(D_{\max}\)
\(\min(p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{g}},\, p_{\mathrm{a}}p_{\mathrm{G}})\), the largest possible \(D\) given the allele frequencies.
\(|D_{\min}|\)
\(\min(p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{G}},\, p_{\mathrm{a}}p_{\mathrm{g}})\), the size of the most negative possible \(D\).

\(D'\) was proposed by Lewontin10 so that LD between SNPs with different allele frequencies could be compared on one scale. \(|D'| = 1\) exactly when \(D\) is at its limit, and \(D\) is at its limit exactly when one of the four cells is zero (the first eq-block above): \(|D'| = 1\) means a haplotype is missing. For slide 124, \(D' = 0.1875/0.1875 = 1\).

\[ r^2 = \frac{D^2}{p_{\mathrm{A}}\,p_{\mathrm{a}}\,p_{\mathrm{G}}\,p_{\mathrm{g}}} \]
\(r^2\)
The squared correlation between the alleles at the two SNPs on the same chromosome copy; from 0 to 1.
\(D\)
The LD coefficient.
\(p_{\mathrm{A}}\,p_{\mathrm{a}}\)
Variance of the 0/1 allele code at SNP 1 (a Bernoulli variance).
\(p_{\mathrm{G}}\,p_{\mathrm{g}}\)
Variance of the 0/1 allele code at SNP 2.

Slide 121 states that \(r\) "is identical to the Pearson correlation" between allele codes: code each copy \(X = 1\) if it carries a at SNP 1 (0 for A) and \(Y = 1\) if it carries g at SNP 2 (0 for G). Then \(\Cov(X, Y) = p_{\mathrm{ag}} - p_{\mathrm{a}}p_{\mathrm{g}} = D\) and the variances are \(p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{G}}p_{\mathrm{g}}\), so \(r = D/\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{a}}p_{\mathrm{G}}p_{\mathrm{g}}}\). The sign of \(r\) depends on the coding; \(r^2\) does not. For slide 124, \(r^2 = 0.1875^2/(0.625 \times 0.375 \times 0.5 \times 0.5) = 0.6\).

Slides 121–122 list how the two measures behave. Each property follows from the formulas.

  • \(|D'| = 1\) is read as "no evidence for historical recombination" (slide 121, "in the absence of other influences on LD"). A missing haplotype is what stage 3 of the origins figure looks like, and the four-gamete test says a fourth haplotype needs a crossover or a repeat mutation. The reverse does not hold: \(|D'| < 1\) can also come from genotyping errors or a repeat mutation.
  • \(|\hat{D}'|\) is biased upward in small samples and for rare alleles. A rare haplotype can be missing from a sample by chance, and then \(|\hat{D}'| = 1\) regardless of history. With 16 copies, as on slide 124, a haplotype with population frequency 0.05 is missing from \(0.95^{16} \approx 44\%\) of samples. Estimates of \(|D'|\) are inflated when the sample is small or an allele is rare, and more so when haplotype frequencies are estimated from genotypes11.
  • \(r^2 = 1\) needs equal allele frequencies (slide 122's "perfect LD"): only two haplotypes are present, and each allele at one SNP always comes with the same allele at the other, so the two SNPs carry the same information. When the frequencies differ, \(r^2\) cannot reach 1 even with \(|D'| = 1\): its ceiling, \(r^2_{\max}\), is 0.6 for slide 124's frequencies (second panel of the figure above) and only 0.053 for allele frequencies 0.5 and 0.05 (Lecture 0 · r² derives \(r^2_{\max}\)). This is slide 85's warning that "high r² between two SNPs requires both have similar MAFs" (minor allele frequencies).
  • \(r^2\) is "very sensitive to marginal allele frequencies" (slide 122), so two SNPs far apart whose rare alleles arose on the same haplotype can show a higher \(r^2\) than two close common SNPs whose allele frequencies differ. Slide 122 cites a table in Devlin and Risch (1995) for this12.
  • \(r^2\) "is directly related to power for association" (slide 122). If a causal SNP is not genotyped, testing a marker in LD with it in \(n\) people gives about the power of testing the causal SNP itself in \(r^2 n\) people13: with \(r^2 = 0.8\), 10,000 people genotyped at the marker are worth about 8,000 genotyped at the causal SNP. This is why tag SNPs are chosen by \(r^2\) (Lecture 0 · Tag SNPs).

LD decay: over generations and with distance Core

Mutation creates LD and recombination removes it. Lecture 0 · How LD arises derives the basic decay law; this section restates it for all three measures, turns physical distance into a recombination fraction, and compares the result with the data of slide 86.

Each generation keeps a fraction 1 − θ of D

The recombination fraction \(\theta\) of two loci is the probability that a chromosome copy passed on in one meiosis is recombinant between them: it carries the allele of one of the parent's two copies at the first locus and the allele of the other copy at the second (Lecture 0 · Linkage). It is near 0 for adjacent SNPs and 1/2 for loci on different chromosomes.

\[ \begin{gathered} D_t = (1 - \theta)^t\, D_0 \\[4pt] r^2_t = (1 - \theta)^{2t}\, r^2_0 \end{gathered} \]
\(D_0\)
LD coefficient in a starting generation.
\(t\)
Number of generations since then.
\(\theta\)
Recombination fraction between the two loci, from 0 to 1/2.
\(D_t, r^2_t\)
\(D\) and \(r^2\) after \(t\) generations.

A copy passed on without recombination (a fraction \(1 - \theta\)) keeps the parents' haplotype and its association; a recombinant copy (a fraction \(\theta\)) joins alleles from two copies that are, under random mating, independent, so its contribution to \(D\) is 0. Each generation therefore multiplies \(D\) by \(1 - \theta\). If allele frequencies do not change, \(D_{\max}\) does not change either, so \(D'\) shrinks by the same factor, and \(r^2\), which grows with \(D^2\), by its square. Half of \(D\) is left after \(\ln 2 / (-\ln(1 - \theta)) \approx 0.69/\theta\) generations: about 614 generations for \(\theta = 0.00113\), and 1 generation for unlinked loci.

The law describes an infinitely large population, in which LD decays steadily toward linkage equilibrium (\(D = 0\)). In a real, finite population, random changes in haplotype frequencies from one generation to the next (genetic drift; Lecture 0 · Drift) create new LD while recombination removes it. That balance sets how much LD to expect at a given distance, below.

From map distance to θ: Haldane's map function

Genetic maps give positions in centimorgans (cM): the map distance between two loci is the expected number of crossovers between them in one meiosis, on the copy passed on, with 1 morgan = 100 cM = one expected crossover (Lecture 0 · Meiosis). The recombination fraction is a different quantity: the probability of an odd number of crossovers between the loci, because an even number puts the original alleles back together. If crossovers occur independently of each other along the chromosome, their number between the loci has a Poisson distribution with mean \(d\), and the probability that it is odd is \(e^{-d}(d + d^3/3! + d^5/5! + \cdots) = e^{-d}\sinh d\). That gives Haldane's map function15:

\[ \begin{gathered} \theta = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 - e^{-2d}\right) \\[4pt] d = -\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(1 - 2\theta) \end{gathered} \]
\(\theta\)
Recombination fraction (0 to 1/2).
\(d\)
Map distance in morgans (centimorgans divided by 100), 0 or more.

For small \(d\), \(e^{-2d} \approx 1 - 2d\) and \(\theta \approx d\): 1 cM gives \(\theta = 0.0099\), essentially 1%. For large \(d\), \(\theta\) levels off at 1/2: 50 cM gives 0.316, 100 cM gives 0.432 and 200 cM gives 0.491, so loci far apart on one chromosome behave almost as if they were on different chromosomes. The second line inverts the first.

To use the formula for two SNPs, multiply their physical distance by the local recombination rate. The genome-wide average is about 1.13 cM per megabase in the deCODE genetic map16, so 100 kb is about 0.113 cM and \(\theta \approx 0.00113\). The local rate varies by orders of magnitude: most crossovers fall in recombination hotspots one or two kilobases long, and stretches between hotspots recombine rarely (Lecture 0 · LD patterns). Real crossovers also interfere with each other (one crossover makes another one nearby less likely), which Kosambi's map function allows for17; over the short distances that matter for LD, every map function gives \(\theta \approx d\).

What to expect at a given distance, and why pairs scatter

Balancing drift against recombination gives Sved's approximation for the expected \(r^2\) between two loci18, which Lecture 0 introduces in words:

\[ \E[r^2] \approx \frac{1}{1 + 4N_{\mathrm{e}}\theta} \]
\(\E[r^2]\)
Expected \(r^2\) between two loci in the population, averaged over the random outcomes of drift.
\(N_{\mathrm{e}}\)
Effective population size: the size of an idealized randomly mating population with the same amount of drift (people).
\(\theta\)
Recombination fraction between the loci.

Drift creates LD at a rate proportional to \(1/N_{\mathrm{e}}\) and recombination removes it at a rate proportional to \(\theta\); their ratio is \(4N_{\mathrm{e}}\theta\). With an illustrative \(N_{\mathrm{e}} = 10{,}000\) and 1.13 cM/Mb, two SNPs 10 kb apart (\(4N_{\mathrm{e}}\theta \approx 4.5\)) have an expected \(r^2\) of about 0.18, 100 kb apart about 0.02, and 1 Mb apart about 0.002. A larger \(N_{\mathrm{e}}\) lowers the expected \(r^2\) at every distance. A finite sample adds a bias: even for unlinked SNPs, \(2n\hat{r}^2\) follows a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom, so \(\hat{r}^2\) averages \(1/(2n)\) instead of 0.

The approximation predicts the average, not individual pairs. Slide 86 shows how widely they scatter. It reproduces a figure from Pritchard and Przeworski13 with five panels, one per chromosomal region studied in 2000–2001: \(r\) (not \(r^2\)) for each pair of SNPs, against the distance between them, from 0 to 250 kb. Points above the solid line are pairs in significant LD at the 0.05 level; points above the dotted line, \(r = 0.316\) or \(r^2 = 0.1\), have what Kruglyak called "useful LD" for mapping19. High values cluster at short distances, but at any one distance some pairs are near 0 and others near 1, and the regions differ: in the chromosome 22 region \(r\) is low beyond a few tens of kilobases, while the chromosome X region (Taillon-Miller) has pairs with \(r\) above 0.5 at 200 kb and more. Four causes add up:

  • Recombination is uneven. Equal physical distances are unequal map distances; a hotspot between two SNPs 5 kb apart can break their LD, while SNPs 50 kb apart with no hotspot between them stay correlated.
  • Allele frequencies cap \(r^2\). Pairs whose allele frequencies differ cannot have high \(r^2\) at any distance (\(r^2_{\max}\) above).
  • Each pair has its own history. The genealogy of each stretch of chromosome is one random outcome of drift and recombination, so the spread around Sved's average is large even with perfect data.
  • Sampling error. Each \(\hat{r}\) comes from a sample of limited size, and rare alleles make \(|\hat{D}'| = 1\) common.

Slide 85 also lists selection: when an advantageous allele rises quickly in frequency, its whole surrounding haplotype rises with it before recombination can break it up, leaving a long stretch of high LD around the selected site (Lecture 0 · Selection).

These data mattered for study design. From population-genetic simulations, Kruglyak had predicted in 1999 that useful LD was "unlikely to extend beyond an average distance of roughly 3 kb in the general population", which would require about 500,000 SNPs for a genome-wide study19. Data such as slide 86's, and the larger survey of Reich et al., showed that LD often extends tens of kilobases in samples of European ancestry, and less far in samples of African ancestry20, which is the subject of the next section.

Later data agree: across the 26 populations of the 1000 Genomes Project, LD decays fastest with distance in African populations22.

Why. Under the decay law and Sved's balance, LD at a given distance is high when the population has had few generations of recombination since its haplotypes last shared ancestors, and low when it has had many. Human populations outside Africa descend from groups that left Africa and went through a bottleneck: a period of small population size, in which drift is strong and the number of distinct haplotypes shrinks (Lecture 0 · Human population history). A widely used model fitted to genetic data puts that bottleneck at an effective size of about 2,100 for roughly 4,750 generations23. Haplotypes that passed through it share recent common ancestors, and recombination has had fewer generations to cut them up since. Populations in Africa have kept a larger effective size for longer: their haplotypes trace back many more generations, recombination has shortened the shared segments, and drift has rebuilt less LD. In Sved's formula, a larger \(N_{\mathrm{e}}\) means a smaller \(r^2\) at every distance.

What it changes. The differences in LD have practical consequences for every analysis that relies on it:

  • Tag SNPs and arrays. Shorter LD means each SNP stands in for fewer neighbors. HapMap estimated that capturing every common HapMap SNP at \(r^2 \ge 0.8\) needed about 1.09 million tag SNPs in YRI but slightly more than 500,000 in CEU or CHB+JPT24. An array designed from European LD covers African-ancestry genomes less well (Lecture 0 · Tag SNPs).
  • Imputation. Imputation predicts unmeasured variants from the measured ones and a reference panel of sequenced haplotypes (Lecture 0 · Imputation). With shorter LD there are fewer, weaker proxies for each unmeasured variant, so accuracy is lower at the same array density unless the reference panel contains haplotypes similar to the study's; imputation accuracy was lowest in African populations in an early worldwide comparison25. Conversely, the reading notes that the long-range LD of isolated populations "improves imputation accuracy and power"1.
  • Fine-mapping. An association peak contains every SNP in high LD with the causal variant (Lecture 0 · Peaks). Shorter LD gives narrower peaks with fewer candidates, so combining populations with different LD narrows the list of likely causal variants26; in the reading's words, "differences in linkage disequilibrium structure among ancestries can narrow down the regional windows of associations"1.
  • LD references and polygenic scores. Methods that take LD from a reference panel give wrong answers when the panel's ancestry does not match the study's, and a polygenic score built from lead SNPs in one population predicts less well in others, partly because those SNPs tag the causal variants with different \(r^2\)27 (Applications and limits).

LD heatmaps: how to read one

An LD heatmap displays the LD of every pair of SNPs in a region at once. The common layout comes from the Haploview software28: the SNPs are listed in genome order along the top, and each pair of SNPs is a diamond below them, colored by its LD. Pair \(i, j\) sits where the diagonal running down to the right from SNP \(i\) meets the diagonal running down to the left from SNP \(j\), so neighbors form the top row and pairs further apart lie lower down. Only half of the symmetric \(M \times M\) matrix is drawn, rotated 45°.

Haploview offers several color schemes29. In the \(r^2\) scheme, white is 0 and black is 1, with grays between. The standard scheme combines \(D'\) with a LOD: the base-10 logarithm of the likelihood ratio comparing the estimated haplotype frequencies with frequencies under no LD (\(D = 0\)), so a LOD of 2 means the data are 100 times more probable with the estimated LD than without. Pairs with \(D' < 1\) and LOD below 2 are white, \(D' = 1\) with LOD below 2 is blue (a haplotype is missing, but there are too few data to be confident), \(D' < 1\) with LOD of 2 or more is pink to red (redder for larger \(D'\)), and \(D' = 1\) with LOD of 2 or more is bright red. A four-gamete scheme colors a pair black when fewer than four haplotypes are seen. Haploview can also print the value inside each diamond; with \(D'\) shown, a diamond with \(D' = 1\) is left empty29.

Haplotype blocks drawn on heatmaps are defined from \(D'\). In the definition of Gabriel et al., two SNPs are in "strong LD" if the 90% confidence interval of \(D'\) runs from above 0.70 to at least 0.98, and a block is a run of SNPs in which at least 95% of informative pairs are in strong LD30. PLINK's --blocks uses the same definition9. An alternative uses the four-gamete test: a block ends where all four haplotypes of a pair are seen at more than a small frequency.

How this simulation works

The widget does not simulate every person in every generation. It uses the coalescent, which traces the ancestry of the sampled chromosome copies backward in time. In a population of \(N\) people (\(2N\) copies), two copies have their most recent common ancestor in the previous generation with probability \(1/(2N)\), so lineages merge (coalesce) at that rate; when a recombination falls between two positions, the ancestry of the left and right parts of a copy splits into two lineages. Following lineages back until all have merged gives a genealogy for every stretch of the region (Hudson's algorithm for the coalescent with recombination, as in the program ms31). A SNP is one mutation placed on a branch of the genealogy at its position; the copies below that branch carry the derived allele.

  • Samples: 200 chromosome copies (100 people) from each population. People are formed by pairing consecutive copies, for the "unphased genotypes" option.
  • Demography, simplified from the out-of-Africa model of Gutenkunst et al.23, in generations before the present: the AFR-like population has 12,300 people back to 8,800 generations ago and 7,300 before that; the EUR-like population grows from 1,000 people 848 generations ago to about 29,700 today, has 2,100 people from 848 to 5,600 generations ago (the bottleneck), and then merges with the AFR-like population. Migration between the two is \(25 \times 10^{-5}\) per lineage per generation during the bottleneck and \(3 \times 10^{-5}\) afterwards.
  • Recombination map: 0.47 cM/Mb in the background plus four 2-kb hotspots of 0.034 cM each, about 60% of the region's 0.23 cM (an average of about 1.13 cM/Mb).
  • SNP choice: 48 sites, each with the derived allele at a frequency of at least 5% in both populations, so the two heatmaps show the same SNPs. This mimics array SNPs, which are chosen to be common, and is one reason the real HapMap contrast (slide 95) is similar in size.
  • Measures: from phased haplotypes, \(D\), \(D'\), \(r^2\) and the LOD are computed directly; from unphased genotypes, \(r^2\) is the squared correlation of allele counts (as in plink --r2) and \(D'\) and the LOD come from EM haplotype frequencies (two starting points; the higher likelihood is kept). Tag SNPs are counted greedily: repeatedly pick the SNP that covers the most uncovered SNPs at \(r^2 \ge 0.8\).

Simplifications: one simplified demographic history, no selection, no gene conversion, a fixed map, and an exact SNP choice. The numbers illustrate the direction and rough size of the population difference, not any real population.

LD in the data: PLINK, LD matrices and reference panels Core

This section follows LD from a genotype file to the outputs you will meet: PLINK's .ld files, LD matrices, heatmaps and web tools built on reference panels. It ends where the practical picks LD up, at QC step 3.

Correlation of allele counts stands in for haplotype r²

A genotype file gives each person an allele count per SNP: 0, 1 or 2 copies of the counted allele (From molecules to SNP data). The simplest LD estimate ignores haplotypes altogether and correlates two columns of allele counts across people. Under random mating it estimates the same \(r\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \Cov(X_1, X_2) = 2D \\[4pt] \Var(X_1) = 2p_{\mathrm{A}}\,p_{\mathrm{a}} \\[4pt] \operatorname{Corr}(X_1, X_2) = r \end{gathered} \]
\(X_1\)
Number of A alleles a person carries at SNP 1 (0, 1 or 2).
\(X_2\)
Number of G alleles the same person carries at SNP 2 (0, 1 or 2), with \(\Var(X_2) = 2p_{\mathrm{G}}\,p_{\mathrm{g}}\).
\(D\)
The LD coefficient of the haplotypes.
\(r\)
The haplotype correlation, \(D/\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{a}}p_{\mathrm{G}}p_{\mathrm{g}}}\).

Each count is the sum of two 0/1 codes, one per chromosome copy: \(X_1 = a_1 + a_2\) and \(X_2 = g_1 + g_2\). The covariance splits into four terms. The two within-copy terms, \(\Cov(a_1, g_1)\) and \(\Cov(a_2, g_2)\), are each \(D\). The two between-copy terms are 0 under random mating, because a person's two copies are independent draws. So the covariance is \(2D\), the variances are doubled Bernoulli variances, and the factors of 2 cancel in the correlation. Anything that makes a person's two copies dependent (inbreeding, population structure) or that distorts genotypes (calling errors) breaks the equality.

In large samples from one population the two estimates are close; in the EM widget's simulated sample of 200 people they differ only in the second or third decimal. In small samples they can differ a lot: slide 117's eight people give a correlation of allele counts of \(r^2 = 0.333\) against the haplotype estimate 0.6, because five of eight people being double heterozygotes is far from Hardy–Weinberg proportions. The allele-count version needs no EM and no phase, which makes it fast enough for millions of pairs; it is what plink --r2 reports by default9.

Computing LD with PLINK 1.9

PLINK 1.9 is the version the practical uses. Its LD commands read a binary fileset (.bed, .bim, .fam; PLINK files inside out) and, like all of PLINK's LD calculations, use only founders, the people whose father and mother IDs in the .fam file are both 09. In data from unrelated people, such as the practical's, everyone is a founder.

The default report. With no options, --r2 writes a table of pairs of nearby variants:

bash 
$ plink --bfile mydata --r2 --out mydata
# same as: --r2 --ld-window 10 --ld-window-kb 1000 --ld-window-r2 0.2

It reports only pairs that lie within a window of 10 variants and 1,000 kb and have \(r^2\) of at least 0.2. Pairs below 0.2 are not written at all, which is convenient for finding strong LD and misleading if you want to see how LD decays.

Every pair in a window, with D′. To study a region, widen the window and drop the \(r^2\) filter:

plink --bfile mydata --r2 dprime --ld-window-kb 500 --ld-window 99999 --ld-window-r2 0 --out mydata
  1. plink: PLINK 1.9.
  2. --bfile mydata: read mydata.bed, mydata.bim and mydata.fam.
  3. --r2 dprime: report \(r^2\) and add a DP column with \(|D'|\). With dprime, both values come from the maximum-likelihood haplotype frequencies (solved as a cubic), not from allele-count correlations; dprime-signed keeps the sign of \(D'\), and --r instead of --r2 reports signed \(r\).
  4. --ld-window-kb 500: pair variants up to 500 kb apart (default 1,000).
  5. --ld-window 99999: lift the limit on how many variants may lie between the two (default 10), so the kb limit is the only one.
  6. --ld-window-r2 0: report every pair, not only those with \(r^2 \ge 0.2\).
  7. --out mydata: write mydata.ld (and mydata.log).

LD matrices

An LD matrix for \(M\) variants is the \(M \times M\) matrix whose entry \((i, j)\) is the correlation \(r_{ij}\) between variants \(i\) and \(j\) (or its square), with ones on the diagonal. It is symmetric, and in a region it is close to block-diagonal: large values in squares along the diagonal (the LD blocks), small values elsewhere. An LD heatmap is a picture of half of it.

  • Size. The matrix has \(M^2\) entries. For 10,000 variants, a modest region of a dense imputed dataset, that is \(10^8\) numbers: about 800 MB in double precision (PLINK's bin), 400 MB in single precision (bin4), and about 1 GB as text. Tools therefore store LD for limited windows, in sparse or compressed formats, or only for pruned or tagged variants.
  • Sign. Many methods need signed \(r\), not \(r^2\): the sign says whether the counted alleles of the two variants tend to occur together, so it depends on which allele is counted at each variant. The allele coding of the LD matrix must match the allele coding of the summary statistics it is used with.
  • Uses. Fine-mapping methods such as SuSiE combine GWAS summary statistics with an LD matrix to find the variants most likely to be causal; conditional analysis with GCTA-COJO tests for further signals in a region from summary statistics and LD1; LD scores, row sums of \(r^2\) within a window, drive LD score regression (Lecture 0 · LD scores).
  • Matching. When the matrix comes from a reference panel instead of the GWAS sample itself, its ancestry must match the GWAS sample, or these methods report spurious or missed signals (populations section).

When you have no genotypes: reference panels and LDlink

Much LD work starts from published summary statistics, without individual genotypes. LD then comes from a reference panel, a public set of genotypes or haplotypes from people sampled to represent particular populations (Lecture 0 · LD reference panels). The most used for LD is the 1000 Genomes Project: 2,504 people from 26 populations, grouped into five continental superpopulations labeled AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR and SAS22. Population labels describe how people were recruited; they are reference points for genetic similarity, not categories that every person fits (Lecture 0 · Reference samples).

LDlink is a free web application from the National Cancer Institute's Division of Cancer Epidemiology and Genetics (the workshop's host) that computes LD in 1000 Genomes data for any of its populations, on genome build GRCh37 or GRCh3832,14. Its modules include:

  • LDpair: two variants; the 2 × 2 table of haplotype counts, \(D'\), \(r^2\), a chi-square test with its p-value, and which alleles go together when \(r^2 > 0.1\).
  • LDmatrix: an interactive heatmap of \(D'\) and \(r^2\) for a list of variants (up to 300 on the web page).
  • LDproxy: every variant within 500 kb of a query variant, with its \(r^2\), plotted with the recombination rate; used to find proxies for a variant that is missing from your data.
  • LDpop: allele frequencies and LD of two variants across all 1000 Genomes populations.
  • LDhap: haplotypes and their frequencies for up to 30 variants; SNPclip: LD pruning of a variant list; SNPchip: which commercial arrays contain given variants. Further modules link variants and their proxies to GWAS Catalog traits (LDtrait), gene expression (LDexpress), association results (LDassoc) and LD scores (LDscore).

LDlink can also be queried programmatically after registering for an access token, for example from R with the CRAN package LDlinkR14.

From LD to LD pruning (QC step 3)

Several QC steps need SNPs that are roughly independent of each other, because otherwise regions of strong LD count many times over. The practical prunes with

bash 
$ plink --bfile penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01 --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.3 --out penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01

which slides a window of 50 variants along each chromosome, 5 variants at a time, and within each window greedily removes variants until no pair has allele-count \(r^2\) above 0.3. It writes the kept IDs to a .prune.in file and the removed ones to .prune.out9,33. The pruned set is then used for relatedness (step 4), heterozygosity (step 5) and principal components (step 6). QC step 3: LD pruning explains the choice of window and threshold and what pruning leaves out; Lecture 0 · Pruning and clumping contrasts pruning with clumping.

In the data Where LD shows up
  • The genotype matrix: neighboring columns are correlated; plink --r2 turns that into a table of pairs in .ld.
  • QC: .prune.in and .prune.out from --indep-pairwise (step 3).
  • GWAS results: peaks of neighboring variants with small p-values; regional plots color points by \(r^2\) with the lead SNP.
  • Downstream: LD matrices and LD scores from a reference panel, in fine-mapping, conditional analysis and LD score regression; LDlink for single questions.
Pitfall Four easy ways to misread LD
  • Reading \(|D'| = 1\) as "perfectly correlated". It means a haplotype is missing; \(r^2\) can still be tiny, and one SNP is then a poor proxy for the other.
  • Trusting \(|\hat{D}'|\) for rare variants or small samples. A rare haplotype is easily missed by chance, which forces \(|\hat{D}'| = 1\).
  • Using distance as a proxy for LD. A hotspot can separate two SNPs a few kilobases apart, and LD can reach far in a region without one.
  • Using an LD reference from the wrong population. \(r^2\) between the same two SNPs can differ severalfold between populations.
Key points
  • LD between two SNPs is a property of the 2 × 2 table of haplotype frequencies. With the allele frequencies fixed, the table has one free number, \(D = p_{\mathrm{AG}} - p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{G}} = p_{\mathrm{AG}}p_{\mathrm{ag}} - p_{\mathrm{Ag}}p_{\mathrm{aG}}\).
  • A genotype table fixes everyone's haplotypes except the double heterozygotes. EM estimates the haplotype frequencies by splitting each double heterozygote between AG + ag and Ag + aG in proportion to their current probabilities, then recounting, until nothing changes; the likelihood can have more than one peak.
  • \(D' = D/D_{\max}\): \(|D'| = 1\) exactly when a haplotype is missing, read as no evidence of recombination, but inflated in small samples and for rare alleles. \(r^2 = D^2/(p_{\mathrm{A}}p_{\mathrm{a}}p_{\mathrm{G}}p_{\mathrm{g}})\): the squared correlation, 1 only with equal allele frequencies, and the measure that sets power, tagging and imputation.
  • From counts: \(\hat{r}^2 = (n_{\mathrm{AG}}n_{\mathrm{ag}} - n_{\mathrm{Ag}}n_{\mathrm{aG}})^2/(n_{\mathrm{A}}n_{\mathrm{a}}n_{\mathrm{G}}n_{\mathrm{g}})\), and \(2n\hat{r}^2\) is the chi-square test of no LD. Slide 124: \(\hat{D} = 0.1875\), \(\hat{D}' = 1\), \(\hat{r}^2 = 0.6\), chi-square 9.6.
  • Each generation keeps a fraction \(1 - \theta\) of \(D\); Haldane's map function turns map distance into \(\theta\); drift rebuilds LD, so the expected \(r^2\) is about \(1/(1 + 4N_{\mathrm{e}}\theta)\). Individual pairs scatter widely around that average.
  • LD extends less far in samples of African ancestry: more tag SNPs are needed, imputation is harder, fine-mapping is sharper, and LD references must match the study population.
  • plink --r2 reports the correlation of allele counts for nearby pairs with \(r^2 \ge 0.2\) by default; dprime switches to maximum-likelihood haplotype frequencies; LDlink answers LD questions from 1000 Genomes data; --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.3 prunes for QC.

Check your understanding

A 3 × 3 genotype table for 100 people has, by rows GG, Gg, gg and columns AA, Aa, aa: 30, 10, 2 / 8, 20, 4 / 1, 5, 20. What are the known haplotype counts, how many people are double heterozygotes, and what is the first EM estimate of the AG frequency from a no-LD start?

Count the copies of people outside the center cell. AG: two from each AA GG person, one from each AA Gg and each Aa GG person: \(2 \times 30 + 8 + 10 = 78\). Ag: \(2 \times 1 + 8 + 5 = 15\) (AA gg, AA Gg, Aa gg). aG: \(2 \times 2 + 4 + 10 = 18\) (aa GG, aa Gg, Aa GG). ag: \(2 \times 20 + 4 + 5 = 49\) (aa gg, aa Gg, Aa gg). The center cell has \(m = 20\) double heterozygotes. Check: \(78 + 15 + 18 + 49 + 2 \times 20 = 200\) copies.

At a no-LD start \(D = 0\), so \(w^{(0)} = 0.5\), and \(\hat{p}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{AG}} = (78 + 20 \times 0.5)/200 = 0.44\). (The sample allele frequencies are \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{A}} = 113/200 = 0.565\) and \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{G}} = 116/200 = 0.58\), so the no-LD value was \(0.565 \times 0.58 \approx 0.328\); the data already push AG well above it.)

Why does EM assign all five double heterozygotes on slide 117 to AG + ag, rather than splitting them?

The known copies contain no aG haplotype at all. The probability that a double heterozygote is Ag + aG is proportional to \(p_{\mathrm{Ag}}p_{\mathrm{aG}}\), and aG gets copies only from that option, so a small \(p_{\mathrm{aG}}\) makes the option less likely, which makes the next \(p_{\mathrm{aG}}\) smaller still. Each iteration multiplies \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{aG}}\) by about 0.21, and in the limit it is 0 and \(w = 1\). In likelihood terms, the observed table is most probable when aG does not exist. It is the most likely answer, not a certainty: with only eight people, a population in which aG has a small frequency would produce this table almost as often.

Haplotype counts among 200 chromosome copies are AG 60, Ag 20, aG 10, ag 110. Compute \(\hat{D}\), \(\hat{D}'\), \(\hat{r}^2\) and the chi-square statistic.

Allele counts: \(n_{\mathrm{A}} = 80\), \(n_{\mathrm{a}} = 120\), \(n_{\mathrm{G}} = 70\), \(n_{\mathrm{g}} = 130\). Cross-product: \(60 \times 110 - 20 \times 10 = 6{,}400\).

  • \(\hat{D} = 6{,}400/200^2 = 0.16\). (Check: \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{AG}} - \hat{p}_{\mathrm{A}}\hat{p}_{\mathrm{G}} = 0.30 - 0.40 \times 0.35 = 0.16\).)
  • \(\hat{D} > 0\), so \(\hat{D}' = 6{,}400/\min(80 \times 130,\, 120 \times 70) = 6{,}400/8{,}400 \approx 0.76\).
  • \(\hat{r}^2 = 6{,}400^2/(80 \times 120 \times 70 \times 130) = 40{,}960{,}000/87{,}360{,}000 \approx 0.47\).
  • \(X^2 = 200 \times 0.469 \approx 93.8\) with 1 degree of freedom: overwhelming evidence of LD (a p-value below 10⁻²⁰).

All four haplotypes are present, so \(|\hat{D}'| < 1\): recombination (or a repeat mutation) has occurred in the history of these SNPs.

Two SNPs are 500 kb apart in a region with 1.2 cM/Mb. What is their recombination fraction, and after how many generations is half of their D left?

Map distance: \(0.5 \text{ Mb} \times 1.2 \text{ cM/Mb} = 0.6\) cM \(= 0.006\) morgans. Haldane: \(\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - e^{-0.012}) \approx 0.00596\), essentially equal to \(d\) at this distance. Half-life: \(\ln 2/(-\ln(1 - 0.00596)) \approx 116\) generations (the shortcut \(0.69/\theta\) gives the same). After 1,000 generations only \((1 - 0.00596)^{1000} \approx 0.0025\), about 0.25%, of the starting \(D\) is left, which is why SNPs this far apart are usually in weak LD unless the association is recent.

A pair of SNPs has \(|D'| = 1\) but \(r^2 = 0.05\). What does that tell you, and could one serve as a tag SNP for the other?

\(|D'| = 1\) means one of the four haplotypes is missing from the sample: no recombinant has been seen. \(r^2 = 0.05\) means one SNP predicts the other poorly. Both hold when the allele frequencies differ a lot, for example a rare allele that arose on one copy of a common haplotype: for allele frequencies 0.5 and 0.05, \(r^2\) cannot exceed about 0.053 even with \(|D'| = 1\). As a tag, the pair is poor: testing the tag in \(n\) people gives about the power of testing the other SNP in \(0.05n\) people. If the rare allele is not rare in the sample, check the sample size too: a missing haplotype is weak evidence when the data are few.

Why does a genotyping array designed from European LD capture the genomes of people of African ancestry less well, and what does that change for imputation?

Populations of African ancestry have a longer history at a larger effective size; non-African populations went through the out-of-Africa bottleneck. More generations of recombination and weaker drift leave shorter LD in African-ancestry genomes, so each array SNP is correlated with fewer neighbors at high \(r^2\). HapMap estimated about 1.09 million tag SNPs to cover common variants at \(r^2 \ge 0.8\) in YRI against slightly more than 500,000 in CEU. For imputation, each unmeasured variant has fewer and weaker proxies on the array, so accuracy drops unless the reference panel contains haplotypes like the study's; this was the pattern in early worldwide comparisons, where African populations had the lowest accuracy.

You run plink --bfile mydata --r2 --out mydata to plot LD decay with distance. The plot shows no pairs with \(r^2\) below 0.2 and none further than about 10 variants apart. Why, and how do you fix it?

Those are the defaults: --ld-window-r2 0.2 drops pairs with \(r^2\) below 0.2, and --ld-window 10 with --ld-window-kb 1000 pairs each variant only with nearby variants. For a decay plot, add --ld-window-r2 0 --ld-window 99999 --ld-window-kb 500 (or whatever distance you want) and restrict to a region or a subset of variants, because the number of pairs grows quickly. Plot the mean \(r^2\) in distance bins, as on slide 95.

For slide 117's eight people, the correlation of allele counts gives \(r^2 = 0.333\), but the haplotype estimate is 0.6. Which one does plink --r2 report, and why do they differ?

By default plink --r2 reports the squared correlation of allele counts, 0.333. (With --r2 dprime it would report the haplotype-based 0.6.) The two agree in expectation only under random mating, when a person's two chromosome copies are independent; then the between-copy covariance terms vanish and the correlation of counts equals \(r\). Slide 117's table is far from Hardy–Weinberg proportions (five of eight people are heterozygous at both SNPs) and has only eight people, so the two estimates differ. In large samples from one population they are usually close.
