QC step 7: merging with 1000 Genomes and reference-based PCA

Where do the study's people sit relative to people sampled on several continents? To answer this, the practical combines the study's genotypes with those of the 1000 Genomes reference samples and runs a principal component analysis (PCA) on the combined file. The combination works only if both files write every shared SNP with the same two letters, read from the same strand of the DNA, and most of this step is about making sure they do.

Step 7 runs the QC pipeline twice. For SNPs, the statistic is the set of allele letters that the two files report, and the rule "three or more letters" decides which SNPs PLINK refuses to merge. For people, the statistic is a position on principal components computed together with 2,504 reference people, and nobody is removed: the positions describe genetic similarity to the reference samples.

  • A reference panel gives the axes meaning. On its own, the study's PCA in step 6 shows clusters without names. Reference people with documented sampling labels show what each direction separates (what the step finds).
  • Merging compares letters, not biology. PLINK matches SNPs by ID and refuses a SNP when the two files together show three or more allele letters. --flip repairs SNPs reported on the other strand, but A/T and C/G SNPs pass the letter test whichever strand they are on (the statistics).
  • Merge, flip, merge again, then keep only shared SNPs. After the merge, a SNP typed in only one file is missing for everyone in the other (for PennCATH, most of its SNPs); --geno 0.1 removes such SNPs before PCA (run it).
  • Study people over reference clusters. The plot shows whether the study's people overlap one reference group, spread between groups, or sit apart from all of them (look at the PCA).
  • Similarity, not identity. A position near the EUR cluster means "genetically similar to the 1000 Genomes samples labeled EUR", not "European"; any rule for flagging people is set in advance and recorded (choose a rule).

What the step finds Core

Step 6 ran PCA on the study's people alone. Its first PCs show the largest directions of genetic variation among those people, and clusters, if any, but nothing in that plot says what a cluster is. A cluster can be a group of people whose families come from one region, a set of samples genotyped in a different batch, or a group of relatives. Step 7 adds people whose sampling is documented. If the study's people fall on top of the reference people sampled in, say, Europe, and away from those sampled in East Asia, the axes acquire an interpretation in terms of the reference samples.

The step does four jobs:

  • Describe the study's genetic similarity to the reference groups. The practical's question for PennCATH is which 1000 Genomes clusters its participants overlap.
  • Find people who sit apart from the rest of the study. A person far from the study's main group may have different genetic ancestry from most participants, or the sample may be swapped or contaminated. Steps 4–6 found some of these problems; step 7 shows them against a reference.
  • Inform the analysis plan. Whether to analyze everyone together and adjust for principal components (Session 2), to analyze groups separately, or to restrict the analysis to a prespecified group is a design decision, and it needs this picture.
  • Test the data handling. The merge only succeeds when the two files agree on every shared SNP's alleles, so problems with strand, allele coding and SNP names surface here first.

Why it matters for the association tests: allele frequencies differ between populations (Lecture 0 · Frequency differences). If cases and controls differ in genetic ancestry, every SNP whose frequency differs between the groups looks associated with the disease. That is population stratification, a form of confounding. Steps 6–8 describe the structure; Session 2 adjusts for it.

The reference: 1000 Genomes phase 3

The 1000 Genomes Project sequenced the whole genomes of 2,504 people from 26 populations and published their genotypes at more than 88 million variant sites.3 Each person carries two labels: a population (for example GBR, "British in England and Scotland") and one of five super-populations: AFR (African), AMR (admixed American), EAS (East Asian), EUR (European) and SAS (South Asian). Lecture 0 lists all 26 populations with their sizes (Lecture 0 · Reference samples). The practical describes the labels as "defined initially using sampling criteria based on geography, community engagement, and strict self-reported grandparental ancestry".1 They record how people were recruited. They are not measurements of anyone's genome, and the populations overlap genetically.

The workshop provides a reduced version of the reference: the PLINK fileset all_pops_1kgenomes_markersForPCA, with all 2,504 people and 17,490 SNPs on chromosomes 1–22 chosen for PCA and already LD-pruned (the practical says so; the selection procedure is not documented). In its .fam file the family ID and the individual ID are both the 1000 Genomes sample name, such as HG00096, and sex and phenotype are unknown. The labels are not in the fileset. They are in a separate text file from the 1000 Genomes FTP site, integrated_call_samples_v3.20130502.ALL.panel, with one line per person and the columns sample, pop, super_pop and gender. The practical gets that file by cloning the GitHub repository of akt, a toolkit that happens to include a copy of it.

What goes wrong when two files meet

Two datasets genotyped in different laboratories, on different arrays or by sequencing, describe the same SNP in their own way. The merge exposes every difference:

  • Strand. DNA has two complementary strands: A pairs with T and C with G (Lecture 0). A SNP that one file writes as A/G (read on one strand) another file can write as T/C (read on the other). Array manufacturers define their own strand conventions, so array data and sequence data often disagree for a share of the SNPs.
  • Allele order. One file may list the alleles as A/G and the other as G/A. PLINK's allele 1 is usually the minor allele in the file that wrote it (The data: PLINK files), so the order differs between datasets whenever the minor allele differs. This is harmless for merging: PLINK compares sets of letters.
  • A/T and C/G SNPs. When the two alleles are complements of each other, the SNP reads A/T on both strands. A strand difference leaves no trace in the letters (strand-ambiguous SNPs).
  • Names and positions. PLINK matches SNPs by ID only. The same SNP under two names (an old and a merged rsID, or chr:pos in one file) becomes two SNPs, each missing in one dataset. The same name at two positions, for example because the files use different genome builds (GRCh37 or GRCh38), triggers the warning Multiple positions seen for variant.
  • Different SNP sets. An array types a few hundred thousand SNPs; the reference file here has 17,490. Only SNPs in both files carry information about how the two groups of people compare.

The statistics: allele letters, then principal components Core

The merge rule: count the letters

--bmerge reads the study fileset (the --bfile) and the reference fileset. The result contains the union of the people and the union of the SNPs: everyone from both files, and every SNP that appears in either. After writing the merged list of people (a .fam file) and before writing any genotypes, PLINK checks each SNP whose ID appears in both .bim files. It collects the allele letters of the two rows, ignoring a 0 (the code PLINK writes when it saw only one allele), and counts the distinct letters. Two or fewer: the SNP merges, whatever the order of allele 1 and allele 2. Three or more: the SNP's ID is written to a file named out-merge.missnp, and when the check has finished, PLINK stops with the error N variants with 3+ alleles present and writes no merged .bed or .bim.2 (The -merge in the name comes from the temporary file name PLINK uses for the merged fileset before --make-bed writes the final one.)

The rule tests letters, not genotypes. It catches a strand difference only when the complement letters are new letters, so it misses every A/T and C/G SNP. It also cannot detect a SNP that is named the same in both files but is a different variant with the same two letters.

What --flip changes, and what it cannot fix

--flip file reads a list of SNP IDs and replaces each allele letter of those SNPs by its complement: A↔T, C↔G. The genotypes themselves are unchanged; a person who carried two copies of allele 1 still does, but allele 1 is now written with the other strand's letter. The practical flips exactly the SNPs listed in .missnp and merges again. If the second merge succeeds, every listed SNP was a strand difference. If it fails again, the new .missnp lists SNPs whose alleles differ on both strands; those are excluded, for example with --exclude.

A/T and C/G SNPs never reach .missnp, so the practical never flips them. If one file reports such a SNP on the other strand, the merge silently pairs the study's A with the reference's A although the study's A is the reference's T. Every study person's genotype is then counted for the wrong allele: someone with two copies of the reference's A is recorded with none. Allele frequencies can reveal this when they are not close to 0.5.

\[ d_{\mathrm{same}} = \lvert \hat{p}_{\mathrm{S}} - \hat{p}_{\mathrm{R}} \rvert \] \[ d_{\mathrm{flip}} = \lvert \hat{p}_{\mathrm{S}} - (1 - \hat{p}_{\mathrm{R}}) \rvert \]
\(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{S}}\)
The frequency of one letter of an A/T or C/G SNP (say A) in the study, estimated from the study's genotypes; between 0 and 1.
\(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{R}}\)
The frequency of the same letter in the reference, best taken from the reference group most similar to the study (for PennCATH, the EUR samples).
\(d_{\mathrm{same}}\)
How far apart the two frequencies are if both files read the SNP from the same strand.
\(d_{\mathrm{flip}}\)
How far apart they are if the files read opposite strands: then the study's A is the reference's T, whose frequency is \(1 - \hat{p}_{\mathrm{R}}\).

Two populations with similar genetic ancestry have similar allele frequencies, so the correct reading gives the smaller distance. With \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{S}} = 0.79\) and \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{R}} = 0.22\), \(d_{\mathrm{same}} = 0.57\) and \(d_{\mathrm{flip}} = 0.01\): the study reports the other strand, and the SNP must be flipped. Choosing the smaller distance is the same as asking whether both frequencies lie on the same side of 0.5, because \(d_{\mathrm{flip}}^2 - d_{\mathrm{same}}^2 = (2\hat{p}_{\mathrm{S}} - 1)(2\hat{p}_{\mathrm{R}} - 1)\). Near 0.5 the two readings give almost the same frequencies: with \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{S}} = 0.47\) and \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{R}} = 0.45\) the distances are 0.02 and 0.08, and a frequency difference of a few percentage points between two populations could swap the answer. Harmonization software therefore gives up above a minor allele frequency of 0.42 (the TwoSampleMR default) and drops the SNP.

What to notice. Start with "Opposite strand": the first merge sees four letters, --flip turns T/C into A/G, and the second merge goes through. Switch to "A/T SNP, opposite strand": the merge never complains, nothing is flipped, and only the frequencies show that every study genotype now counts the wrong allele. Change the letters yourself to check any row of the table.

The usual remedies are to exclude A/T and C/G SNPs from both files before merging, as Anderson et al. do,4 to resolve them with the frequency comparison above, or to align both files to a documented strand (the forward strand of the reference genome) before merging. A PCA uses thousands of SNPs, so dropping a few dozen A/T and C/G SNPs costs almost nothing. The workshop's reference file contains such SNPs (count them with the command in the real-data section), and the practical keeps them, as it notes: "a production analysis would typically resolve them with additional reference/allele-frequency information or exclude ambiguous variants when appropriate".1

SNPs typed in only one file

After the merge, a SNP that only the study typed has no genotypes for the reference people, and a SNP that only the reference typed has none for the study's people. PLINK stores those genotypes as missing. The missing fraction of such a SNP depends only on the two sample sizes.

\[ m_{\mathrm{S}} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{R}}}{n_{\mathrm{S}} + n_{\mathrm{R}}} \] \[ m_{\mathrm{R}} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{S}}}{n_{\mathrm{S}} + n_{\mathrm{R}}} \]
\(n_{\mathrm{S}}\)
The number of study people in the merged file.
\(n_{\mathrm{R}}\)
The number of reference people in the merged file (2,504).
\(m_{\mathrm{S}}\)
The missing fraction of a SNP typed only in the study (at least this much; study genotypes that failed add to it).
\(m_{\mathrm{R}}\)
The missing fraction of a SNP typed only in the reference.

Everyone in the other file lacks the SNP, so the missing fraction is the other file's share of the people. For PennCATH after step 1, with 1,380 study people, the practical computes \(m_{\mathrm{S}} = 2504/3884 \approx 0.645\) and \(m_{\mathrm{R}} = 1380/3884 \approx 0.355\).1 Both are far above 0.1, so --geno 0.1 removes every one-file SNP and leaves the SNPs typed in both. The filter works only while each file holds more than a tenth of the people: \(m_{\mathrm{R}} > 0.1\) exactly when \(n_{\mathrm{S}} > n_{\mathrm{R}}/9\). A study of 200 people merged with the same reference gives \(m_{\mathrm{R}} = 200/2704 \approx 0.074\), and the reference-only SNPs would survive.

In the lab, the merged file holds ? people: the ? toy study people left after step 1 and the ? people of toy_1kg. Each file holds far more than a tenth of them, so --geno 0.1 removes every one-file SNP here too; a question at the end of the page computes both fractions with the shell.

Without this filter the PCA would use thousands of SNPs that are missing for one whole group. Their missingness pattern alone separates the study from the reference, and the first PC would measure "which file a person came from". The more robust approach for any sample size is to make the list of shared SNP IDs explicitly and keep only those (below).

PCA on the merged data

The PCA itself is the computation of step 6: PLINK standardizes each person's genotype at each SNP by the SNP's allele frequency, builds the relationship matrix between all pairs of people, and writes the top 20 eigenvectors as PC1–PC20.2 Three things differ here:

  • The SNPs. Only SNPs typed in both files survive --geno 0.1, and --maf 0.05 then removes SNPs whose minor allele frequency in the merged sample is below 5%. The practical does not apply the study's .prune.in list from step 3: it was built from the study's full SNP set, while the reference's SNPs were already pruned.1
  • The people who define the axes. PCA finds the directions of largest variation among everyone in the file. With 2,504 reference people from five continental groups, those directions mostly separate the reference groups. Variation within the study's own group gets the later PCs or none.
  • Joint analysis rather than projection. The practical computes the PCs from everyone together, so the axes would shift if the study were larger or more varied. An alternative computes the PCs from the reference people only and places the study's people on those fixed axes (projection; below).

Each output file, column by column:

-merge.missnp
Written only when the merge fails. One SNP ID per line, sorted, each a SNP with three or more distinct allele letters across the two files. It is the input of --flip.
.eigenvec
One line per person in the merged file, no header, separated by spaces: family ID, individual ID, then PC1 to PC20. In R, fread names the columns V1, V2, …, so PC1 is V3. For the reference people both IDs are the sample name (HG00096); for the study's people they are the study's IDs.
.eigenval
Twenty numbers, one per line: the eigenvalue of each PC, which measures how much variation it captures (step 6).
.panel (1000 Genomes)
Tab-separated with a header: sample (the sample name, matching the reference's family and individual IDs), pop (one of the 26 population codes), super_pop (AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR or SAS) and gender. The study's people are not in it. The real file's header line ends with two extra tabs, so it names six columns over four columns of data: R's fread warns "Detected 6 column names but the data has 4 columns" and adds two empty columns. The warning is harmless. The toy panel has no extra tabs.

Predict which SNPs the merge will refuse

The merge matches SNPs by ID and then compares their allele letters, so the two .bim files are enough to predict which shared SNPs it will refuse. The first suggested command makes that prediction before anything is merged:

bash 
$ awk 'BEGIN { comp["A"] = "T"; comp["T"] = "A"; comp["C"] = "G"; comp["G"] = "C" }
  FILENAME ~ /toy_1kg/ { ref[$2] = $5 $6; pos[$2] = $1 ":" $4; next }
  ($2 in ref) {
    if (($1 ":" $4) != pos[$2]) moved++
    if (($5 $6) == ref[$2] || ($6 $5) == ref[$2]) kind = (($5 $6) ~ /^(AT|TA|CG|GC)$/) ? "A/T or C/G" : "same alleles"
    else if ((comp[$5] comp[$6]) == ref[$2] || (comp[$6] comp[$5]) == ref[$2]) kind = "complementary alleles"
    else kind = "other"
    n[kind]++
  }
  END { split("same alleles,complementary alleles,A/T or C/G,other", kinds, ",")
        for (i = 1; i <= 4; i++) print n[kinds[i]] + 0, kinds[i]
        print moved + 0, "at a different position" }' \
  ${reference_dir}/toy_1kg.bim ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.bim

awk reads the reference's .bim first: for its lines (FILENAME ~ /toy_1kg/) it stores the two allele letters, written next to each other as one string such as AG, and the position, both keyed by SNP ID, and next skips the remaining rules. The array comp maps each letter to its complement. For every study SNP whose ID the reference also has ($2 in ref), the command counts one of four kinds:

  • same alleles: the study's two letters are the reference's, in either order, and they are not an A/T or C/G pair. The merge accepts the SNP.
  • A/T or C/G: the same letters, forming an A/T or C/G pair, whose complements are the same pair. The merge accepts the SNP whichever strand each file reports (above).
  • complementary alleles: the study's letters are the complements of the reference's. The merge sees four letters and refuses the SNP; --flip repairs it.
  • other: anything else, such as a third letter, or the −9 that PennCATH writes in place of a missing letter (The data: PLINK files · A1 and A2). The merge refuses the SNP, and a flip cannot repair it. A 0 in place of a letter is the exception: PLINK reads 0 as a missing allele and takes the letter from the other file, so the merge accepts such a SNP if its one letter matches.

At the end, split puts the four names in a fixed order so that every count is printed, 0 included (+ 0 turns an unset count into 0), and the last line counts shared SNPs whose chromosome or position differs between the two files. The four counts add up to the number of shared SNPs. c ? a : b is awk's conditional expression, Python's a if c else b. Before running the merge, predict how many lines its .missnp file will have: the complementary SNPs plus the "other" SNPs, less any with a 0 whose letter matches. The wc -l after the merge checks the prediction.

The first merge fails, on purpose

plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 --bmerge ${reference_dir}/toy_1kg.bed ${reference_dir}/toy_1kg.bim ${reference_dir}/toy_1kg.fam --make-bed --out ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.merge1kgenomes || true
  1. plink — PLINK 1.9.
  2. --bfile PREFIX — the base fileset: the study after the step 1 filters. Its people and SNPs come first in the merged file.
  3. --bmerge BED BIM FAM — the fileset to merge in, given as its three file names (PLINK 1.9 also accepts a single prefix). The merge happens before any other operation in the command.2
  4. --make-bed — write the merged people and SNPs as a new fileset.
  5. --out PREFIX — the output name. A failed merge leaves PREFIX-merge.missnp, PREFIX.log and PREFIX-merge.fam (the merged list of people, which PLINK writes before it compares the SNPs), but no .bed or .bim.

Read the output from the top. PLINK first reports the base fileset (… people loaded from ….fam), then the fileset to merge (… people to be merged from ….fam) and how many of those people are new; no person should be in both files. The same three lines follow for SNPs (markers): how many the base has, how many the reference has, and how many of the reference's SNPs are new rather than already in the base. Then comes the error … variants with 3+ alleles present with PLINK's advice: try --flip with the .missnp file, and beware that "strand errors involving SNPs with A/T or C/G alleles probably remain in your data".2

In the lab, the base fileset has ? people and ? SNPs. toy_1kg adds ? people, all new, and ? SNPs, of which ? are new and ? are already in the base. The error reports ? variants with three or more alleles, the count of complementary SNPs that the first suggested command printed: no shared toy SNP is of the "other" kind. Flip, then merge again

plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01 --flip ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.merge1kgenomes-merge.missnp --make-bed --out ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01_flipSNPs
  1. The study fileset, not the failed merge (which does not exist).
  2. --flip FILE — complement the allele letters of the SNPs listed in FILE. The log reports --flip: N SNPs flipped.

Choose a rule and act Core

The practical's choice. Step 7 removes nobody. Its purpose is to look: the practical's conclusion for PennCATH is that "the PennCATH samples lie predominantly within or very close to the cluster formed by 1KG samples carrying the EUR reference label", to be read "as reference-based genetic similarity, not as a categorical determination that every participant is 'European'".1 The decision about flagging people comes in the exercise, after GrafAnc (step 8), and the practical's advice there is explicit: "Do not exclude a sample solely because its GrafAnc label differs from the majority."1

Rules used in published pipelines. Each needs to be fixed before looking at association results and recorded with the IDs it flags.

  • A region on the PCs around the intended reference group. Anderson et al. merged their study with HapMap reference samples, computed PCs, and excluded "all individuals with a 2nd principal component score less than 0.072" because the study aimed at people of European descent.4 Variants of this rule draw an ellipse, or a fixed number of standard deviations, around the reference group's center.
  • Iterated outlier removal. EIGENSOFT's smartpca removes people more than 6 standard deviations from the mean along any of the top 10 PCs, recomputes the PCs, and repeats up to five times.5 This uses the study's own spread, not a reference.
  • A classifier trained on reference labels. gnomAD trains a random forest on the PCs of reference samples with known labels and assigns each person to a "genetic ancestry group" only when the classifier is confident; everyone else is left unassigned.6
  • Keep everyone and adjust. Analyze all people together and include PCs as covariates, or use a mixed model (Session 2), or analyze reference-relative groups separately and combine the results. This keeps the study's sample size and its diversity.7,8

Remove versus flag. A flag is information attached to an ID: it can change how the person is analyzed (for example in a sensitivity analysis) without discarding their data. Removal is for samples that fail a quality check (a swapped or contaminated sample). Restricting an analysis to one group is a design choice, which the practical accepts "when that restriction is scientifically justified and prespecified".1 Whichever you choose, the merged file serves only this step: the association analysis in Session 2 uses the study's own fileset, never a file that contains the 2,504 reference people.

Projection instead of a joint PCA Optional

The practical computes PCs from the study and the reference together. A common alternative computes the PCs from the reference people alone and then places each study person on those axes. The axes then do not depend on the study's size or composition. PLINK 1.9 does this with --pca plus --within (a file assigning people to clusters) and --pca-cluster-names: the PCs come from the named clusters and everyone else is projected.2 Anderson et al. used the equivalent option of smartpca.4 The price is a known bias: projected scores are pulled toward the center of the plot ("shrinkage bias, where projected PC scores regress toward the mean"), so a projected person looks less distinct than a reference person of the same ancestry would.9 Why this happens, and how much: Population structure · Projection and shrinkage.

What to look at.

  • The first merge's log (….merge1kgenomes.log): how many reference SNPs are already in PennCATH (the shared SNPs; the four counts of the second pass add up to that number), and whether the number of lines in .missnp (wc -l) matches your prediction.
  • The flip's log: --flip: N SNPs flipped. N should equal the number of lines of .missnp, and the second merge should end with Merged fileset written to. If it does not, open the log: || true is only on the first merge, but a failure further down a script is easy to miss.
  • SNPs whose allele is written as −9. PLINK 1.9 reads −9 as the name of an allele, so a shared SNP with −9 in one file and two letters in the other gives the merge three allele names, and a flip cannot repair it; the fix is --exclude (Flip, then merge again). With the practical's files this does not happen: the only 2 shared SNPs with −9 fail step 1, and the second pass reports no "other" SNPs. It can happen when PennCATH is merged with other files: awk '$5 == "-9" || $6 == "-9"' ${analysis_dir}/penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.bim | wc -l counts the SNPs with −9 left after step 1.
  • The PCA log: the SNPs removed by --geno and by --maf, and the number left. That number, not 17,490, is what the PCs are built from. With real PLINK 1.9.0, --geno 0.1 removes 564,721 SNPs: the 564,041 one-file SNPs and 680 shared SNPs that the reference file leaves uncalled for about a fifth of its people or more. --maf 0.05 removes 9 more, leaving 10,897 SNPs and 3,884 people.
  • The A/T and C/G SNPs, which the merge cannot check: the second pass counts how many enter the merged file unchecked. The frequency comparison in What --flip changes can check each one against the reference's EUR samples.
  • The plots of PC1 against PC2 and PC2 against PC3. The practical's reading is quoted in Choose a rule and act; check it yourself, and look for study people outside the cluster they mostly overlap.

How the real data differ from the toy data. PennCATH has 1,401 people and 861,473 SNPs before QC (from its PLINK log) and 1,380 people after step 1 (from the practical); the reference has 2,504 people and 17,490 SNPs. The published PennCATH GWAS included 1,401 patients described as "white patients", recruited before coronary angiography at the University of Pennsylvania Medical Center,10 and the practical finds the PennCATH samples "predominantly within or very close to" the EUR reference cluster.1

Its genotypes and allele frequencies are simulated, so its PC values cannot be compared with those of the real files.

Pitfalls Common mistakes in step 7
  • || true hides every failure. It makes the first merge's expected error harmless, but it would hide a missing file or a typo too. Read the log, and check that the second merge wrote its .bed file.
  • A/T and C/G SNPs merge silently. --flip never touches them, because they never reach .missnp. Exclude them before merging or check their frequencies.
  • Different SNP names or genome builds. The merge matches IDs only. A SNP named differently in the two files becomes two one-file SNPs; the same name at two positions produces Multiple positions seen for variant warnings. Convert both files to the same build and naming first (liftover).
  • Forgetting --geno 0.1, or a small study. PCs computed with one-file SNPs separate the two files by missingness. With a study smaller than about a ninth of the reference, --geno 0.1 no longer removes the reference-only SNPs; extract the shared SNPs explicitly.
  • Using the study's .prune.in on the merged data. It was built from the study's full SNP set and may keep almost none of the shared SNPs.
  • Running the association on the merged file. It contains 2,504 reference people with no phenotype. Downstream analyses use the study's own files.
  • Reading reference labels as identities. A study person near the EUR cluster is genetically similar to the 1000 Genomes EUR samples. That is neither a self-identified ethnicity nor a race, and a person between clusters is not "misclassified".
Key points
  • Step 7 merges the study with 2,504 reference people from 26 populations so that the PCs can be read against documented samples.
  • --bmerge refuses SNPs whose two rows together show three or more allele letters and lists them in -merge.missnp; --flip on that list repairs SNPs reported on the other strand.
  • A/T and C/G SNPs pass the letter test on either strand. Exclude them, or resolve them with allele frequencies when the minor allele frequency is well below 0.5.
  • After the merge, one-file SNPs are missing for everyone in the other file; --geno 0.1 removes them when both files hold more than a tenth of the people.
  • The practical removes nobody here. Positions describe genetic similarity to the reference samples; any flagging rule is fixed in advance and recorded.

The second merge succeeds. What does that tell you about the SNPs that were in .missnp, and what does it not tell you?

Every listed SNP became a two-letter SNP after complementing the study's letters, so each was a strand difference, not a different variant. It says nothing about A/T and C/G SNPs: they were never listed, so the success of the second merge does not show that they are on the same strand in both files.

A SNP is G/C in the study and C/G in the reference. Can it appear in .missnp? How would you decide whether the study reports the other strand?

No. The two rows show only the letters C and G, on either strand, so the merge accepts it. Compare frequencies: if the study's frequency of G is 0.80 and the reference's (EUR) frequency of G is 0.18, the same-strand reading is 0.62 away and the opposite-strand reading 0.02 away, so the study reports the other strand and the SNP must be flipped. If both frequencies were near 0.5 (minor allele frequency above about 0.42), exclude the SNP.

In the merged toy fileset, what fraction of genotypes is missing for a SNP typed only in the toy study? Compute it with the shell, then check one such SNP.

Count people: wc -l on the merged .fam gives \(n_{\mathrm{S}} + n_{\mathrm{R}}\), and wc -l on toy_1kg.fam gives \(n_{\mathrm{R}}\). The missing fraction is \(n_{\mathrm{R}}/(n_{\mathrm{S}} + n_{\mathrm{R}})\). To check, run plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.merge1kgenomes --missing --out ${analysis_dir}/merged and look up a study-only SNP in merged.lmiss: its F_MISS is that fraction or slightly more. Why does the practical not apply PennCATH's .prune.in list to the merged data?

The list was made by pruning PennCATH's full SNP set in step 3, so it keeps one SNP from each correlated group among PennCATH's SNPs. Most of the 17,490 reference SNPs need not be on that list even when PennCATH typed them, so extracting it would discard many shared SNPs for no reason. The reference marker set was already pruned when it was made.

A study person lies halfway between the AFR and EUR clusters on PC1. What can you conclude, and what not?

The person's genotypes are about equally similar to the 1000 Genomes AFR and EUR samples on this axis, which is the pattern of a person whose recent ancestors include people from both groups; the 1000 Genomes ASW samples sit in a similar place. You cannot conclude how the person identifies, what their race or ethnicity is, or that the sample is faulty. Other PCs and GrafAnc (step 8) add detail, and the analysis keeps the person unless a prespecified rule says otherwise.

What changes if you run the merged PCA without --geno 0.1? Try it in the terminal.

Run plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/toy.geno0.01.mind0.01.merge1kgenomes --maf 0.05 --pca --out ${analysis_dir}/nogeno and compare the log's SNP count with the run that used --geno 0.1: it is much larger, because the one-file SNPs are kept. Then choose "Without --geno 0.1" in the file menu of the widget above. In the lab, PC1 and PC2 look much as before, because the continental differences among the reference people still dominate them. The change is on the later PCs: try PC3 against PC4. On one axis the reference people bunch together near 0 while the study's people split into three groups, by their number of inverted copies of the chromosome 8 region of step 6: almost all of the region's SNPs are study-only, and this PCA uses no pruned list. On the other, the study's African-like and admixed people stand apart from every reference person, including the AFR people they overlapped in the practical's PCA. PLINK computes each pair's similarity from the SNPs that both people have, so two study people are compared on thousands of study-only SNPs, and a study person and a reference person on the shared SNPs alone. Part of what those PCs measure is therefore which file a person came from. How high up the list of PCs this reaches depends on the number of one-file SNPs and on the sizes of the two files.

The R code joins by c("V1" = "sample"). Why does that work for the reference people, and why do the study's people end up black?

V1 is the family ID column of the .eigenvec file. In the reference fileset the family ID equals the sample name (HG00096), which is the panel's sample column, so every reference person finds their row. The study's family IDs are not in the panel, so left_join keeps those rows with super_pop missing (NA), and the second geom_point layer draws exactly those rows in black.
