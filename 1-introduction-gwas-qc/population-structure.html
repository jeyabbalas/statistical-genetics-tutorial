Population structure: allele frequencies across populations, STRUCTURE and PCA

The same SNP can have an allele frequency of 0.9 in one human population and 0.2 in another. This page shows how large such differences are, where they come from (drift, founder events, migration, selection, mutation) and how the fixation index \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\), the share of genetic variance that lies between populations, measures them. It then takes apart the two methods the lecture uses to place people by their genotypes: a model of ancestral populations (STRUCTURE and ADMIXTURE) and principal component analysis (PCA). Both summarize the people they are given, and that decides how the practical can use them for quality control and as covariates.

Mental model

Populations have allele frequencies, genotypes are draws from them, and Q and PCs summarize the rows

Each population has its own allele frequency at every SNP; each person's genotypes are draws from a mix of those frequencies; model-based methods estimate the mix (the matrix Q) and PCA finds the main axes along which the rows of the genotype matrix differ. Both describe the sample they were given.

  • Frequencies differ. Most SNPs differ a little between populations, smoothly with geography; \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) is about 0.004 between two European samples and 0.1–0.14 between continents.
  • Mechanisms. Drift moves a frequency by about \(\sqrt{p(1 - p)/2N}\) per generation in a population of \(N\) people; founder events, migration, selection and mutation add or remove differences.
  • Model-based. \(g_{ij} \sim \Binomial(2, \sum_k q_{ik} f_{kj})\): STRUCTURE and ADMIXTURE estimate each person's proportions \(q_{ik}\) and each component's allele frequencies \(f_{kj}\).
  • PCA. Standardize the genotypes, form the \(n \times n\) relationship matrix, take its top eigenvectors: each person gets coordinates, the principal components.
  • Limits. Group sizes, missing groups, regions of strong LD and relatives move the components and the axes.
  • In practice. PLINK --pca writes .eigenvec and .eigenval files; the practical adds 1000 Genomes reference samples and GrafAnc's reference-based assignments.

How allele frequencies differ between populations Core

In this lecture a population is a group of people sampled under one description, usually a place: "Yoruba in Ibadan, Nigeria" (YRI) is one of the reference samples of the HapMap Project. Such a label records how the sample was collected (place, community, sometimes the birthplaces of grandparents); it is not a biological category (Lecture 0 · Reference samples). A population's allele frequency at a SNP is the fraction of its allele copies that carry a given allele, estimated from \(n\) people as \(\hat{p} = (2n_{11} + n_{12})/(2n)\) (Genotype and allele frequencies). The spectrum of one sample: why so many singletons

A singleton is a variant whose allele is seen once in a sample: one copy in one person. Population genetics predicts how many singletons a sample should have. Take the simplest model: a population of constant size, random mating, no selection, and new mutations arising at a constant rate. Every variant began as one mutant copy of the ancestral allele; the new allele is the derived allele. The expected number of variant sites at which the derived allele is carried by exactly \(i\) of the \(2n\) sampled copies is2

\[ \E[\xi_i] = \frac{\theta}{i}, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, 2n - 1 \]
\(\xi_i\)
Number of variant sites in the sample at which the derived allele appears in exactly \(i\) of the \(2n\) allele copies of \(n\) people.
\(i\)
The count, from 1 (a singleton) to \(2n - 1\) (all copies but one).
\(\theta\)
The population mutation rate of the region studied: \(4N\mu\) summed over its sites, where \(N\) is the population size and \(\mu\) the mutation rate per site per generation. It sets the scale and cancels out of every share below.

A derived allele starts as one copy and its count then changes at random from generation to generation; most new alleles stay rare or are lost, and few drift to high counts. The expected number of sites therefore falls as \(1/i\): half as many sites at count 2 as at count 1, a tenth as many at count 10. Summing over \(i\) gives the total number of variant sites, \(\theta \sum_{i=1}^{2n-1} 1/i\). For the \(2n = 1{,}394\) copies of slide 88 the sum of \(1/i\) is about 7.8, so under this model singletons would make up \(1/7.8 \approx 13\%\) of the variant sites. Large human samples have more singletons than this, because the human population grew rapidly over the past few hundred generations and every generation added new mutations that have not had time to rise in frequency.3 Slide 88 counts the minor allele rather than the derived allele, which folds counts \(i\) and \(2n - i\) together; at a rare variant the minor allele is almost always the derived one.

Each population exchanges migrants mainly with its neighbors, so neighbors keep similar frequencies while distant populations, connected only through many steps, drift apart. Measuring the differences: FST

Lecture 0 defines \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) by comparing pairs of allele copies drawn within and between populations (Lecture 0 · \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\)). Wright's original definition6 uses a variance instead, and it connects directly to the drift model of the next section. Consider many populations that descend from one ancestral population and have since changed independently:

\[ F_{\mathrm{ST}} = \frac{\Var(p_k)}{p_0(1 - p_0)} \]
\(p_k\)
Frequency of an allele in population \(k\), one of many populations descended from the same ancestral population.
\(\Var(p_k)\)
Variance of \(p_k\) across those populations.
\(p_0\)
Frequency of the allele in the ancestral population, which is also the mean of \(p_k\) when the populations changed only by drift.
\(p_0(1 - p_0)\)
The largest variance possible with mean \(p_0\), reached when every population has lost one allele or the other (each \(p_k\) is 0 or 1).

\(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) is the variance of the frequencies as a fraction of its maximum. Populations that all keep the ancestral frequency give 0; populations that have each fixed one allele give 1. Dividing by \(p_0(1 - p_0)\) puts SNPs of different frequencies on one scale: a SNP at \(p_0 = 0.5\) can vary much more than one at \(p_0 = 0.02\). Under drift, every SNP gets the same expected \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) (next section), which is why a single number can describe a pair of populations and why it is estimated by pooling many SNPs.

In data there are only \(K\) observed populations and their frequencies, not the ancestral frequency. Weir and Cockerham's estimator,7 in the form it takes when every population's frequency is known exactly (very many people per population), pools SNPs \(j = 1, \ldots, m\):

\[ \hat{F}_{\mathrm{ST}} = \frac{\sum_j s_j^2}{\sum_j \bigl[\bar{p}_j(1 - \bar{p}_j) + s_j^2/K\bigr]} \]
\(K\)
Number of populations compared.
\(\bar{p}_j\)
Mean of the \(K\) population frequencies at SNP \(j\).
\(s_j^2\)
Their sample variance, \(\sum_k (p_{kj} - \bar{p}_j)^2/(K - 1)\).
\(s_j^2/K\)
A correction: \(\bar{p}_j(1 - \bar{p}_j)\) is smaller than \(p_0(1 - p_0)\) on average, because \(\bar{p}_j\) itself varies around \(p_0\) with variance \(\Var(p_k)/K\).
\(\sum_j\)
Sums over the \(m\) SNPs, taken separately in the numerator and the denominator.

With only a few populations, the mean \(\bar{p}_j\) stands in for the unknown \(p_0\); dividing by \(K - 1\) and adding \(s_j^2/K\) remove the bias this causes. For two populations the estimator is exactly Lecture 0's pairwise formula: with frequencies 0.2 and 0.4, \(\bar{p} = 0.3\), \(s^2 = 0.02\) and \(\hat{F}_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.02/(0.21 + 0.01) = 0.091\), the same as \((0.2 - 0.4)^2/0.44\). Some texts use the plain ratio \(\sum_k (p_k - \bar{p})^2 / \bigl(K \bar{p}(1 - \bar{p})\bigr)\), Nei's \(G_{\mathrm{ST}}\); for two populations it gives about half (0.048 here). Summing over SNPs before dividing (a ratio of averages) weights each SNP by its \(\bar{p}_j(1 - \bar{p}_j)\); averaging per-SNP ratios instead gives rare SNPs, whose ratios are small and noisy, too much weight.

With real samples, each frequency is itself an estimate from a limited number of people, so the squared difference between two sample frequencies contains sampling noise of about \(p(1 - p)/(2n)\) per population even when the populations are identical. The full Weir–Cockerham estimator and Hudson's estimator subtract it.7,8 Bhatia and colleagues showed how much these choices matter:9 the 1000 Genomes pilot reported \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) of 0.052 between European and East Asian samples and 0.071 between European and West African samples; recomputed as a ratio of averages with Hudson's estimator, the values are 0.106 and 0.139. Between the HapMap 3 samples CEU and TSI (Tuscans in Italy) the value is about 0.004.10 PLINK 2 computes Hudson's estimator by default (--fst with a column of population labels) and reports a ratio of averages.11 When you read an \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) value, check which estimator and which SNPs it used.

Most variation is within populations, yet many SNPs separate them

Lewontin (1972) asked what share of human genetic diversity lies between groups.12 With heterozygosity as the measure of diversity, the answer is a one-line calculation: if \(H_{\mathrm{T}} = 2\bar{p}(1 - \bar{p})\) is the heterozygosity of the pooled population and \(H_{\mathrm{S}}\) the average heterozygosity within populations, the share within populations is \(H_{\mathrm{S}}/H_{\mathrm{T}}\) and the share between them is \((H_{\mathrm{T}} - H_{\mathrm{S}})/H_{\mathrm{T}}\), which is Nei's \(G_{\mathrm{ST}}\). Lewontin used 17 blood-group and protein loci and Shannon's information measure instead of heterozygosity, and found 85.4% of the diversity within populations, 8.3% between populations of the same continental "race" of his grouping, and 6.3% between those groups. A DNA-based study of 1,056 people from 52 populations found 93–95% within populations.13

A small share between populations at each SNP does not mean that people cannot be told apart. Take two populations with frequencies 0.45 and 0.55 at a SNP, so \(F_{\mathrm{ST}} \approx 0.02\). For a person from the first population, the log of the likelihood ratio "population 1 versus population 2" of their genotype averages \(2 \times 0.1 \times \log(0.55/0.45) = 0.040\) per SNP, with a standard deviation of 0.28. Over 1,000 independent such SNPs the log ratios add up to an average of 40 with a standard deviation of 9, so the person is almost never assigned to the wrong population; with 10 SNPs the average is 0.4 with a standard deviation of 0.9, and one person in three is misassigned. Edwards (2003) made this point against the conclusion that such groups cannot be told apart genetically,14 and Witherspoon and colleagues measured it: with a few loci, two people from different populations are often more similar than two people from the same population, while with thousands of SNPs this almost never happens for populations sampled on different continents.15 STRUCTURE, ADMIXTURE and PCA work because they add up many small differences that point the same way.

Why frequencies differ: drift, founders, migration, selection, mutation Core

Lecture 0 describes each force with examples (Lecture 0 · Why allele frequencies differ). This section gives the models behind the first two, which produce most of the genome-wide differences that \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\), STRUCTURE and PCA detect.

Drift in the Wright–Fisher model

The Wright–Fisher model is the simplest model of a population over time: generations do not overlap, every generation has \(N\) people and therefore \(2N\) allele copies, and each copy of the next generation is drawn independently, at random, from the copies of the current generation. There is no selection, mutation or migration. The number of copies of an allele in the next generation is then a binomial draw:

\[ \begin{gathered} 2N p_{t+1} \sim \Binomial(2N, p_t) \\[4pt] \E[p_{t+1}] = p_t \\[4pt] \Var(p_{t+1}) = \frac{p_t(1 - p_t)}{2N} \end{gathered} \]
\(t\)
Generation, \(t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\)
\(p_t\)
Frequency of the allele among the \(2N\) copies of generation \(t\).
\(N\)
Number of people per generation.
\(2N p_{t+1}\)
Number of copies of the allele in generation \(t + 1\), from 0 to \(2N\).
\(\E[p_{t+1}]\), \(\Var(p_{t+1})\)
Mean and variance of the next generation's frequency, given \(p_t\).

Each new copy carries the allele with probability \(p_t\), so the count is binomial with \(2N\) trials. The frequency has no tendency to rise or fall (its mean is \(p_t\)), but it moves by a random step with standard deviation \(\sqrt{p_t(1 - p_t)/2N}\): 0.035 per generation for \(N = 100\) and \(p_t = 0.5\), 0.0035 for \(N = 10{,}000\). The steps accumulate. Once the frequency reaches 0 (the allele is lost) or 1 (it is fixed), it stays there; because the mean is preserved, an allele at frequency \(p\) is eventually fixed with probability \(p\).

Now let several populations split from one ancestral population at generation 0, each then drifting on its own. Their frequencies spread out, and the spread is \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \Var(p_t) = p_0(1 - p_0)\, F_t \\[6pt] F_t = 1 - \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{2N}\Bigr)^{t} \end{gathered} \]
\(p_0\)
Frequency at the split.
\(p_t\)
Frequency in one population \(t\) generations after the split.
\(\Var(p_t)\)
Variance of \(p_t\) across populations that drifted independently.
\(F_t\)
\(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) among these populations after \(t\) generations, from 0 to 1. It is also the probability that two allele copies drawn from one population descend from a single copy present since the split.

Two copies drawn from a population are copies of the same parental copy with probability \(1/(2N)\); otherwise they come from two parental copies, which share a single ancestral copy with probability \(F_t\). So \(1 - F_{t+1} = (1 - 1/(2N))(1 - F_t)\), and \(1 - F_t\) shrinks by the factor \(1 - 1/(2N)\) every generation. While \(t\) is small compared with \(2N\), \(F_t \approx t/(2N)\). Populations of 100 people separated for 100 generations reach \(F_t = 1 - 0.995^{100} = 0.39\); populations of 10,000 people separated for 2,000 generations reach \(1 - e^{-0.1} \approx 0.095\), the order of the values between continents. The expected heterozygosity within each population falls by the same factor, \(H_t = H_0(1 - F_t)\).

Real populations do not follow the Wright–Fisher model: generations overlap, numbers change, and people do not have equal numbers of children. In the formulas, \(N\) is therefore the effective population size, the size of a Wright–Fisher population that would drift at the same rate; it is usually far smaller than the number of people alive.17

Founder events and bottlenecks

A population founded by \(N_{\mathrm{F}}\) people starts with frequencies sampled from \(2N_{\mathrm{F}}\) copies. That one sampling step adds \(1/(2N_{\mathrm{F}})\) to \(F\) at once: 0.1 for 5 founders, as much as 21 generations of drift in a population of 100. A bottleneck, \(t_{\mathrm{b}}\) generations at a small size \(N_{\mathrm{b}}\), adds about \(t_{\mathrm{b}}/(2N_{\mathrm{b}})\). Each founder event also multiplies heterozygosity by \(1 - 1/(2N_{\mathrm{F}})\) and loses rare alleles that no founder carried. A chain of founder events, as in the spread of humans out of Africa, makes heterozygosity fall step by step with the number of events, which is why it falls with distance from Africa (Lecture 0 · Serial founder effects).

Migration: the island model

Wright's island model describes migration between many populations ("islands") of \(N\) people each. Every generation, a fraction \(m\) of each population's parents are migrants drawn from a common pool, whose frequency is the average over islands and stays at \(p_0\). Migration pulls each island back toward \(p_0\) while drift pushes it away:6,17

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{t+1} &= \tfrac{1}{2N} \\ &\quad + \bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{2N}\bigr)(1 - m)^2 F_t \end{aligned} \]
\[ F^{*} \approx \frac{1}{1 + 4Nm} \]
\(m\)
Fraction of each population's parents who are migrants from the common pool, per generation, from 0 to 1.
\(F_t\)
\(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) among the islands at generation \(t\).
\((1 - m)^2\)
Probability that neither of two sampled copies came from a migrant.
\(F^{*}\)
Equilibrium value of \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\), where drift and migration balance.
\(Nm\)
Number of migrants per island per generation.

Two copies sampled in generation \(t + 1\) are copies of one parental copy with probability \(1/(2N)\). Otherwise they share an ancestral copy with probability \(F_t\), but only if neither of them came from a migrant, which happens with probability \((1 - m)^2\). Setting \(F_{t+1} = F_t\) gives \(F^{*} = (1/2N)/\bigl(1 - (1 - m)^2(1 - 1/2N)\bigr)\), close to \(1/(1 + 4Nm)\) when \(m\) and \(1/(2N)\) are small. Only the number of migrants \(Nm\) matters: one migrant per generation holds \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) near 0.2 whatever the population size (0.2016 exactly for \(N = 100\), \(m = 0.01\)); ten migrants hold it near 0.024. When migrants come mostly from neighbors rather than a common pool, the same balance produces the clines of slides 89–91.

Selection, non-random mating and mutation

Selection changes the frequency of one SNP, and of the SNPs in LD with it, in the environment where the allele matters (examples: Lecture 0 · Natural selection). Drift acts on the whole genome alike, so a SNP under selection shows up as an outlier: its \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) is far above the genome-wide value, an idea due to Lewontin and Krakauer.18 The lactase-persistence SNP rs4988235 has \(F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.51\) between the 1000 Genomes European and East Asian samples, against a genome-wide 0.106 (Lecture 0). Non-random mating (slide 97, D), choosing partners within a community or among relatives, changes genotype frequencies (more homozygotes; Lecture 0 · Wahlund effect) and keeps groups apart long enough for drift to differentiate them. Mutation creates a new allele in one person of one population. Recent mutations are therefore rare and found in few populations: in the 1000 Genomes data, rare variants are typically shared only among closely related populations, while most common variants are found on several continents.16

Model-based clustering: STRUCTURE and ADMIXTURE Core

Slides Slide 98 · Map coordinates from genotypes

"Map coordinates" here are a few numbers per person, computed from their genotypes, that place them relative to other people. A model-based method (STRUCTURE, GRAF-pop) computes them under an explicit model of populations and allele frequencies; the model-free method, PCA, finds the axes in the data without such a model. The practical uses the coordinates in quality control (step 6, step 7, step 8), and association analyses use them as covariates to adjust for confounding by ancestry (Testing association).

The model and its likelihood

STRUCTURE19 and ADMIXTURE20 describe a sample with \(K\) ancestral components. Component \(k\) has its own frequency \(f_{kj}\) of allele 1 at every SNP \(j\), and each person \(i\) has admixture proportions \(q_{i1}, \ldots, q_{iK}\), non-negative and summing to 1. Each of the person's two allele copies at each SNP comes from component \(k\) with probability \(q_{ik}\), independently of the other copy and of other SNPs, and then carries allele 1 with probability \(f_{kj}\). Lecture 0 states this model (Lecture 0 · Model-based estimates); this section fits it. The quantity to maximize is the log-likelihood, the log of the probability of all observed genotypes given the parameters:

\[ \begin{gathered} s_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} q_{ik} f_{kj} \\[6pt] \log L = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \bigl[\, g_{ij} \log s_{ij} \\ + (2 - g_{ij}) \log(1 - s_{ij}) \,\bigr] \end{gathered} \]
\(g_{ij}\)
Genotype of person \(i\) (\(i = 1, \ldots, n\)) at SNP \(j\) (\(j = 1, \ldots, m\)): copies of allele 1, 0, 1 or 2.
\(K\)
Number of components, chosen by the analyst. (\(K\) is a count here; the bold \(\mathbf{K}\) of the PCA section is a matrix.)
\(q_{ik}\)
Admixture proportion of person \(i\) for component \(k\), from 0 to 1; row \(i\) of the \(n \times K\) matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\).
\(f_{kj}\)
Frequency of allele 1 at SNP \(j\) in component \(k\); the \(K \times m\) matrix \(\mathbf{F}\) (ADMIXTURE's P file).
\(s_{ij}\)
Probability that one allele copy of person \(i\) at SNP \(j\) carries allele 1.
\(\log L\)
Log-likelihood, up to a constant (the log of the binomial coefficients, which does not depend on \(\mathbf{Q}\) or \(\mathbf{F}\)).

Given its parameters, a genotype is \(\Binomial(2, s_{ij})\), so its log-probability is \(g \log s + (2 - g)\log(1 - s)\) plus a constant. Within each component, genotypes are assumed to be in Hardy–Weinberg proportions, and adding over SNPs assumes that SNPs are independent, which is why the input is LD-pruned. The fit has \(n(K - 1) + Km\) free numbers: for the 2,504 people of 1000 Genomes, 100,000 SNPs and \(K = 5\), about 510,000 numbers estimated from 250 million genotypes. Each person's proportions rest on their \(m\) genotypes, and each frequency on up to \(2n\) allele copies. The components have no names: their numbering is arbitrary (two runs may number the same components differently), and only the data decide what a component represents.

Fitting the model with EM

FRAPPE, a predecessor of ADMIXTURE, maximizes \(\log L\) with the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm,21 the same algorithm that estimates haplotype frequencies from unphased genotypes on the Linkage disequilibrium page. The unobserved information is the component each allele copy came from. The E-step computes, for every person and SNP, how many of the person's copies are expected to come from each component:

\[ \begin{gathered} a_{ijk} = g_{ij}\, \frac{q_{ik} f_{kj}}{s_{ij}} \\[6pt] b_{ijk} = (2 - g_{ij})\, \frac{q_{ik}(1 - f_{kj})}{1 - s_{ij}} \end{gathered} \]
\(a_{ijk}\)
Expected number of the person's allele-1 copies at SNP \(j\) that come from component \(k\), between 0 and \(g_{ij}\).
\(b_{ijk}\)
The same for the allele-2 copies, between 0 and \(2 - g_{ij}\).
\(q_{ik} f_{kj}/s_{ij}\)
Probability that a copy carrying allele 1 came from component \(k\).

This is Bayes' rule. Before looking at the allele, a copy comes from component \(k\) with probability \(q_{ik}\); seeing allele 1 multiplies that by \(f_{kj}\), and dividing by the sum over components, \(s_{ij}\), makes the probabilities add to 1. Example: \(q_{i1} = 0.7\), \(q_{i2} = 0.3\), \(f_{1j} = 0.9\), \(f_{2j} = 0.2\) and \(g_{ij} = 1\). Then \(s_{ij} = 0.63 + 0.06 = 0.69\); the allele-1 copy came from component 1 with probability \(0.63/0.69 = 0.913\) and the allele-2 copy with probability \(0.07/0.31 = 0.226\). This SNP assigns 1.139 of the person's 2 copies (57%) to component 1, less than the current 70%, because a heterozygote fits component 2 (allele frequency 0.2) relatively well.

The M-step turns these expected counts into new parameters:

\[ \begin{gathered} q_{ik} \leftarrow \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{m} \bigl(a_{ijk} + b_{ijk}\bigr) \\[6pt] f_{kj} \leftarrow \frac{\sum_{i} a_{ijk}}{\sum_{i} \bigl(a_{ijk} + b_{ijk}\bigr)} \end{gathered} \]
\(\leftarrow\)
"Is replaced by": the new value for the next cycle.
\(2m\)
Number of allele copies a person has over \(m\) SNPs (2 per SNP with a non-missing genotype).
\(\sum_i a_{ijk}\)
Expected number of allele-1 copies at SNP \(j\) assigned to component \(k\), over all people.
\(\sum_i (a_{ijk} + b_{ijk})\)
All copies at SNP \(j\) assigned to component \(k\).

The new \(q_{ik}\) is the share of the person's \(2m\) copies assigned to component \(k\); the new \(f_{kj}\) is the share of allele 1 among the copies assigned to component \(k\). Each E-step plus M-step cannot lower \(\log L\), but the likelihood has several local maxima (parameter values better than all nearby values but worse than the best fit), so different random starting values can end at different fits. Analyses therefore run several starts and keep the one with the highest \(\log L\); the widget below does this with three starts. ADMIXTURE itself uses a faster optimization method (block relaxation with quasi-Newton acceleration) that converges in far fewer cycles,20 and the widget speeds up EM with SQUAREM, an extrapolation step taken between EM cycles.22 STRUCTURE treats the same model in a Bayesian way: it puts prior distributions (assumed spreads of plausible values before seeing the data) on \(\mathbf{Q}\) and \(\mathbf{F}\) and uses Markov chain Monte Carlo, a simulation that produces a long sequence of parameter values in proportion to how well they fit, which is slower but gives a measure of uncertainty.19

Choosing K

The fit improves whenever \(K\) grows, so \(\log L\) alone cannot choose \(K\). ADMIXTURE offers cross-validation: it splits the observed genotypes into 5 parts (folds), hides one part, fits the model to the rest, predicts each hidden genotype from the fitted \(2 s_{ij}\), and repeats for every part; the average prediction error is the CV error, and a \(K\) with low CV error is preferred.23 For STRUCTURE, Evanno and colleagues proposed \(\Delta K\), based on how sharply the log-likelihood changes from one \(K\) to the next.24 Both are heuristics (slide 99 says "various heuristics for selecting the 'optimal' K"). Human populations are connected by migration and clines, so there is usually no true \(K\): \(K\) sets the resolution of the description.

Supervised fits, GRAF-pop and GrafAnc

Slide 99 distinguishes two ways of running the model. In an unsupervised run, the components are estimated from the data. In a supervised run, reference people whose population is given are fixed to their own component (ADMIXTURE's --supervised option reads the labels from a .pop file), so the components are defined by the reference populations and only the other people's proportions are free. GRAF-pop,25 which slide 99 says "uses a similar conceptual model", and its successor GrafAnc26 go one step further: the allele frequencies of their reference populations are fixed in advance, and each person is compared with them on their own. A person's result therefore does not depend on who else is in the study, which is not true for unsupervised ADMIXTURE or PCA.

Pitfall Reading a bar plot as a family tree

A proportion of 0.3 for component 2 does not mean that 30% of a person's ancestors lived in one place. It means that, among the \(K\) sets of allele frequencies that fit this sample best, set 2 explains 30% of the person's allele copies. Change \(K\), add or remove reference people, or prune the SNPs differently, and the number changes. Principal component analysis, from a data matrix to genotypes Core

Slides Slide 102 · PCA rotates the axes

On the left, each circle is one observation with two measured features; point \(x_i\) has coordinates \(x_{i,1}\) and \(x_{i,2}\). On the right, the same points get new axes: direction 1 runs along the longest spread of the cloud and direction 2 is perpendicular to it. The point's new coordinates, \(z_{i,1}\) and \(z_{i,2}\) on the slide, are its scores on the two principal components. "Algebra: orthonormal transform; geometry: axis rotation" means that the new axes are perpendicular and of unit length, so the change of coordinates is a rotation: distances between points stay the same, only the axes turn.

PCA in general

Start with a data matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) of \(n\) rows (observations) and \(d\) columns (features), each column centered to mean 0. The covariance matrix collects the covariance of every pair of features; PCA is its eigendecomposition. An eigenvector of a square matrix is a direction that the matrix only stretches, and the eigenvalue is the stretch factor:

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{n - 1}\, \mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X} \\[6pt] \mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}_k = \ell_k \mathbf{v}_k \\[6pt] t_{ik} = \mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\mathbf{v}_k \end{gathered} \]
\(\mathbf{X}\)
Centered data, \(n \times d\); \(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\) is its transpose.
\(\mathbf{C}\)
Covariance matrix, \(d \times d\): entry \((a, b)\) is the covariance of features \(a\) and \(b\), and the diagonal holds their variances.
\(\mathbf{v}_k\)
The \(k\)-th eigenvector of \(\mathbf{C}\): a vector of \(d\) weights of length 1, the direction of principal component \(k\).
\(\ell_k\)
The \(k\)-th largest eigenvalue, \(\ell_1 \ge \ell_2 \ge \ldots \ge 0\): the variance of the data along \(\mathbf{v}_k\).
\(\mathbf{x}_i\)
Row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{X}\), the \(d\) features of observation \(i\).
\(t_{ik}\)
Score of observation \(i\) on component \(k\) (the slide's \(z_{i,k}\); on this page \(z\) is kept for standardized genotypes).

The variance of the projections \(\mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\mathbf{v}\) onto a unit direction \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}\). It is largest for \(\mathbf{v}_1\), where it equals \(\ell_1\); \(\mathbf{v}_2\) maximizes it among directions perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}_1\), and so on. The directions are perpendicular, so scores on different components are uncorrelated. The eigenvalues add up to the total variance (the sum of the \(d\) feature variances, the trace of \(\mathbf{C}\)), so \(\ell_k / \sum_{k'} \ell_{k'}\) is the share of the variance that component \(k\) captures. Keeping all \(d\) components only rotates the data; keeping the first few gives the best approximation of \(\mathbf{X}\) by that many variables, in squared error.

The same components from the people-by-people matrix

Genotype data have many more columns (SNPs) than PCA software wants to put in a \(d \times d\) matrix. The singular value decomposition (SVD), a factorization that exists for every matrix, shows that the person-by-person matrix gives the same components:

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^{\top} \\[6pt] \tfrac{1}{m}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top} = \mathbf{U}\,\tfrac{\mathbf{S}^2}{m}\,\mathbf{U}^{\top} \end{gathered} \]
\(\mathbf{Z}\)
Centered (here: standardized) genotype matrix, \(n\) people \(\times\) \(m\) SNPs.
\(\mathbf{U}\)
\(n \times r\) matrix whose columns \(\mathbf{u}_k\) have length 1 and are perpendicular: one score per person for each component. \(r\) is the rank of \(\mathbf{Z}\), its number of independent columns, at most \(n - 1\) because the columns are centered.
\(\mathbf{S}\)
Diagonal \(r \times r\) matrix of singular values \(s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \ldots > 0\).
\(\mathbf{V}\)
\(m \times r\) matrix whose columns \(\mathbf{v}_k\) are the SNP weights (loadings), the directions \(\mathbf{v}_k\) of the general PCA.
\(\tfrac{1}{m}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\)
The \(n \times n\) relationship matrix \(\mathbf{K}\); its eigenvalues are \(\ell_k = s_k^2/m\).

Because \(\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\) is the identity matrix, multiplying \(\mathbf{Z}\) by its transpose cancels \(\mathbf{V}\): the eigenvectors of the \(n \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}/m\) are the score vectors \(\mathbf{u}_k\), and those of the \(m \times m\) matrix \(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}\) are the SNP weights \(\mathbf{v}_k\). The two routes give the same components, linked by \(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{v}_k = s_k\mathbf{u}_k\). With 1,380 people and 100,000 SNPs, the person-by-person matrix has 1.9 million entries and the SNP-by-SNP matrix 10 billion. PLINK 1.9 builds the person-by-person matrix and computes its eigenvectors; for very large samples, PLINK 2's approx mode uses a randomized algorithm that works on \(\mathbf{Z}\) directly.11

Applied to genotypes

Before PCA, each SNP's genotypes are centered and scaled. With \(\hat{p}_j\) the frequency of allele 1 at SNP \(j\) in the sample (Lecture 0 · PCA derives the steps in detail):29,30

\[ \begin{gathered} z_{ij} = \frac{g_{ij} - 2\hat{p}_j}{\sqrt{2\hat{p}_j(1 - \hat{p}_j)}} \\[6pt] \mathbf{K} = \tfrac{1}{m}\, \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top} \end{gathered} \]
\(g_{ij}\)
Genotype of person \(i\) at SNP \(j\): copies of allele 1, 0, 1 or 2.
\(\hat{p}_j\)
Frequency of allele 1 at SNP \(j\) in the people analyzed: the column mean of the genotypes divided by 2.
\(z_{ij}\)
Standardized genotype, an entry of the \(n \times m\) matrix \(\mathbf{Z}\).
\(m\)
Number of SNPs used: in the practical, SNPs that passed quality control, have a minor allele frequency of at least 5% and remain after LD pruning.
\(\mathbf{K}\)
Relationship matrix, \(n \times n\); PLINK calls it the variance-standardized relationship matrix.

Subtracting \(2\hat{p}_j\) centers each column, which PCA requires. Dividing by \(\sqrt{2\hat{p}_j(1 - \hat{p}_j)}\), the standard deviation of a \(\Binomial(2, \hat{p}_j)\) genotype, gives every SNP a variance of about 1, so rare SNPs, whose few carriers get large values, count as much as common ones. EIGENSTRAT divides by \(\sqrt{\hat{p}_j(1 - \hat{p}_j)}\) instead,29 which doubles every eigenvalue and leaves the eigenvectors unchanged. An entry of \(\mathbf{K}\) averages the products \(z_{ij}z_{i'j}\) over SNPs: near 1 on the diagonal, near 0 for two unrelated people from one homogeneous population, positive for people who share recent ancestry.

Centering: the largest group sits at the origin

Every component with a nonzero eigenvalue has scores that add up to zero, as in the worked example. The reason is centering: each column of \(\mathbf{Z}\) has mean 0, so each row of \(\mathbf{K}\) adds up to 0, the vector of all ones is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 0, and every other eigenvector is perpendicular to it. Take two groups of people separated along PC1:

\[ n_1 \bar{x}_1 + n_2 \bar{x}_2 = 0 \]
\(n_1\), \(n_2\)
Numbers of people in groups 1 and 2.
\(\bar{x}_1\), \(\bar{x}_2\)
Mean PC1 score of each group.

The scores add up to 0, and the sum is \(n_1\bar{x}_1 + n_2\bar{x}_2\) when each group sits at its mean. So \(\bar{x}_1/\bar{x}_2 = -n_2/n_1\): the larger group sits nearer the origin. With 120 people from one group and 15 from another, the large group's mean is 8 times closer to 0. The origin is the average person of this sample, not a fixed point, and adding or removing people moves every coordinate.

McVean showed what the coordinates mean in terms of the genealogy of the sample.34 For two allele copies, the coalescence time is the number of generations back to their most recent common ancestor copy. The expected entries of \(\mathbf{K}\) are linear in the average coalescence times between pairs of people, relative to the average over the whole sample; the PCs are the main axes of this matrix of times. Three consequences: the relative numbers of people from each population change the positions and the order of the components; a population with few people may not get a component of its own; and a person with admixture proportion \(q\) from population 1 and \(1 - q\) from population 2 is expected at \(q\bar{x}_1 + (1 - q)\bar{x}_2\), on the straight line between the two groups.

Regions of strong LD and relatives make their own components

PCA assumes nothing about where SNPs are, so it finds any large set of SNPs that vary together across people. A region of strong, long-range LD, such as the MHC on chromosome 6 or a large inversion (for example on chromosome 8p23), contributes hundreds of SNPs that carry the same information. If they are not removed, a component can capture that one region: its SNP weights are concentrated in the region, and people are sorted by which haplotypes they carry there rather than by genome-wide ancestry. Price and colleagues listed 24 such long-range LD regions to exclude,35 and Privé and colleagues found that PCs 19–40 of UK Biobank capture LD structure rather than population structure.36 The practical computes PCs from LD-pruned SNPs (step 3) for this reason; plotting each component's SNP weights along the genome shows whether one region dominates.

Relatives create components too. Close relatives share long chromosome segments identical by descent, so a family is a small group with its own allele frequencies, and a large enough family can get a component of its own. The usual remedy is to compute PCs from unrelated people (one of each close pair; step 4) and then project the relatives onto those PCs. Technical differences, such as genotyping batches, can also create components, which is why the practical says that PCs "can also be affected by technical artifacts".37

Projection, and why projected people shrink toward the origin

To place a new person on PCs computed from other people (a reference panel, or the unrelated part of a study), multiply their standardized genotypes by the SNP weights. Each genotype is standardized with the reference people's allele frequency \(\hat{p}_j\):

\[ \hat{x}_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}} \sum_{j=1}^{m} z_j\, v_{jk} \]
\(z_j\)
The new person's standardized genotype at SNP \(j\), computed with the reference \(\hat{p}_j\).
\(v_{jk}\)
Weight of SNP \(j\) on component \(k\): entry \(j\) of the reference PCA's \(\mathbf{v}_k\), which has length 1.
\(m\)
Number of SNPs.
\(\hat{x}_k\)
The new person's coordinate on component \(k\), on the scale of \(\mathbf{u}_k\sqrt{\ell_k}\).

For a reference person the sum reproduces their own coordinate: \(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{v}_k/\sqrt{m} = s_k\mathbf{u}_k/\sqrt{m} = \mathbf{u}_k\sqrt{\ell_k}\). The weights, however, were fitted to the reference people's genotypes, including their random noise, so reference people sit farther from the origin than new people from the same population. Projected coordinates therefore shrink toward the origin, more on later components and when the number of SNPs is large compared with the number of reference people.36 A projected person who looks "between" two clusters may simply be shrunken. Privé and colleagues describe a correction.

Pitfall Naming the clusters

A cluster on a PC plot is a set of people with similar genotypes in this analysis. Calling it a race or an ethnicity confuses social categories with genetic similarity. The National Academies recommend describing such groups by their genetic similarity to named reference samples (for example "people genetically similar to the 1000 Genomes EUR samples") and saying how the groups were made.38 The practical uses the same rule: "genetic ancestry refers to patterns of genetic similarity inferred relative to reference samples or statistical models. It is not interchangeable with self-identified race, ethnicity, nationality, or personal identity."37

How many PCs?

There is no single rule; four kinds of evidence are used together. A scree plot shows the eigenvalues in decreasing order: components that reflect structure stand above a smooth floor formed by noise, as in the bar chart of the widget above. The Tracy–Widom test of Patterson and colleagues (EIGENSOFT's twstats) asks whether the largest remaining eigenvalue is larger than expected without structure;39 in large samples it declares many components significant, including those made by LD regions or relatives. Plots of each component against the next, colored by known labels, and plots of SNP weights along the genome show what each component captures. Finally, the use decides: for quality control, the components that separate the clusters the analyst is checking; for adjustment in an association analysis, enough components to remove the confounding by ancestry (Testing association). PLINK computes 20 components by default in version 1.9 and 10 in version 2; UK Biobank provides 40, of which the later ones capture LD structure.11,30,36

Step 7: joint PCA with 1000 Genomes, or projection

Study-only PCs say who in the study differs from whom, not how the study relates to other populations. Step 7 merges the 1,380 PennCATH people with the 2,504 people of 1000 Genomes, keeps the SNPs typed in both (a 10% missingness filter removes the others), and runs --pca on all 3,884 people together: a joint PCA. The 1000 Genomes people are then colored by their super-population labels (AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR and SAS: African, admixed American, East Asian, European and South Asian) and the PennCATH people drawn in black; the practical finds them "predominantly within or very close to the cluster formed by 1KG samples carrying the EUR reference label".37

In a joint PCA the study's own people help define the axes, so a large study can pull the axes toward its own variation (the centering result above). The alternative is projection: compute the PCs from the reference people only and project the study people onto them with the formula above. The axes then do not depend on the study, at the cost of shrinkage. PLINK 1.9 does this with --within and --pca-cluster-names; PLINK 2 exports SNP weights with --pca allele-wts and projects with --score:11

bash 
# PLINK 2: PCs and SNP weights from the reference, then projection of new people
$ plink2 --pfile ref_data --freq counts --pca allele-wts vcols=chrom,ref,alt --out ref_pcs
$ plink2 --pfile new_data --read-freq ref_pcs.acount \
         --score ref_pcs.eigenvec.allele 2 5 header-read no-mean-imputation variance-standardize \
         --score-col-nums 6-15 --out new_projection

The PLINK documentation notes that the projected values are scaled differently from the reference .eigenvec and must be multiplied or divided by a multiple of \(\sqrt{\ell_k}\) to be put on the same scale. The page QC step 7 compares the two approaches on the practical's data.

Step 8: reference-based assignment with GrafAnc

GrafAnc compares each person's genotypes at 282,424 preselected SNPs with allele frequencies of reference populations. It reports genetic-distance scores (columns GD1–GD3 and others), three ancestry-component estimates (Pe, Pf and Pa, for components referenced to European, African and East Asian populations) and an assigned group (AncGroupID, whose first digit is the continental group).26,37 Its model is fixed in advance, as in a supervised fit, so a person's result does not change when other people are added to the study, unlike PCA and unsupervised ADMIXTURE. Its groups are defined by its reference populations and thresholds: the practical calls them "algorithmic assignments relative to GrafAnc's reference populations". For PennCATH it expects more than 99% of the people to be assigned to the European continental group. The page QC step 8 runs it and compares the result with step 7.

The text describes the workflow of this page in one sentence: "Ancestry is usually considered in GWAS through an iterative process using principal component analysis; the genotypes of all individuals are used to define clusters of individuals with similar genotypes."40 The PCs are used first to find and exclude outliers and then as covariates in the association models. Key points
  • Allele frequencies differ between populations mostly by small amounts that change smoothly with geography. \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\), the variance of the frequencies relative to its maximum, is about 0.004 between two European samples and 0.1–0.14 between continents; compute it as a ratio of averages over SNPs and check which estimator was used.
  • Under drift, \(\Var(p_t) = p_0(1 - p_0)F_t\) with \(F_t = 1 - (1 - 1/2N)^t\). A founder event adds \(1/(2N_{\mathrm{F}})\) at once; migration holds \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) near \(1/(1 + 4Nm)\); selection makes single-SNP outliers; mutation adds rare, local variants.
  • Each SNP separates populations poorly, but thousands of SNPs together separate them well; STRUCTURE, ADMIXTURE and PCA add up these small differences.
  • STRUCTURE and ADMIXTURE fit \(g_{ij} \sim \Binomial(2, \sum_k q_{ik}f_{kj})\) by MCMC or maximum likelihood. EM alternates between assigning each allele copy to components and re-estimating \(\mathbf{Q}\) and \(\mathbf{F}\). \(K\) is a choice of resolution, and a component need not correspond to a real population.
  • PCA of genotypes: standardize, form \(\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}/m\), take its eigenvectors (equivalently, the SVD of \(\mathbf{Z}\)). An eigenvalue divided by the trace, about \(n\), is that component's share of the variance; the scores of each component add up to 0.
  • PCs and admixture proportions describe the sample: group sizes set the origin and the order of components, missing source groups change proportions, LD regions and relatives can create components, and projected people shrink toward the origin.
  • In the practical: PLINK --pca on pruned common SNPs, a joint PCA with 1000 Genomes, and GrafAnc's reference-based groups, all reported as genetic similarity to reference samples, not as race or ethnicity.

Check your understanding

Two populations have allele frequencies 0.1 and 0.3 at a SNP. What is \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) by the Weir–Cockerham (or Hudson) formula, and by Nei's \(G_{\mathrm{ST}}\)?

Mean \(\bar{p} = 0.2\), so \(\bar{p}(1 - \bar{p}) = 0.16\). The sample variance with divisor \(K - 1 = 1\) is \(s^2 = (0.1 - 0.2)^2 + (0.3 - 0.2)^2 = 0.02\), and \(\hat{F}_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.02/(0.16 + 0.02/2) = 0.118\). Hudson's form gives the same: within-population heterozygosity \((0.18 + 0.42)/2 = 0.30\), between-population \(0.1 \times 0.7 + 0.3 \times 0.9 = 0.34\), and \(1 - 0.30/0.34 = 0.118\). Nei's \(G_{\mathrm{ST}}\) uses the variance with divisor \(K = 2\), 0.01, and no correction: \(0.01/0.16 = 0.0625\), about half. Both are in use, so a reported value needs its definition.

Populations of 500 people split 200 generations ago and have not exchanged migrants since. What \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) do you expect? What if they had exchanged one migrant per generation for a very long time?

\(F_t = 1 - (1 - 1/1000)^{200} = 1 - e^{-0.2001} = 0.181\), close to the approximation \(t/(2N) = 0.2\), which is good while \(t\) is small compared with \(2N = 1{,}000\). With one migrant per generation (\(Nm = 1\)), \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) settles near \(1/(1 + 4) = 0.2\) whatever the population size: drift adds differentiation at a rate of about \(1/(2N)\) per generation and migration removes it at a rate of about \(2m\), and the balance depends only on \(Nm\).

In the E-step, a person has \(q_{i1} = q_{i2} = 0.5\) and genotype 2 at a SNP with \(f_{1j} = 0.8\) and \(f_{2j} = 0.2\). How many of their two copies are assigned to component 1? And with genotype 0?

Genotype 2: both copies carry allele 1, \(s_{ij} = 0.5 \times 0.8 + 0.5 \times 0.2 = 0.5\), and each copy came from component 1 with probability \(0.4/0.5 = 0.8\), so 1.6 copies go to component 1. Genotype 0: both copies carry allele 2, and each came from component 1 with probability \(0.5 \times 0.2/(1 - 0.5) = 0.2\), so 0.4 copies. Averaged over thousands of SNPs, these assignments become the person's new \(q_{i1}\) in the M-step.

An ADMIXTURE run at \(K = 2\) on European and East Asian reference samples plus a group of people from South Asia gives the South Asian group about 60% of the "European" component and 40% of the "East Asian" one. Did their ancestors come 60:40 from Europe and East Asia?

No. With two components and no South Asian reference, the model has to describe every person as a mix of the two available sets of allele frequencies, and 60:40 is the best such description of a population with its own history. At \(K = 5\) on slide 100, South Asian samples get a component of their own (orange). This is the widget's lesson (3): when a population is missing from the components, its people are drawn as mixtures of the ones present. The proportions are relative to \(K\) and to the samples included (slide 101).

A study has 1,000 people genetically similar to the 1000 Genomes EUR samples and 30 similar to the AFR samples, and PC1 separates the two groups. Where do the groups lie on PC1, and what happens if 1,000 more people similar to AFR are added?

The scores add up to 0, so \(1{,}000\,\bar{x}_{\mathrm{EUR}} + 30\,\bar{x}_{\mathrm{AFR}} = 0\) and \(\bar{x}_{\mathrm{EUR}} = -0.03\,\bar{x}_{\mathrm{AFR}}\): the large group sits almost at 0 and the small one far out. With 1,030 people in each group the origin moves to the middle and the two groups sit at about equal distances on either side. Nobody's genotypes changed; the coordinates did.

In step 6 of the practical, real PLINK reports 20 eigenvalues for PennCATH's 1,380 people; the first is 6.28 and the 20 add up to 32.6. What share of the genotype variance does PC1 capture?

About \(6.28/1{,}380 = 0.45\%\). The total variance is the trace of \(\mathbf{K}\), close to the number of people because each diagonal entry is close to 1. Dividing by the sum of the 20 reported eigenvalues (\(6.28/32.6 = 19\%\)) ignores the variance in all the other components and greatly overstates the share.

The SNP weights of PC4 are concentrated in a few megabases of chromosome 8, and people form three bands on PC4. What is PC4 measuring, and what should you do?

A region of strong long-range LD, such as the 8p23 inversion: the three bands are people with 0, 1 or 2 copies of one long haplotype (for an inversion, the inverted orientation). PC4 sorts people by their genotype in one region, not by genome-wide ancestry. Exclude the known long-range LD regions, check that the SNPs are LD-pruned, and recompute the PCs; then check the SNP weights again.
