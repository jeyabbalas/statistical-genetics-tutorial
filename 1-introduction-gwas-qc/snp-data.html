From molecules to SNP data: alleles, strands and names

A genome-wide association study never looks at DNA directly. It works on a table of numbers with one row per person and one column per position in the genome where people differ, and each entry holds 0, 1 or 2. This page follows one such position from the DNA molecule into that table, and shows which labels a number needs before it means anything: where the position is, which of its two DNA letters was counted, and which of the two DNA strands the letters were read from.

Slides 55–70 ≈ 60 min Shows up in the data as: .bim and .fam lines, rsIDs, A1/A2 and REF/ALT columns, 0/1/2 genotype counts
Mental model

A genotype number counts one allele, at one named position, on one strand

Every entry of a genotype matrix is a count, 0, 1 or 2, of one allele at one SNP. The count can be read only together with three labels that travel with it: the SNP’s position (an rsID, or a chromosome and base-pair position on a named genome build), the allele that was counted, and the DNA strand on which the allele letters are written.

  • The molecule. DNA is two complementary strands. A SNP is one base pair at which chromosome copies differ, so every SNP has a pair of letters on each strand (slides 56–63).
  • A SNP and its names. A SNP is named by an rsID, or by chromosome and position on a genome build. The same SNP has different positions on GRCh37 and GRCh38; PennCATH uses GRCh37.
  • From DNA to a matrix. An array measures two light signals per SNP and person, a calling algorithm turns them into genotypes, and PLINK stores the people × SNPs matrix in three files.
  • A number counts one allele. 0, 1 and 2 count copies of one chosen allele (A1 in PLINK 1.9, ALT in a VCF). Counting the other allele turns \(g\) into \(2 - g\), an allele frequency \(p\) into \(1 - p\) and an effect \(\beta\) into \(-\beta\).
  • Letters depend on the strand. The same SNP is C/T on one strand and G/A on the other. A/T and C/G SNPs read the same on both strands, so only allele frequencies can line them up across files.

Slide 55 (“Fragen?”, German for “Questions?”) closes the introduction to genetic epidemiology. Slide 56 opens the molecular genetics recap.

Slide 57 is a textbook drawing of the double helix, from Strachan and Read’s Human Molecular Genetics6. Two of its phrases need decoding. “Relatively weak glue”: each hydrogen bond between paired bases is weak, which is what lets the strands separate when DNA is copied or read. “Antisense (mirror image) copies”: the strands are complementary and run in opposite directions, so each strand, read in its own 5′→3′ direction, is the reverse complement of the other. That reverse complement is why one SNP has two names (see Strands).

Slides Slides 58–59 · The genome by the numbers, and its chromosomes

Slide 58’s 3.3 billion base pairs is an older round figure; GRCh38 has 3.1 billion per set of chromosomes, and a person’s body cells carry two sets. “1.5% protein-coding” counts the protein-coding exons. “Over 99.9% shared” counts single-base differences: two copies of a chromosome differ at about one base in 1,300.

Slides Slide 60 · A karyotype, and how bands are named

A karyotype is a picture of one cell’s chromosomes, stained and arranged in pairs by number. The stain leaves light and dark bands. A band name gives the chromosome, the arm (p for the short arm, q for the long arm), then the region and band counted outward from the centromere, then any sub-bands after a point: 1p36.33 is chromosome 1, short arm, region 3, band 6, sub-band 33, said “one p three six point three three”.

The slide’s last note matters for reading the picture: chromosomes are this compact only when a cell divides. The rest of the time the DNA is unwound and spread through the nucleus, which is how it can be copied and read.

Slides Slides 61–63 · From DNA to protein, and how a change can matter

Slide 61 states why molecular genetics matters for epidemiology: a change in DNA, inside or outside a gene’s coding sequence, can change what a cell does. Slide 62 shows four places where that happens. Transcription factors bind a gene’s promoter and enhancers and decide when the gene is copied into RNA (transcription). The RNA’s exons are joined and its introns removed (splicing). A ribosome reads the RNA three bases at a time and transfer RNAs bring the matching amino acids (translation). The genetic code table lists which amino acid, or stop signal, each three-base codon stands for.

Slide 63 lists the kinds of change. In coding sequence: nonsense (a new stop codon), missense or non-synonymous (a different amino acid) and synonymous (same amino acid). Outside it: changes in promoters, enhancers, splice sites, untranslated regions (UTRs) and chromatin structure. Its last line, that fewer than half of disease-causing loci are coding changes, holds in human GWAS: most associated SNPs lie in introns or between genes5.

SNV, polymorphism and SNP

A single-nucleotide variant (SNV) is a position in the genome where one base is replaced by another in some chromosome copies: G in most copies and A in others, for example. The two bases are the variant’s alleles. A polymorphism is, by a traditional convention, a variant whose less common allele has a frequency of at least 1% in some population, and a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) is an SNV that common. In practice “SNP” is used for every single-base variant on a genotyping array, rare or not, and this course follows that usage.

Nearly all SNPs are biallelic: they have exactly two alleles, because a second mutation at the same base is rare. Some positions have three or four alleles in a large enough sample; dbSNP lists G, A and C at rs1801133 in the MTHFR gene, for example8. A single 0/1/2 column can describe only two alleles (Lecture 0 shows how files split a multiallelic site).

The pair of alleles also sorts SNPs into two kinds. A transition exchanges the two purines (A↔G) or the two pyrimidines (C↔T); every other change is a transversion. Transitions arise more often than transversions in human DNA6, and Table 2 shows the result in PennCATH. Two of the transversion pairs, A/T and C/G, will need special care: their two alleles are complementary, so they read the same on both strands (see A/T and C/G SNPs).

Run it Count allele pairs yourself

With the practical’s files on your computer (see Set up your computer), one line of awk counts the A/T and C/G SNPs: column 5 of the .bim file is A1 and column 6 is A2.

bash 
$ awk '$5$6=="AT" || $5$6=="TA" || $5$6=="CG" || $5$6=="GC"' penncath.bim | wc -l
$ awk '{print ($5 < $6 ? $5"/"$6 : $6"/"$5)}' penncath.bim | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn

The first command should print 128999. The second prints the counts of Table 2, with the one-allele row split by the allele that was seen (-9/A, -9/C and so on) and rs9428247 as -9/-9.

rsIDs: names from dbSNP

dbSNP, the public database of human variants at the US National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), gives each known variant position a reference SNP identifier, the rsID: “rs” followed by a number, as in rs671 or rs30943159. An rsID names a position and the variants seen there. It does not name an allele, it does not say on which strand the letters are written, and it stays the same when the reference genome is updated. Every one of the 861,473 SNPs in penncath.bim is named by an rsID.

Because an rsID does not fix the alleles, a results table that gives only an rsID and an “effect allele” still leaves the strand open. The Strands section shows what that costs.

Positions and genome builds

The other way to name a SNP is by its address: chromosome and base-pair position, often followed by the two alleles, as in slide 68’s chr12:111803962:G:A for rs671. A position counts bases along the chromosome’s + strand, the strand that runs 5′→3′ from the tip of the short arm, starting from 1 in PLINK and VCF files. A position is only meaningful together with its genome build: the version of the reference genome it refers to. Each new build adds and corrects sequence, so the same base gets a new number.

Two builds are in use. GRCh37 (called hg19 by the UCSC Genome Browser) was released in 2009 and GRCh38 (hg38) in December 201310. Liftover tools convert positions from one build to the other.

In the data Which build is PennCATH on?

The .bim file does not say. Two checks settle it. The largest position on chromosome 1 in penncath.bim is 249,198,692, which is longer than chromosome 1 of GRCh38 (248,956,422 base pairs) but fits within GRCh37’s (249,250,621)1. Slide 68 makes four points:

  1. SNPs almost always have two alleles.
  2. The same SNP is “a C/T SNP” on one strand and “a G/A SNP” on the other, so the strand must be stated.
  3. The usual choice is the + strand of the reference genome, which runs 5′→3′ from the telomere of the short (p) arm. The current build is GRCh38/hg38.
  4. A SNP is named by its rsID or by chromosome and position: rs671 and chr12:111803962:G:A are the same variant on GRCh38 (on GRCh37 it is chr12:112241766:G:A).

From a DNA sample to a genotype matrix Core

The slides move from molecules straight to genotypes. In between is a measurement, and the measurement shapes the data. PennCATH’s genotypes were measured on the Affymetrix Genome-Wide Human SNP Array 6.012,13. Figure 2 shows the chain from a person’s DNA to the files the practical opens.

Measuring. A genotyping array is a glass chip carrying short single-stranded DNA probes, each matching the genome next to one chosen SNP. A person’s DNA is cut into fragments, labeled with a fluorescent dye and washed over the chip. Fragments stick (hybridize) to the probes whose sequence they match, and a scanner measures the light at each probe. For each SNP the result is two numbers per person: a signal for allele 1 and a signal for allele 2. Affymetrix scanners write these intensities into one .CEL file per person. Lecture 0 explains the chemistry step by step (How an array reads one SNP).

Calling. Genotype calling turns the two signals into a genotype. The calling software takes all people at one SNP and plots the signal for allele 1 against the signal for allele 2, one point per person (a cluster plot). People with the same genotype form a cluster, and each person gets the genotype of the cluster they fall in. A person between clusters gets no call, which becomes a missing value. Lecture 0’s cluster-plot widget lets you move the cluster boundaries and watch the calls change (From light to genotype calls).

Two consequences matter for the rest of the lecture. A SNP whose clusters are poorly separated gets wrong or missing calls in many people at once, and every person’s call depends on how well their sample worked. Finding such SNPs (columns) and such people (rows) is what quality control does (QC step 1: missing calls; QC step 2: Hardy–Weinberg).

In PennCATH, A1 is the less frequent allele (the minor allele) at every SNP: its frequency is below 0.5 at 861,234 SNPs and exactly 0.5 at the other 239 (computed for this page). That is PLINK 1.9’s rule: when it writes a fileset, it makes the major allele A2 unless --keep-allele-order is given14; the practical’s log shows that PennCATH was last written by PLINK 1.9 with --make-bed7.

penncath.bed is binary: a 3-byte header, then one block per SNP in .bim order, with each person’s genotype in 2 bits, in .fam order. A block for 1,401 people takes 351 bytes (1,401 / 4 = 350.25, rounded up), so the file is 3 + 861,473 × 351 = 302,377,026 bytes, exactly its size. Because the blocks run SNP by SNP (SNP-major order), reading one SNP for everyone is fast, and reading one person means visiting all 861,473 blocks.

Pitfall A VCF exported by PLINK is not aligned to the reference

A .bim file does not record which allele is in the reference genome. plink --recode vcf therefore writes A2 as REF and flags it as provisional (a PR entry in the INFO column)14. For rs3094315 it would write REF = T and ALT = C: − strand letters, and a REF that is not the reference base. Before such a file is combined with a reference-aligned one, its alleles must be checked against the reference genome (see Harmonizing two files).

Counting the other allele

Suppose a column \(g\) counts copies of one allele in \(m\) people with a call. Its allele frequency estimate and its column mean follow directly.

\[ \hat{p} = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} g_i \]
\(\hat{p}\)
Estimated frequency of the counted allele in these people, between 0 and 1.
\(m\)
Number of people with a call at this SNP; people with a missing call are left out.
\(i\)
Index of a person with a call, from 1 to \(m\).
\(g_i\)
Copies of the counted allele that person \(i\) carries: 0, 1 or 2.
\(2m\)
Number of allele copies the \(m\) people carry, two each.

Each person carries two copies of the SNP, so the column sum counts copies of the counted allele and \(2m\) counts all copies. If everyone had \(g_i = 0\), then \(\hat{p} = 0\); if everyone had 2, then \(\hat{p} = 1\). The column mean is \(2\hat{p}\). Worked number: the first column of Table 4 has \(m = 11\) calls summing to 9, so \(\hat{p} = 9/22 = 0.41\). Grouping people by genotype gives the slides’ form \((2n_{11} + n_{12})/(2n)\), where \(n_{11}\) and \(n_{12}\) count people with genotypes 11 and 12. The next page asks how precise this estimate is.

Counting the other allele replaces every entry by 2 minus itself. Its frequency is then 1 minus the old one.

\[ \begin{gathered} g_i' = 2 - g_i \\ \hat{p}' = 1 - \hat{p} \end{gathered} \]
\(g_i'\)
Copies of the other allele that person \(i\) carries: 2, 1 or 0 where \(g_i\) is 0, 1 or 2.
\(\hat{p}'\)
Estimated frequency of the other allele.

A person’s two copies are either the counted allele or the other one, so the two counts add to 2. Summing \(g_i'\) over people and dividing by \(2m\) gives \((2m - \sum g_i)/(2m) = 1 - \hat{p}\). Worked number: counting G instead of A in the first column of Table 4 gives \(13/22 = 0.59 = 1 - 0.41\).

The effect of a SNP on a trait is estimated by fitting a straight line of the trait \(y\) against \(g\) by least squares (the line that minimizes the sum of squared vertical distances to the points). Its slope \(\hat{\beta}\) is the change in average \(y\) per extra copy of the counted allele. Writing the same fitted line in terms of \(g'\) shows what recoding does to it.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y} &= \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta}\, g \\ &= \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta}\,(2 - g') \\ &= (\hat{\alpha} + 2\hat{\beta}) - \hat{\beta}\, g' \end{aligned} \]
\(\hat{y}\)
Trait value predicted by the fitted line for a person with \(g\) copies of the counted allele.
\(\hat{\alpha}\)
Intercept: the predicted trait value at \(g = 0\), that is, for a person with no copy of the counted allele.
\(\hat{\beta}\)
Slope: the change in the predicted trait value per extra copy of the counted allele.
\(g\), \(g'\)
Copies of the counted allele and of the other allele, \(g' = 2 - g\).

Recoding moves no person and changes no trait value; it reads the horizontal axis from the other end. So the fitted line is the same line. In the new coding its slope is \(-\hat{\beta}\) and its intercept, the line’s value at \(g' = 0\) (which is \(g = 2\)), is \(\hat{\alpha} + 2\hat{\beta}\). How well the line fits, its \(R^2\) and its p-value, does not change. For a case–control trait the same holds on the log-odds scale: the log odds ratio changes sign, so the odds ratio becomes its reciprocal. Worked number from the widget below, SNP 1: counting A gives \(\hat{\beta} = 4.40\) and \(\hat{\alpha} = 99.94\); counting G gives \(\hat{\beta} = -4.40\) and \(\hat{\alpha} = 99.94 + 2 \times 4.40 = 108.75\).

Pitfall “Reference allele” has three meanings
  1. The base in the reference genome on the + strand: REF in a VCF (G at rs3094315).
  2. In some programs, the allele whose copies are counted.
  3. In a comparison, the baseline allele that an effect is measured against: in “the G allele increases the trait relative to the T allele” (slide 69), T is the reference in this sense.

An example from the workshop’s own data folder: next to the PennCATH files sits sim_pheno.par, written by GCTA, a program that simulated a quantitative trait from three chosen SNPs. It lists rs10736303 with RefAllele T, Frequency 0.479 and Effect −1.2233. GCTA’s T is PennCATH’s A1 (frequency 0.479 in penncath.bed), the counted allele, so “reference” there has meaning 2. The reference genome’s base at this position (10:123,334,457 on GRCh37) is G on the + strand4, which is C on PennCATH’s strand: in meaning 1, GCTA’s “reference allele” is the non-reference allele. When you write or read “reference allele”, say which meaning is intended.

In the data How the practical’s merge handles strands

PLINK’s --bmerge matches alleles by their letters, so it handles swaps by itself. It cannot handle flips: rs2465136 would have four letters after merging (C, T, G and A), which is impossible for a biallelic SNP. PLINK stops and lists every such SNP in a .missnp file. The practical then complements those SNPs in PennCATH with --flip and merges again7,14. A flipped-and-swapped SNP becomes a plain swap after the flip, which the second merge handles.

The 27 A/T and C/G SNPs never reach the .missnp file: an A/T SNP read from the other strand is still A/T, so the letters always match. The practical’s notebook notes that these SNPs stay strand-ambiguous, and that a production analysis would resolve them with allele frequencies or remove them7. QC step 7 runs the merge.

A/T and C/G SNPs

Slides Slide 69 · The same claim in two studies, and when you can tell

The SNPs and traits are made up; the background is Pieter Bruegel the Elder’s painting The Tower of Babel (1563). Lecture 0 decodes both examples (Strands in SNP data):

  • rs8675309. Study A’s G/T read from the other strand is C/A, Study B’s pair. The letters alone prove the studies used opposite strands, and both say the same thing: “strand unambiguous”.
  • rs90210. A C/G SNP. On the same strand, Study B’s “C decreases relative to G” is Study A’s “G increases relative to C”: they agree. On opposite strands, Study B’s C is Study A’s G, and they contradict each other. The letters cannot say which: “strand ambiguous”.

Allele frequencies can settle rs90210 when they are far from 0.5. Suppose Study A’s G has frequency 0.20. If Study B’s C has frequency 0.79, then Study B’s G has 0.21: same strand, and the studies agree. If Study B’s C has frequency 0.21, it matches Study A’s G read from the other strand: opposite strands, and the studies disagree about the direction of the effect.

An A/T SNP’s complement is T/A, the same pair, so its letters cannot reveal the strand; the same holds for C/G. Lecture 0 calls these strand-ambiguous or palindromic SNPs. Only frequencies are left: an allele with frequency 0.2 in one file should have a frequency near 0.2 in the other, if the two samples come from similar populations. Figure 3 applies this to the 27 A/T and C/G SNPs shared by PennCATH and the panel.

The rule behind Figure 3, the default of the TwoSampleMR software for example, has three parts21,22:

  1. If the minor allele frequency is above a threshold (0.42 by default) in either file, the SNP is ambiguous and is dropped.
  2. Otherwise, if the letter’s frequency is on the same side of 0.5 in both files, the files use the same strand.
  3. If not, they use opposite strands, and file 2’s letters are complemented.

For the 27 SNPs this gives 13 on the same strand, 9 on opposite strands, 4 ambiguous and 1 inconsistent.

Why not trust a frequency of 0.45 against 0.55? Estimates carry sampling error: from 1,401 people, an allele frequency near 0.45 has a standard error of about 0.009, and from 503 people about 0.016 (the next page derives these numbers). Real frequencies also differ between populations, and between cases and controls at a SNP that affects the disease, by a few hundredths. Near 0.5, differences of that size can move a frequency across 0.5, and the rule would pick the wrong strand.

A real case: rs1333049, the coronary artery disease SNP in band 9p21.323, is a C/G SNP. In PennCATH its G allele has frequency 0.475. On the + strand of GRCh37, 1000 Genomes gives G a frequency of 0.528 and C 0.472 among the 503 people labeled EUR4. Both readings put the files within 0.06 of each other, and the minor allele frequency is above 0.42 in both, so the rule drops the SNP. One of the best-known SNPs for the disease that PennCATH studies cannot be strand-aligned from frequencies; its strand has to come from the array’s annotation file, which gives each SNP’s strand and flanking sequence. Across the whole of PennCATH, 15,884 of the 128,999 A/T and C/G SNPs have a minor allele frequency above 0.42 (computed for this page): 1.8% of all SNPs would be dropped by this rule whenever PennCATH is aligned to another file by frequencies.

Harmonizing two files

Harmonizing two files means rewriting one of them so that every SNP’s genotype counts, allele frequency and effect refer to the same allele as in the other. For each SNP in both files:

  1. Match the SNP by rsID, or by chromosome and position after checking that both files use the same build.
  2. Compare the letters: same, swap, flip, flip + swap, or no match (drop the SNP).
  3. For A/T and C/G SNPs, compare frequencies; drop the SNP if its minor allele frequency is too close to 0.5.
  4. After the change, check that each SNP’s effect allele has a similar frequency in both files; a large difference points to an error or to different populations.
  5. Record what was changed and what was dropped.

Pitfall Flipping A/T and C/G SNPs without evidence

PLINK’s --flip complements the letters of every SNP it is given. On an A/T SNP that turns A1 = A into A1 = T: the counts in the .bed file stay, but they now count the other allele, so every genotype is reversed in letters. If the SNP was already on the same strand as the other file, the files now disagree, and the merge raises no error because A/T is still A/T. The reverse mistake, leaving an opposite-strand A/T SNP unflipped, does the same damage: in the merged column the A of one dataset is the T of the other, the allele frequency differs sharply between the two datasets, and any comparison between them, such as cases genotyped in one batch and controls in another, finds a false difference. Flip A/T and C/G SNPs only on evidence (frequencies far from 0.5, or the array annotation), or remove them before merging.

The next two, genotype and allele frequencies and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, start from the counts this page has defined: Estimating frequencies and testing Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium asks how precisely \(\hat{p}\) is estimated and whether the three genotype counts of a SNP fit what its allele frequency predicts.

Key points
  • Each entry of a genotype matrix counts copies (0, 1 or 2) of one allele at one SNP. It can be interpreted only with three labels: the SNP’s position, the counted allele, and the strand of the letters.
  • An rsID names a position, not an allele or a strand. A base-pair position needs its genome build: rs3094315 is at 752,566 on GRCh37 (PennCATH’s build) and 817,186 on GRCh38.
  • An array measures two signal intensities per SNP and person; calling clusters people into genotypes; PLINK stores the matrix as .bed + .bim + .fam. PennCATH: 1,401 people × 861,473 SNPs, 2.25% of calls missing.
  • PLINK 1.9 counts A1 (made the minor allele), a VCF counts ALT, and PLINK 2’s --export A counts REF. Counting the other allele maps \(g \to 2 - g\), \(\hat{p} \to 1 - \hat{p}\), \(\hat{\beta} \to -\hat{\beta}\) and \(\hat{\alpha} \to \hat{\alpha} + 2\hat{\beta}\); an odds ratio becomes its reciprocal.
  • The same SNP is C/T on one strand and G/A on the other. Merging handles swaps; flips are found through the .missnp file and fixed with --flip. A/T and C/G SNPs, 15% of PennCATH, can be aligned only by frequencies far from 0.5, or must be dropped.
  • “Reference allele” can mean the reference-genome base, the counted allele or the baseline allele of a comparison: always say which.

Check your understanding

Line 4 of penncath.bim is 1 rs3094315 0 752566 C T. Person 10004 has genotype CT. What number does PLINK’s genotype matrix hold for this person, and what would a VCF aligned to the GRCh37 + strand hold?

PLINK counts A1, here C: one copy, so 1. On the + strand C reads G and T reads A, so CT is GA. The VCF’s REF is G and ALT is A, so GT is 0/1 and the count of ALT is also 1.

For a TT person the two files differ: PLINK holds 0 (no copy of C), while the VCF holds 1/1, two copies of A. At this SNP the VCF count is always 2 minus the PLINK count: PLINK counts C, the partner of the reference base G, while the VCF counts A, the partner of PennCATH’s T. The two files count different alleles.

Compute the frequency of A1 at rs3817856 among the 12 people of Table 4. Why can it be above 0.5 when PLINK makes A1 the minor allele?

The column has 11 calls (10009 is NA): 2, 0, 1, 2, 0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2. They sum to 14, so \(\hat{p} = 14/22 = 0.64\).

A1 is the minor allele in the whole file: its frequency among all 1,401 people is 0.494. Twelve people are a tiny sample. With 22 allele copies the standard error of a frequency near 0.5 is about \(\sqrt{0.25/22} \approx 0.11\), so 0.64 is only about 1.3 standard errors above 0.494. The minor allele is a property of the whole sample that PLINK saw when it wrote the file, not of any subset.

A least-squares fit of a trait on copies of T at a C/T SNP gives intercept 99.03 and slope 2.81 per copy. What are the intercept and slope if C is counted instead? Does the p-value change?

With \(g' = 2 - g\), the same line is \(\hat{y} = (99.03 + 2 \times 2.81) - 2.81\, g'\): slope −2.81 per copy of C and intercept 104.65. The intercept is now the predicted trait value for a person with two copies of T, which was the old line’s value at \(g = 2\).

The p-value, \(R^2\) and every fitted value stay the same, because no point moved; only the axis is read from the other end. These are the numbers of SNP 2 in the widget.

Slide 69, rs90210: Study A reports that G increases the trait relative to C, with G at frequency 0.20. Study B reports that C decreases it relative to G, with C at frequency 0.79. Do the studies agree? What if Study B’s C had frequency 0.21?

With C at 0.79, Study B’s G has frequency 0.21, close to Study A’s 0.20 for G. Both frequencies are far from 0.5 and on the same side of it, so the studies used the same strand. Study B’s “C decreases relative to G” is then “G increases relative to C”: they agree.

With C at 0.21, Study B’s C has the frequency of Study A’s G, so Study B’s C is Study A’s G read from the other strand. Study B then says that Study A’s G decreases the trait: the studies disagree about the direction. The letters were identical in both scenarios; only the frequencies told them apart.

When the practical merges PennCATH with the 1000 Genomes panel, rs2465136 (G/A in PennCATH, C/T in the panel) lands in the .missnp file, but an A/T SNP written on opposite strands does not. Why, and what goes wrong if nothing is done about the A/T SNP?

After merging, rs2465136 would have four letters, C, T, G and A, which a biallelic SNP cannot have, so PLINK stops and lists it. --flip turns PennCATH’s G/A into C/T, and the second merge succeeds.

An A/T SNP read from the other strand is still A/T, so its letters match and PLINK merges it without complaint. PennCATH’s A then stands for the panel’s T. In the merged column the genotypes of one dataset are reversed, the allele frequency differs sharply between the datasets, and an analysis that mixes them, such as the reference-based PCA of QC step 7, sees a difference that is not real.

A summary statistics file lists rs3094315 at chromosome 1, position 817,186, and does not name its genome build. Which build is it? What happens if you match it to PennCATH by chromosome and position?

817,186 is rs3094315’s GRCh38 position (GRCh37: 752,566), so the file is on GRCh38.

Matching by position to PennCATH, which is on GRCh37, would miss rs3094315. At other SNPs it could match a different variant that sits at the same number in GRCh37. Lift the positions over to one build first, or match by rsID, and then check the alleles.

Classify each allele pair as a transition or a transversion, and say which are strand-ambiguous: A/G, C/G, T/A, G/T, C/T, A/C.

Transitions: A/G and C/T (purine to purine, pyrimidine to pyrimidine). Transversions: C/G, T/A, G/T and A/C. Strand-ambiguous: C/G and T/A, the pairs whose letters are complementary.

Every strand-ambiguous SNP is a transversion, and no transition is ambiguous: the complement of A/G is T/C, a different pair. That is why the 575,207 transitions in PennCATH can always be strand-aligned from their letters, while 128,999 of its 260,112 transversions cannot.

A colleague exports PennCATH with plink --recode vcf and finds REF = T and ALT = C at rs3094315, while dbSNP says the reference allele is G. Which is wrong?

The genotypes are right; the VCF’s REF is not the reference base. A .bim file does not record the reference allele, so PLINK wrote A2 (T) as REF and flagged it as provisional with PR in the INFO column. The reference base on the + strand is G, which is C on PennCATH’s strand. Before merging with a reference-aligned VCF, check each SNP’s alleles against the reference genome and flip or swap as Table 7 describes.
