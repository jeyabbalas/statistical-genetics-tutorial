Genetic epidemiology: what it is and how it improves health

Epidemiology measures how often a disease occurs, in whom, and what makes it more or less likely. Genetic epidemiology adds the genotype, a determinant of health that is fixed at conception. This page defines both fields, shows the data each one collects, and follows the routes from a genetic finding to better health that slides 10–25 describe: new drugs, screening of newborns and of people at high risk, and two methods that use genotypes to answer causal questions, Mendelian randomization and the transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS).

Mental model

One causal chain, read in four ways

Genetic epidemiology studies one causal chain, from genotype through molecules and biomarkers to disease, with environment, social factors and confounders acting along the way. Its uses read the chain in different ways: a variant as evidence about a drug target, a genotype as a risk marker, a genotype as an instrument for a causal question, and a genotype as a predictor of a molecule that was not measured.

  • The chain and its data. Epidemiology measures how disease is distributed and what determines it; genetic epidemiology adds G, a determinant fixed at conception and measured for millions of variants at once.
  • 1 · Drug targets. People whose variant lowers a protein (G → M) show what a lifetime of a drug against that protein does to X and Y: ANGPTL3, PCSK9, BCL11A.
  • 2 · Screening. Rare high-risk variants, newborn tests and polygenic scores identify people at high absolute risk of Y early enough to prevent or detect it.
  • 3 · Mendelian randomization. Because alleles are assigned at random at conception, the ratio of G’s effects on Y and on X estimates the arrow X → Y without the confounders U, if G has no other path to Y.
  • 4 · TWAS. Weights learned where expression was measured predict M from genotypes in GWAS samples, so the arrow M → Y can be tested in hundreds of thousands of people.

What the data look like

For a data scientist the difference between the two fields is a difference in columns and rows. Classical epidemiology works with a participant table: one row per person, a few hundred to a few thousand columns of exposures, covariates and outcomes. Genetic epidemiology joins that table, by participant ID, to a genotype matrix with one column per genetic variant, and sometimes adds the relationships between participants.4

Why a genotype is an unusual determinant

A germline genotype differs from every other column in the participant table in five ways, and each one is used or guarded against somewhere in the course:

  • It is fixed at conception. A disease cannot change a person’s germline genotype, so an association between a genotype and a disease cannot be reverse causation (Lecture 0, why inherited genotypes are special variables).
  • It is assigned at random within a family. Each parent passes one of their two alleles to a child with probability ½, independently of the child’s later diet or income. This is the basis of Mendelian randomization (below).
  • It is measured once, cheaply and with little error, for millions of variants at once, which is what makes GWAS possible.
  • It is correlated with ancestry. Allele frequencies differ between populations, and so do environments. Without adjustment, a genotype can be associated with a trait only because both differ between groups: population stratification (page 7, and the simulator on page 3).
  • It is correlated with nearby variants. Linkage disequilibrium means the variant that shows the association is often not the one that causes it (page 6).

From disease biology to drug targets Core

A drug target is the molecule, usually a protein, that a drug binds to change its activity. Most candidate drugs fail in development because acting on the target does not change the disease enough, or because it causes harm. Human genetics can show both before a drug exists. A loss-of-function variant is a variant that reduces or abolishes the activity of the gene’s product, so people who carry one have had less of the protein’s activity since conception. Comparing carriers with non-carriers, in the same population, shows what lowering the protein does over a whole life (the G → M → X → Y row of the mental model):

  • if carriers have lower levels of a biomarker that causes disease, such as LDL cholesterol, the protein affects the disease pathway;
  • if carriers have less disease, inhibiting the protein is likely to help;
  • if carriers, including people with two inactive copies, are otherwise healthy, inhibiting it is likely to be safe.

Slides 15–17 show three ways of finding such variants. In forward genetics the search starts from people with an unusual phenotype and looks for the variant; in reverse genetics it starts from a gene and looks for people whose copies do not work; in a GWAS it starts from a common trait and tests millions of common variants.

Story ANGPTL3: from one family to a drug (forward genetics, slide 15)

2010. Musunuru and colleagues sequenced the exomes (the protein-coding parts of the genome) of two siblings from a family with very low LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol and triglycerides. Both carried two different nonsense variants in ANGPTL3, one on each copy of the gene: they were compound heterozygotes with no working ANGPTL3 protein, and they were healthy.5

2017. Dewey and colleagues sequenced 58,335 participants of the DiscovEHR study. Carriers of one loss-of-function copy had lower triglycerides and LDL cholesterol, and loss-of-function variants were found in 0.33% of people with coronary artery disease against 0.45% of controls, an odds ratio of 0.59 (95% CI 0.41–0.85). In the same paper, evinacumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks ANGPTL3, lowered triglycerides by up to 76% and LDL cholesterol by up to 23% in healthy volunteers.6

2020–2021. In a trial of 65 patients with homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, whose LDL cholesterol stays very high despite other drugs because their LDL receptors barely work, evinacumab lowered LDL cholesterol by 47.1% against a 1.9% rise on placebo.7 The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved it for this disease on 11 February 2021.8

Story PCSK9: from a gene to a class of drugs (reverse genetics, slide 16)

2003. Variants in PCSK9 that increase the protein’s activity were found in French families with very high LDL cholesterol inherited as a dominant trait.9 The gene was now known to act on LDL cholesterol, so the next question ran the other way: what happens to people whose PCSK9 does not work?

2005–2006. Cohen and colleagues sequenced PCSK9 in people with low LDL cholesterol and found two nonsense variants, carried by about 2% of African Americans and associated with 40% lower LDL cholesterol.10 In 15 years of follow-up of the ARIC cohort, the 2.6% of 3,363 Black participants with one of these variants had 28% lower LDL cholesterol and 88% fewer coronary heart disease events (hazard ratio 0.11, 95% CI 0.02–0.81); among 9,524 white participants, the 3.2% carrying the missense variant R46L had 15% lower LDL cholesterol and 47% fewer events (hazard ratio 0.50).11 In 2006 a healthy woman with two inactivating variants, one on each copy, was found to have no detectable PCSK9 in her blood and LDL cholesterol of 14 mg/dL.12

2015–2017. The FDA approved two monoclonal antibodies against PCSK9, alirocumab (July 2015) and evolocumab (August 2015).13 In the FOURIER trial of 27,564 patients with cardiovascular disease, evolocumab lowered LDL cholesterol by 59%, from a median of 92 to 30 mg/dL, and over a median of 2.2 years the primary outcome (cardiovascular death, heart attack, stroke, hospital admission for unstable angina or coronary revascularization) occurred in 9.8% of patients on evolocumab against 11.3% on placebo (hazard ratio 0.85).14

Story BCL11A: from a GWAS to gene editing (slide 17)

Background. Fetal hemoglobin (HbF) is the main hemoglobin before birth. Adults who keep making more of it have milder sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia, the two most common hemoglobinopathies (inherited diseases of hemoglobin), because HbF dilutes or replaces the faulty adult hemoglobin. Slide 17’s first sentence says this: HbF “contributes to variability in the expression of hemoglobinopathies”.

2007–2008. A study of people with extreme F-cell levels mapped a locus at BCL11A on chromosome 2p15.15 Uda and colleagues then ran a GWAS of HbF in 4,305 Sardinians genotyped at 362,129 SNPs: the strongest association, at rs11886868 in BCL11A, had a p-value below 10⁻³⁵. The HbF-raising allele was more common in patients with milder forms of β-thalassemia than in those with the severe form, and the same variant was associated with HbF levels in patients with sickle cell disease.16 Later in 2008, laboratory work showed that the BCL11A protein represses the fetal globin genes in adult red-cell precursors.17

2011–2013. Turning off BCL11A in the red-cell lineage of mice that carry the human sickle cell genes restored HbF and corrected the disease (Xu et al., the second paper on the slide).18 The GWAS variants turned out to lie in an enhancer, a regulatory DNA element, that drives BCL11A only in red-cell precursors; disrupting that enhancer lowers BCL11A there and nowhere else.19

2020–2026. Frangoul and colleagues (the third paper on the slide) edited the enhancer with CRISPR-Cas9 in the blood stem cells of the first two patients, one with transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia and one with sickle cell disease; about 80% of the target alleles were edited, and for more than a year the first patient needed no transfusions and the second had no pain crises (vaso-occlusive events).20 The therapy, exagamglogene autotemcel (exa-cel, sold as Casgevy), was approved by the FDA on 8 December 2023 for sickle cell disease from age 12,21 after 29 of 30 evaluable patients in the pivotal trial were free of vaso-occlusive crises for at least 12 months.22 Approval for transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia followed in January 2024, and on 1 July 2026 both approvals were extended to children from age 2.23

Lifelong genetic effects and trial effects

A variant acts from conception; a drug in a trial acts for a few years in adults who already have disease. The two can be compared per unit of biomarker. Combining nine variants in six genes that lower LDL cholesterol, in 312,321 people, Ference and colleagues estimated that each 1 mmol/L (38.7 mg/dL) of lifelong lower LDL cholesterol goes with a 54.5% (95% CI 48.8–59.5%) lower risk of coronary heart disease, about three times the reduction per mmol/L seen when a statin is started later in life.24 In the statin trials, which lasted about five years, each 1.0 mmol/L reduction lowered the risk of major vascular events by about 22% (rate ratio 0.78).25 The same logic explains the PCSK9 numbers: a 28% reduction from a typical LDL cholesterol of about 3.5 mmol/L is about 1 mmol/L, yet carriers had 88% fewer events. Genetic studies therefore show the direction and the safety of an effect better than its size in a short trial.

Genetic support raises the success rate of drugs

These stories are not exceptions. Nelson and colleagues traced drug development programs from the start to approval: the share of drug mechanisms with support from human genetic associations rose from 2.0% at the preclinical stage to 8.2% among approved drugs, and they estimated that choosing genetically supported targets could double the success rate.26 With ten more years of data, Minikel and colleagues estimated that a drug mechanism with genetic support is 2.6 times as likely to succeed in clinical development as one without.27

In the reading Uffelmann et al. 2021, “Outlook”

The reading makes the same point in its Outlook section: “Retrospective reviews of drug development projects have shown that studies targeting GWAS disease risk genes were less likely to fail owing to lack of efficacy.”28 It names the PCSK9 inhibitors as the example inspired by a rare loss-of-function variant, and the Open Targets database as a tool that links GWAS genes to drugs.

Pitfall A variant is not a drug
  • Timing and size. A variant’s effect accumulates over a lifetime; a drug started at 60 acts on disease that has already developed (previous section).
  • Direction. A gain-of-function variant that causes disease, like the 2003 PCSK9 variants, suggests a target, but only carriers of loss-of-function variants show what inhibiting it does.
  • Few carriers. Rare variants give wide confidence intervals: the hazard ratio of 0.11 for PCSK9 nonsense carriers had a 95% interval from 0.02 to 0.81.
  • Other effects. Carriers show the protein’s effects on every trait, not only the one of interest; a drug may also act on other proteins. Both are checked by testing the variant against many traits (a phenome-wide association study).

Screening means testing people without symptoms to find those who have a disease, or a high risk of it, early enough to act. Slides 19 and 20 show three kinds of genetic screening. Predictive testing for rare high-risk variants

Pathogenic variants in BRCA1 or BRCA2, genes whose proteins repair broken DNA, are carried by about 1 in 400 people (0.2–0.3%), and by about 2% of people of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry.30 In a prospective study of 6,036 BRCA1 and 3,820 BRCA2 carriers, the risk of breast cancer by age 80 was 72% and 69%, and of ovarian cancer 44% and 17%,31 against about 13% and 1.1% over a lifetime for women in general in the United States.30 Carriers are offered breast MRI from a younger age and surgery that removes the ovaries or breasts to reduce risk. Lynch syndrome, caused by a pathogenic variant in one of the DNA mismatch-repair genes, is carried by about 1 in 279 people and brings high risks of colorectal and endometrial cancer; carriers are offered colonoscopy every one to two years.32

Two numbers show what such testing can and cannot do. Each first-degree relative of a carrier has a 50% chance of carrying the same variant, so testing the relatives of a known carrier (cascade testing) finds many carriers with few tests. But carriers are rare: with the round numbers above, 0.25% of women times a 70% risk is about 0.18% of women, roughly 1 in 70 of all breast cancers if about 13% of women develop one. Most breast cancers occur in women without a high-risk variant, which is why risk-stratified screening (below) adds common variants.

Newborn screening

Newborn screening tests every baby, usually from drops of blood taken from the heel a day or two after birth and dried on filter paper, for treatable conditions that cause no symptoms at first. Phenylketonuria (PKU) is the classic example: babies with two non-working copies of PAH cannot break down the amino acid phenylalanine, which builds up and damages the developing brain, and a low-phenylalanine diet started in the first weeks prevents the damage. The genotype is fixed, but the disease is preventable by changing the environment, here the diet.

In 2006 the ACMG recommended a uniform U.S. panel of 29 core conditions, chosen by scoring each candidate on whether a reliable test exists, whether early treatment helps, and how well the condition is understood, plus 25 secondary conditions that the same tests find.29,33 The federal Recommended Uniform Screening Panel that grew from it listed 38 core and 26 secondary conditions after infantile Krabbe disease was added in July 2024.34 Slide 19’s table groups the 29 core conditions by the test that finds them:

Most of these tests measure a molecule that the genotype controls (M or X in the mental model), not the DNA itself: an inborn error of metabolism, an inherited block in a chemical pathway, shows up as a high or low concentration of a metabolite. Tandem mass spectrometry measures dozens of metabolites in one blood spot in a few minutes, which is why one test covers 20 of the 29 conditions.

A screen is not a diagnosis. Take illustrative numbers: a condition affects 1 in 10,000 newborns, the test flags every affected baby, and it also flags 0.1% of healthy babies. Of 1,000,000 newborns, 100 are affected and about 1,000 healthy babies are flagged as well, so only about 100 of 1,100 positive screens (9%) are true. This share is the positive predictive value, and it is why every positive screen is followed by a confirmatory test.

Risk-stratified screening

Breast cancer screening by mammography is offered by age: in England from 50 to 71 every three years, in the United States from 40. Risk-stratified screening sets the start, the interval or the type of test by each woman’s estimated absolute risk instead: the probability that she develops breast cancer over a stated period, here from age 20 to 80. Slide 20 shows the model that computes it, BOADICEA, in the version of Lee and colleagues (2019). It combines rare variants in BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, CHEK2 and ATM; a polygenic score of 313 SNPs (a weighted sum of risk alleles); a residual polygenic and family component; questionnaire risk factors such as age at first period, number of children, height, body mass index, alcohol and hormone use; and mammographic density, the share of the breast that looks dense on the mammogram.35

Risk models multiply relative risks. A relative risk is the risk in one group divided by the risk in a reference group, and models usually assume that each factor multiplies the rate of disease by its own relative risk, whatever the other factors are. The polygenic score of Mavaddat and colleagues has an odds ratio of 1.61 per standard deviation (95% CI 1.57–1.65): a woman 1 SD above the mean has 1.61 times the rate of the average woman, 2 SD above has 1.61² ≈ 2.6 times.37 The model turns the combined relative risk into an absolute risk:

\[ \begin{gathered} r_i = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\Lambda_0\, e^{\eta_i}\right) \\ \eta_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \ln \mathrm{RR}_{ik} \end{gathered} \]
\(r_i\)
Absolute risk of woman \(i\): the probability that she is diagnosed with breast cancer between ages 20 and 80, between 0 and 1.
\(\Lambda_0\)
Baseline cumulative hazard from 20 to 80: the sum of the yearly rates of breast cancer, for a woman whose relative risks are all 1. Set so that the model’s average risk matches cancer registry rates; about 0.10 in the widget below.
\(e^{\eta_i}\)
Her combined relative risk: the factor by which her rates are multiplied, from 0 upward.
\(\eta_i\)
The logarithm of the combined relative risk, the sum of the logarithms of the separate relative risks.
\(\mathrm{RR}_{ik}\)
Relative risk of woman \(i\) from factor \(k\), for example \(1.61^{z}\) for a polygenic score \(z\) standard deviations from the mean.
\(k, K\)
Index and number of risk factors (polygenic score, density, questionnaire factors, rare variants).

Relative risks multiply, so their logarithms add, which is why \(\eta_i\) is a sum. Multiplying every yearly rate by \(e^{\eta_i}\) multiplies the cumulative hazard by the same factor, and the probability of at least one event when the cumulative hazard is \(H\) is \(1 - e^{-H}\). For small \(H\) the risk is close to \(H\) itself; for large \(H\) it approaches 1 but never exceeds it, which a plain product of relative risks would not guarantee. With \(\Lambda_0 = 0.10\): a woman with all relative risks 1 has \(1 - e^{-0.10} = 9.5\%\); a score 2 SD above the mean gives \(e^{\eta} = 2.59\) and \(1 - e^{-0.259} = 22.8\%\), in the moderate band; 2 SD below gives 3.8%; a BRCA1 carrier with a relative risk of about 12 gives \(1 - e^{-1.2} = 70\%\). The model ignores death from other causes, which real models such as BOADICEA include.

With all factors, BOADICEA predicts lifetime risks for women in the UK from 2.8% at the 1st percentile to 30.6% at the 99th; 14.7% of women fall in the moderate band and 1.1% in the high band.35 The widget simulates a cohort with the same spread and asks the screening question directly: if extra screening is offered above a risk threshold, how many women are offered it, and how many of the women who will develop breast cancer are among them?

Try itWho would a risk threshold offer extra screening?

What to notice. With no personal information every woman gets the average risk of 11%, so a 17% threshold offers nobody extra screening and a 10% threshold offers everybody. Each information set spreads the predicted risks further apart: at the 17% threshold the polygenic score alone offers extra screening to about 12% of women, who include about 22% of the future cases, and all factors together offer it to about 14%, who include about 27% of the cases. These women have about twice the rate of breast cancer of the women below the threshold (about 210 against 90 per 1,000). Adding a BRCA1/BRCA2 test moves the 1 in 400 carriers to the top of the curve but changes the overall numbers little, because carriers are rare. Most future cases remain below any threshold that offers extra screening to a small group: a risk model ranks women, it does not say who will get the disease.

In the reading Uffelmann et al. 2021, “Applications · Risk prediction”

The reading describes polygenic scores as weighted sums of risk alleles that “can be used to identify individuals at a high risk of disease for clinical interventions and provide additional information over traditional clinical risk scores for stratified screening”, and its Figure 4 shows how a score is computed from GWAS summary statistics. It also warns that scores built from GWAS of one ancestry predict less well in others.28

Pitfall High relative risk, modest absolute risk

A score in the top 1% has a relative risk of about 3 compared with the average woman, which sounds alarming, but the lifetime risk it implies is about 30%: most women in the top 1% never develop breast cancer. Thresholds such as 17% and 30% are policy choices that weigh the benefits of extra screening against its costs and harms. And the 313-SNP score was developed in women of European ancestry; its relative risks are smaller in other ancestries, a consequence of who has been studied (page 3).

Mendelian randomization: genotypes as instruments Core

Does raising HDL cholesterol prevent heart attacks? Does lowering C-reactive protein (CRP), a blood marker of inflammation, prevent them? Observational data cannot answer this directly: people with high HDL cholesterol also exercise more, drink differently and have different diets, and these confounders (U in the mental model) affect both the exposure X and the outcome Y. A randomized controlled trial, which assigns the treatment at random, answers it, but needs a drug, years and tens of thousands of patients. Mendelian randomization (MR) uses the random assignment of alleles at conception instead.38,39 The idea was first proposed by Martijn Katan in 1986, for the question whether low cholesterol causes cancer.40

Why the naive regression fails, in one simulation

The widget below simulates people from the simplest model in which MR works. Each person \(i\) has a genotype \(g_i\) (0, 1 or 2 copies of an allele with frequency 0.3), an unmeasured confounder \(u_i\), an exposure and an outcome:

\[ \begin{gathered} X_i = \gamma g_i + c\,u_i + \varepsilon_{Xi} \\ Y_i = \beta X_i + c\,u_i + \alpha g_i + \varepsilon_{Yi} \end{gathered} \]
\(X_i, Y_i\)
Exposure (for example HDL cholesterol) and outcome of person \(i\), in standardized simulation units.
\(\gamma\)
Change in the exposure per copy of the allele: the strength of the instrument (Assumption 1 needs \(\gamma \ne 0\)).
\(g_i\)
Number of copies of the allele that person \(i\) carries: 0, 1 or 2.
\(c\)
Strength of confounding: \(u_i\) raises both \(X_i\) and \(Y_i\) by \(c\) per unit.
\(u_i\)
Unmeasured confounder of person \(i\), standard normal and independent of \(g_i\) (Assumption 2).
\(\varepsilon_{Xi}, \varepsilon_{Yi}\)
Independent standard normal noise.
\(\beta\)
The causal effect of \(X\) on \(Y\), which the study wants: the change in \(Y\) when \(X\) is raised by one unit.
\(\alpha\)
Direct effect of the genotype on \(Y\) that bypasses \(X\) (horizontal pleiotropy). Assumption 3 says \(\alpha = 0\).

Regressing \(Y\) on \(X\) gives a slope that converges to \(\beta\) plus \(c^2 / \Var(X)\), because people with high \(u\) are high on both. With \(\beta = 0\), \(c = 1\), \(\gamma = 0.25\) and allele frequency 0.3, \(\Var(g) = 2 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.42\) and \(\Var(X) = 0.25^2 \times 0.42 + 1 + 1 \approx 2.03\), so the naive slope is about 0.49 although \(X\) has no effect at all. Regressing on the genotype instead avoids \(u\): \(g\) is independent of \(u\), so the slope of \(X\) on \(g\) is \(\gamma\) and the slope of \(Y\) on \(g\) is \(\beta\gamma + \alpha\). Their ratio is \(\beta + \alpha/\gamma\), which equals \(\beta\) when \(\alpha = 0\).

The two slopes on the genotype are estimated by ordinary regressions, and their ratio is the Wald ratio:

\[ \begin{gathered} \hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IV}} = \frac{\hat{\beta}_{GY}}{\hat{\beta}_{GX}} \\ \SE(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IV}}) \approx \frac{\SE(\hat{\beta}_{GY})}{|\hat{\beta}_{GX}|} \end{gathered} \]
\(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IV}}\)
The instrumental-variable (MR) estimate of the causal effect \(\beta\), in units of \(Y\) per unit of \(X\).
\(\hat{\beta}_{GY}\)
Estimated slope of the outcome on the genotype (units of \(Y\) per allele; a log odds ratio per allele for a disease).
\(\hat{\beta}_{GX}\)
Estimated slope of the exposure on the genotype (units of \(X\) per allele).
\(\SE(\cdot)\)
Standard error of an estimate; the second line is the first-order approximation that treats \(\hat{\beta}_{GX}\) as known.

Each allele moves the exposure by \(\beta_{GX}\) and, only through the exposure, the outcome by \(\beta\,\beta_{GX}\); dividing removes the per-allele scale. The denominator is also why precision depends on instrument strength: the standard error of \(\hat{\beta}_{GY}\) is divided by \(|\hat{\beta}_{GX}|\), so halving the variant’s effect on the exposure doubles the standard error of the ratio. Worked example: \(\hat{\beta}_{GX} = 0.25\) per allele and \(\hat{\beta}_{GY} = 0.05\) (standard error 0.03) give \(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IV}} = 0.20\) with standard error \(0.03/0.25 = 0.12\), a 95% confidence interval from −0.04 to 0.44. When \(\hat{\beta}_{GX}\) is near 0 the approximation fails and the ratio becomes unstable.

Instrument strength is summarized by the F statistic of the regression of the exposure on the genotype:

\[ \begin{gathered} F = \left(\frac{\hat{\beta}_{GX}}{\SE(\hat{\beta}_{GX})}\right)^{2} \\ \approx \frac{n R^2}{1 - R^2} \end{gathered} \]
\(F\)
First-stage F statistic for one variant: the squared z-score of its association with the exposure, from 0 upward.
\(n\)
Number of people in which the exposure was regressed on the genotype.
\(R^2\)
Share of the variance of the exposure explained by the genotype, between 0 and 1; single variants usually explain well under 1%.

The z-score of a regression slope grows with the square root of the sample size and with the variance the predictor explains, so \(F\) grows in proportion to \(n R^2\). A common rule asks for \(F \ge 10\). Below that, chance correlation between the genotype and the confounders in the sample is not small compared with the genotype’s real effect, and in one-sample MR the estimate is pulled toward the confounded naive estimate: for an expected \(F\) of 10 the bias is roughly a tenth of the naive estimate’s bias.41 In the default simulation, \(n = 5{,}000\) and \(R^2 = 1.3\%\) give \(F \approx 66\); with \(\gamma = 0.03\), \(R^2 = 0.02\%\) and \(F \approx 1\), a weak instrument.

With the “Pleiotropy” preset (α = 0.05) the Wald ratio converges to α/γ = 0.2 instead of 0, and a larger sample only makes the wrong answer more precise: no sample size fixes a broken assumption.

Two-sample MR from GWAS summary statistics

The two slopes do not have to come from the same people. In two-sample MR, \(\hat{\beta}_{GX}\) comes from a published GWAS of the exposure and \(\hat{\beta}_{GY}\) from a GWAS of the outcome in other people, both read from summary statistics (per-variant estimates and standard errors) after the alleles are aligned. Many variants are combined into one estimate, for example by inverse-variance weighting of their Wald ratios, and methods such as MR-Egger and the weighted median test whether some variants violate Assumption 3. Page 10 simulates them. In two-sample designs a weak instrument biases the estimate toward zero rather than toward the naive estimate.42

CRP and HDL cholesterol: MR and the trials

Slide 23’s two examples are the cases in which MR is most useful: strong observational associations that turn out not to be causal.

  • HDL cholesterol. In observational data, each 1 SD higher HDL cholesterol went with 38% lower odds of myocardial infarction, a heart attack (odds ratio 0.62). Voight and colleagues used a variant in LIPG, 396Ser (allele frequency 2.6%), that raises HDL cholesterol by 0.14 mmol/L and changes no other lipid. Observational data predicted an odds ratio of 0.87 for carriers; among 20,913 cases and 95,407 controls the observed odds ratio was 0.99 (95% CI 0.88–1.11). A score of 14 HDL-raising variants gave 0.93 (0.68–1.26) per SD. As a positive control, a score of 13 LDL-raising variants gave 2.13 per SD, larger than the observational 1.54, as expected for a lifelong cause.43
  • CRP. Elliott and colleagues found five loci associated with CRP in a GWAS of 17,967 people plus 13,615 for replication. Among 28,112 cases of coronary heart disease and 100,823 controls, variants in the CRP gene associated with 20% lower CRP gave an odds ratio of 1.00 (0.97–1.02), where observational data predicted 0.94.44 A larger analysis of 194,418 participants, 46,557 with coronary heart disease, concluded that CRP “is unlikely to be even a modest causal factor”.45

What did the trials find? For HDL cholesterol, two large trials raised it without benefit. Dalcetrapib raised HDL cholesterol by 31–40% in 15,871 patients after an acute coronary syndrome, and the primary outcome occurred in 8.3% on dalcetrapib against 8.0% on placebo (hazard ratio 1.04, 95% CI 0.93–1.16); the trial was stopped for futility.46 Extended-release niacin raised HDL cholesterol by 6 mg/dL in 25,673 patients without reducing major vascular events.47 For CRP the story has a twist that MR anticipated. No drug lowers CRP alone, but a variant in IL6R, the gene for the receptor of interleukin-6, a signal upstream of CRP, lowers CRP by 8.35% per allele and also lowers the risk of coronary heart disease (odds ratio 0.95 per allele among 25,458 cases and 100,740 controls).48 MR thus pointed to the interleukin pathway rather than to CRP itself, and in the CANTOS trial canakinumab, an antibody against interleukin-1β, lowered CRP and reduced recurrent cardiovascular events (hazard ratio 0.85 at the 150 mg dose, 95% CI 0.74–0.98, in 10,061 patients) without changing lipids.49

In the reading Uffelmann et al. 2021, “Applications”

The reading presents MR as a use of GWAS summary statistics that “can be applied when a randomized control trial is not feasible”, lists the same three assumptions as slide 22, and distinguishes vertical pleiotropy (trait A causes trait B, which MR estimates) from horizontal pleiotropy (a variant affects two traits separately, which breaks Assumption 3).28

Pitfall Assumptions 2 and 3 cannot be proven from the data
  • Pleiotropy (Assumption 3): a variant chosen for HDL cholesterol may also change triglycerides. Voight and colleagues chose LIPG 396Ser because it changes no other lipid; with many variants, methods that allow some invalid instruments are used (page 10).
  • Population stratification (Assumption 2): if allele frequency and the outcome both differ by ancestry, the genotype is linked to confounders. MR inherits every bias of the GWAS it uses (page 3).
  • Linkage disequilibrium: the instrument may be correlated with a variant in a neighboring gene that affects the outcome directly.
  • Lifelong, small changes: MR estimates the effect of a lifelong shift in the exposure, which is not the effect of a drug given for five years (see above).

TWAS: testing genetically predicted gene expression Core

Gene expression, the amount of a gene’s RNA in a tissue, is one of the molecules M between genotype and disease. Measuring it needs tissue samples, which no biobank of 500,000 people has. A transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) tests expression without measuring it in the GWAS sample, in two stages (slide 25).51,52

  1. Train. In a reference dataset that has both genotypes and measured expression, such as GTEx (Genotype-Tissue Expression, a collection of tissues from deceased donors), fit a prediction model for each gene: its expression regressed on the variants near the gene. The models use penalized regression, such as the lasso or the elastic net, or only the strongest expression-associated variant (eQTL). FUSION uses the variants within 500 kb of the gene and keeps the model with the best cross-validated accuracy; PrediXcan uses an elastic net on the variants within 1 Mb.53
  2. Test. Apply the weights to the GWAS data and test the predicted expression against the trait, one gene at a time.
\[ \hat{m}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{J} w_j\, g_{ij} \]
\(\hat{m}_i\)
Predicted expression of one gene in person \(i\) of the GWAS sample, in the units of the training data (usually standardized).
\(j, J\)
Index and number of variants in the gene’s model, from one to a few hundred variants near the gene.
\(w_j\)
Weight of variant \(j\), estimated in the reference data; the lasso and the elastic net set many weights to 0.
\(g_{ij}\)
Number of copies (0, 1 or 2, or an imputed dosage) of variant \(j\)’s counted allele in person \(i\).

This is the same weighted sum as a polygenic score, applied to one gene’s expression instead of to a disease. It predicts only the part of expression that the nearby variants explain, the genetically regulated part; the rest, from environment and from distant variants, is missing. Predicted expression is a function of genotypes alone, so it is fixed at conception like a genotype, and testing it against the trait asks whether the inherited part of the gene’s expression goes with the trait. If a gene’s model has one variant with weight 0.8, then \(\hat{m}_i\) is 0, 0.8 or 1.6 for 0, 1 or 2 copies.

With only GWAS summary statistics, the test statistic for a gene combines the variants’ z-scores with the same weights:52

\[ z_{\mathrm{TWAS}} = \frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{w}}} \]
\(z_{\mathrm{TWAS}}\)
The gene’s TWAS z-score; approximately standard normal if the predicted expression is not associated with the trait.
\(\mathbf{w}\)
Vector of the \(J\) weights of the gene’s expression model.
\(\mathbf{z}\)
Vector of the GWAS z-scores (effect estimate divided by its standard error) of the same \(J\) variants, with alleles aligned to the weights.
\(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\)
The \(J \times J\) matrix of correlations (linkage disequilibrium) between the variants, taken from a reference panel such as 1000 Genomes.

The numerator is a weighted sum of z-scores. Under the null hypothesis the z-scores are standard normal with correlations \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), so the variance of the sum is \(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}\), and dividing by its square root makes the result standard normal again. With one variant of weight 1, \(z_{\mathrm{TWAS}}\) is just that variant’s z-score. With two variants of weight 0.5 and z-scores of 3 each: if they are uncorrelated, \(3/\sqrt{0.5} = 4.24\); if their correlation is 0.8, \(3/\sqrt{0.9} = 3.16\), because correlated variants carry partly the same information and add less evidence.

Pitfall A TWAS hit is not proof of the causal gene

Predicted expression of neighboring genes is correlated whenever their eQTLs are in linkage disequilibrium, and a single causal variant can make several genes in a region significant. A variant can also affect the trait through a path other than the gene’s expression in the chosen tissue. Wainberg and colleagues, a Session 5 reading, showed in simulations and real data that TWAS often prioritizes non-causal genes; fine-mapping and colocalization (Session 4) are used to narrow the candidates.55

Key points
  • Epidemiology studies the distribution and determinants of health in populations; genetic epidemiology adds the genotype, a determinant fixed at conception, assigned at random within families and measured for millions of variants, but correlated with ancestry and with nearby variants.
  • Loss-of-function variants show what a lifetime of lower protein activity does. ANGPTL3 (forward genetics), PCSK9 (reverse genetics) and BCL11A (GWAS) became approved drugs in 2021, 2015 and 2023; genetically supported targets are about 2.6 times as likely to succeed.
  • Screening uses genotypes to predict: rare high-risk variants (BRCA1/BRCA2, Lynch syndrome), newborn screening of metabolites for 29 conditions in 2006 (38 core in 2024), and risk-stratified screening that combines a polygenic score with other factors into an absolute risk, \(r = 1 - \exp(-\Lambda_0 e^{\eta})\).
  • Mendelian randomization estimates a causal effect as the Wald ratio \(\hat{\beta}_{GY}/\hat{\beta}_{GX}\); it needs a strong instrument (\(F \ge 10\)), no link between the variant and the confounders, and no path to the outcome that bypasses the exposure. It showed that HDL cholesterol and CRP are not causes of coronary disease, as the trials did.
  • A TWAS predicts gene expression from genotypes with weights trained in reference data and tests it against a trait; its z-score is \(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z}/\sqrt{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}}\), and its hits point to regions and candidate genes rather than proving the causal gene.

Check your understanding

A UK Biobank analysis of coronary heart disease uses these columns: age, sex, region of the assessment centre, year of diagnosis, LDL cholesterol, smoking, statin use, and the genotype at rs11591147. Which columns describe the distribution of the disease and which are candidate determinants? What sets the genotype apart from the other determinants?

Age, sex, region and calendar year describe the distribution: who gets the disease, where and when, from which prevalence and incidence are computed. LDL cholesterol, smoking, statin use and the genotype are candidate determinants. (Age and sex are determinants too, but they are used here to describe the distribution.)

The genotype was fixed at conception, so the disease cannot have changed it (no reverse causation), and it was assigned at random within the family, so it is not influenced by the person’s later behavior. Statin use, by contrast, is a consequence of high LDL cholesterol and of diagnosis, so its association with heart disease mixes cause and consequence. But the genotype is correlated with ancestry and with neighboring variants, so its association still needs adjustment for ancestry and does not identify the causal variant.

Carriers of a PCSK9 nonsense variant in ARIC had 28% lower LDL cholesterol and 88% fewer coronary events. Assume a typical LDL cholesterol of 3.5 mmol/L and the statin-trial effect of 22% lower risk per 1 mmol/L. What reduction would the trials predict, and why is the observed reduction larger?

28% of 3.5 mmol/L is about 1.0 mmol/L. Statin trials give a rate ratio of 0.78 per mmol/L, so they predict about 22% fewer events, a rate ratio near 0.78. The observed hazard ratio was 0.11.

The main reason is duration: carriers have had lower LDL cholesterol since conception, so less atherosclerosis built up over decades, whereas trial participants were treated for about five years in middle or old age. Mendelian randomization studies of LDL-lowering variants estimate about 54.5% lower risk per mmol/L of lifelong lowering, about three times the trial effect. Part of the gap is also chance: the estimate came from a few events among the 2.6% of carriers, and its 95% interval (0.02–0.81) is wide.

A variant raises an exposure by \(\hat{\beta}_{GX} = 0.10\) units per allele. In an independent study its effect on the log odds of a disease is \(\hat{\beta}_{GY} = 0.03\) with standard error 0.01. Compute the Wald ratio, its approximate standard error and a 95% confidence interval, and express the result as an odds ratio per unit of exposure.

\(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{IV}} = 0.03/0.10 = 0.30\) log odds per unit of exposure. Standard error \(\approx 0.01/0.10 = 0.10\). The 95% interval is \(0.30 \pm 1.96 \times 0.10\), from 0.10 to 0.50.

As odds ratios: \(e^{0.30} = 1.35\) per unit, with interval \(e^{0.10} = 1.11\) to \(e^{0.50} = 1.65\). This is valid only if the variant is a strong instrument, is not linked to confounders of the exposure and the disease, and affects the disease only through the exposure.

A variant explains \(R^2 = 0.1\%\) of an exposure’s variance. What is the first-stage F statistic in 10,000 people? In 5,000? How many people would a variant with \(R^2 = 0.05\%\) need to reach F = 10?

\(F \approx nR^2/(1 - R^2)\). With \(n = 10{,}000\): \(10{,}000 \times 0.001 / 0.999 \approx 10.0\), at the usual threshold. With \(n = 5{,}000\): about 5.0, a weak instrument whose one-sample MR estimate would be biased toward the confounded observational estimate.

For \(R^2 = 0.0005\): \(n \approx 10 \times 0.9995 / 0.0005 \approx 20{,}000\) people. Doubling the variance explained halves the sample needed. This is why MR studies combine many variants and use GWAS of hundreds of thousands of people.

HDL cholesterol is strongly associated with fewer heart attacks in observational data, but the LIPG 396Ser variant, which raises HDL cholesterol by 0.14 mmol/L, does not lower risk. What does this suggest, which assumption would have to fail for MR to be wrong here, and what did the trials show?

It suggests that HDL cholesterol itself does not protect against myocardial infarction: the observational association comes from confounding (people with high HDL differ in diet, exercise, triglycerides and other factors) or from HDL being a marker of something else. The expected odds ratio for carriers was 0.87 (0.84–0.91); the observed was 0.99 (0.88–1.11), so the data exclude the expected effect.

For MR to be wrong, the variant would need an opposite effect through another path (a violation of Assumption 3, for example raising risk through another lipid), or its effect on HDL would need to differ from the effect of raising HDL in other ways. The authors chose a variant that changes no other lipid, and a score of 14 HDL variants gave the same answer, while LDL variants, the positive control, behaved as expected. The trials agreed: dalcetrapib raised HDL cholesterol by 31–40% with a hazard ratio of 1.04, and niacin raised it without reducing events.

Using \(r = 1 - \exp(-\Lambda_0 e^{\eta})\) with \(\Lambda_0 = 0.10\), compute the lifetime risk of a woman whose polygenic score is 1.5 SD above the mean (relative risk \(1.61^{1.5}\)) and whose breast density gives a relative risk of 1.3. Which NICE band is she in, with and without the polygenic score?

\(1.61^{1.5} = e^{1.5 \times 0.476} = e^{0.714} = 2.04\). Combined relative risk \(2.04 \times 1.3 = 2.66\), so \(\eta = \ln 2.66 = 0.98\). Cumulative hazard \(0.10 \times 2.66 = 0.266\), risk \(1 - e^{-0.266} = 23.3\%\): the moderate band (17% to below 30%).

Without the polygenic score her relative risk is 1.3: \(1 - e^{-0.13} = 12.2\%\), near-population risk. The polygenic score changes her category, which is the point of slide 20: information that spreads predicted risks apart moves women across the thresholds.

A newborn screening test flags every baby with a condition that affects 1 in 25,000 births, and also flags 0.05% of healthy babies. In a state with 100,000 births a year, how many positive screens are there, and what share are true? What happens to that share if the false-positive rate is halved?

True positives: \(100{,}000/25{,}000 = 4\). False positives: \(0.0005 \times 99{,}996 \approx 50\). Positive screens: about 54, of which 4 are true, a positive predictive value of about 7%.

Halving the false-positive rate gives 25 false positives and \(4/29 \approx 14\%\). For a rare condition the positive predictive value is limited by the false positives among the many healthy babies, which is why screening programs set cutoffs carefully and confirm every positive screen with a diagnostic test.

A gene’s FUSION model has two variants with weights 0.5 and 0.5, whose GWAS z-scores are 4.0 and 2.0 and whose LD correlation is 0.5. Compute the TWAS z-score. If the neighboring gene shares one of these eQTLs, what can you conclude when both genes are significant?

Numerator: \(0.5 \times 4.0 + 0.5 \times 2.0 = 3.0\). Denominator: \(\sqrt{0.25 + 0.25 + 2 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.5} = \sqrt{0.75} = 0.866\). So \(z_{\mathrm{TWAS}} = 3.0/0.866 = 3.46\). With uncorrelated variants the denominator would be \(\sqrt{0.5}\) and the z-score 4.24.

If both genes are significant because they share an eQTL in LD with the GWAS signal, the TWAS cannot say which gene, if either, carries the effect: the two predicted expressions are correlated across people. The result marks a region and candidate genes; fine-mapping, colocalization and experiments are needed to name the causal gene.
