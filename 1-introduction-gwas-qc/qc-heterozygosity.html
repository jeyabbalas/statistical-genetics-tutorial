QC step 5: heterozygosity

Does anyone carry too many or too few heterozygous genotypes? A person's heterozygosity rate is the share of their called SNPs at which they carry two different alleles. Too many heterozygous calls point to DNA from two people mixed in one sample; too few to related parents or poor DNA; and genetic ancestry that differs from most of the sample moves the rate in either direction. PLINK's --het counts each person's homozygous genotypes at the LD-pruned SNPs, compares the count with the number expected from the allele frequencies, and reports the inbreeding coefficient estimate F. This page explains both statistics, the two rules the practical compares, and what to do with the people they flag.

Command: plink --het Writes: .het, one row per person ≈ 40 min
Mental model

One heterozygosity rate per person, compared with the sample and with its expectation; outliers are explained before anyone is removed

For each person, PLINK counts the homozygous genotypes among the called, pruned SNPs and the number expected from the sample's allele frequencies. The heterozygosity rate is the share of calls that are heterozygous; F is the relative shortfall of heterozygous calls against their expectation. Most people sit in a narrow peak; people in either tail are flagged, the likely cause of each is worked out with missingness, relatedness and genetic ancestry, and then they are removed or kept.

  • Too high is usually technical. Mixed DNA is called heterozygous wherever the two people differ (contamination).
  • Too low is often biological. Children of related parents carry long homozygous stretches (runs of homozygosity); poor DNA can also lose heterozygous calls (what the step finds).
  • F depends on whose allele frequencies are used. People whose genetic ancestry differs from most of the sample can sit in either tail without any error (ancestry).
  • Two rules, two answers. The practical's mean ± 3 SD of the heterozygosity rate adapts to the sample; |F| > 0.1 is fixed, so they flag different people (two rules).
  • Read it next to missingness. A plot of F (or the rate) against the missing-call rate separates contamination from inbreeding (the plots).
Background From Lecture 0 and the earlier steps

Heterozygosity, the expected heterozygosity \(2pq\) of a SNP under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, the inbreeding coefficient \(F\) and PLINK's formula for it are introduced in Lecture 0 · Heterozygosity; pooling groups with different allele frequencies is the Wahlund effect. Per SNP, the same shortfall of heterozygotes is what step 2 tests. This step uses step 1's missing-call rates and the pruned SNP list (.prune.in) of step 3, and it is read together with step 4.

What the step finds Core

At a SNP with allele frequency \(p\), a person drawn from the sample's population is heterozygous with probability \(2pq\), so over tens of thousands of SNPs every person's heterozygosity rate lands close to the average of \(2pq\). Departures from it have a small number of causes:

Why it matters. A contaminated or degraded sample has wrong genotypes at thousands of SNPs. If such samples are more common among cases than controls, or concentrated on plates that hold more cases, the errors become differences between cases and controls and produce false associations. Poor samples can also spoil other samples' calls: software that calls genotypes by clustering a batch of samples together can draw the clusters wrongly when the batch contains poor samples. A quality-control review of four GWAS describes both. In the two genotyped on the Affymetrix 6.0 array, the array PennCATH used, it found 28 and 26 low-quality samples with high heterozygosity, high relatedness to other samples and poor allele-intensity plots; they were spread over many plates, and no false associations were found. It also recounts a study on the Affymetrix 5.0 array in which eight such samples sat on one plate with a disproportionately high number of cases; the false associations they caused remained after the eight were removed and disappeared only when the plate's other samples were re-called.1 Removing people for low heterozygosity caused by related parents or by ancestry, in contrast, removes correct data and makes the sample less diverse.

Contamination: how mixed DNA turns homozygous calls into heterozygous ones

A genotyping array does not see alleles directly. At each SNP it measures two fluorescence intensities, one for each allele, and the calling software converts them into the share of the signal that comes from allele B (Lecture 0's B allele frequency): about 0 for AA, 0.5 for AB and 1 for BB. In a mixture, each person contributes signal in proportion to their share of the DNA. Where person A is AA and person B is BB, a 20% share of B's DNA moves the signal from 0 to about 0.2, between the clusters, where it is called heterozygous or not called at all. The widget simulates this.

What to notice. The left panel shows 300 of the 20,000 simulated SNPs, grouped by person A's true genotype; the right panel the expected rates at every mixing share. Move the slider slowly from 0%. Up to about 10% nothing changes: B's DNA moves the signal of A's homozygous SNPs only a little, and they stay inside the AA or BB band. Between about 10% and 25%, A's homozygous SNPs at which B carries a different allele drift into the gaps between bands and get no call. The missing-call rate rises, and so does the heterozygosity rate, although the last line of the readout shows that no homozygous genotype has yet been called heterozygous: the lost calls are all homozygous, so the heterozygous calls make up a larger share of the calls that remain, and F falls (below −0.1 at 20%). From about 25%, many of those SNPs cross into the AB band and are called heterozygous: the rate reaches about 0.5 near 35% and F about −0.4. Beyond 40%, A's own heterozygous SNPs also leave the AB band, and the sample becomes a mass of no calls. The calling rule here is simplified (real software draws each SNP's clusters from all samples), but the pattern is the one seen in real data: a contaminated sample has a high missing-call rate in step 1, an excess of heterozygous calls here, and, because a heterozygous call is never an opposite homozygote, apparent relatedness to many people in step 4.1,3

The statistic: the heterozygosity rate and F Core

--het works on one person at a time and on autosomal SNPs only. For each person it counts \(N_i\), the SNPs with a genotype call, leaving out SNPs that are monomorphic in the sample; \(O_i\), how many of those calls are homozygous; and \(E_i\), how many homozygous calls the sample's allele frequencies predict.4,5 The practical turns the counts into a rate; PLINK turns them into \(F\).

The heterozygosity rate

\[ h_i = \frac{N_i - O_i}{N_i} \]
\(i\)
A person.
\(N_i\)
PLINK's N(NM): the number of autosomal SNPs at which person \(i\) has a genotype call, among SNPs that are not monomorphic in the sample.
\(O_i\)
PLINK's O(HOM): the number of those calls that are homozygous.
\(h_i\)
The heterozygosity rate, the practical's het_rate: the share of the person's calls that are heterozygous.

\(N_i - O_i\) is the number of heterozygous calls. Dividing by \(N_i\) rather than by the number of SNPs makes people with different numbers of missing calls comparable. The rate itself has no fixed expected value: it depends on which SNPs are used. Over SNPs with common alleles it is around 0.3 to 0.4, because each SNP contributes its own \(2pq\), at most 0.5. That is why the practical judges it against the sample (mean ± 3 SD) rather than against a fixed number.6,7

The expected number of homozygous calls

\[ E_i = \sum_{j \in C_i} \left( 1 - 2\hat{p}_j \hat{q}_j \right) \]
\(C_i\)
The set of SNPs counted in \(N_i\): autosomal, called in person \(i\), not monomorphic.
\(\hat{p}_j, \hat{q}_j\)
Frequencies of the two alleles of SNP \(j\). PLINK estimates them from the founders, the people whose father and mother IDs in the .fam file are both 0 (in PennCATH, everyone), or reads them from a file given with --read-freq.
\(E_i\)
PLINK's E(HOM): the number of homozygous calls expected for person \(i\).

Under Hardy–Weinberg proportions a person is homozygous at SNP \(j\) with probability \(\hat{p}_j^2 + \hat{q}_j^2 = 1 - 2\hat{p}_j\hat{q}_j\); adding these probabilities over the person's called SNPs gives the expected count. Because the sum runs over that person's calls only, a missing call removes the SNP from both the observed and the expected count. The frequencies come from the whole sample, so \(E_i\) is the expectation for a person drawn from the sample's population; for someone whose ancestry differs from most of the sample it can be off (below). By default PLINK omits the small-sample factor \(n/(n-1)\) of Nei's formula; the modifier small-sample restores it.4

F, the inbreeding coefficient estimate

The inbreeding coefficient \(F_i\) of a person is the probability that their two alleles at a position are identical by descent from an ancestor of both parents. On that fraction of the genome the person is homozygous for sure; elsewhere Hardy–Weinberg proportions apply. PLINK estimates \(F_i\) by the method of moments:

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathrm{E}[O_i] = E_i + F_i\,(N_i - E_i) \\[6pt] \hat{F}_i = \frac{O_i - E_i}{N_i - E_i} \end{gathered} \]
\(F_i\)
Person \(i\)'s inbreeding coefficient: 0 for a child of unrelated parents, 1/16 = 0.0625 for a child of first cousins.
\(\mathrm{E}[O_i]\)
Expected number of homozygous calls of a person with inbreeding coefficient \(F_i\).
\(N_i - E_i\)
Expected number of heterozygous calls when \(F_i = 0\): the sum of \(2\hat{p}_j\hat{q}_j\) over the person's called SNPs.
\(\hat{F}_i\)
PLINK's F: the value of \(F_i\) that makes the expected count equal the observed count \(O_i\).

At SNP \(j\) the person is homozygous with probability \(1 - 2\hat{p}_j\hat{q}_j(1 - F_i)\): inbreeding removes a fraction \(F_i\) of the expected heterozygotes. Adding over SNPs gives the first line; solving it for \(F_i\) with \(O_i\) in place of its expectation gives the second. Equivalently, \(\hat{F}_i\) is 1 minus the ratio of observed to expected heterozygous calls, the per-person version of the per-SNP shortfall in step 2. \(\hat{F}_i\) is 0 when the person has as many heterozygous calls as expected, positive with a shortfall and negative with an excess, which a probability cannot be: a clearly negative \(\hat{F}_i\) means something added heterozygous calls (contamination) or the sample's frequencies do not fit the person (ancestry). A worked example: a person with \(N_i = 50{,}000\) calls and \(E_i = 32{,}000\) expects \(18{,}000\) heterozygous calls (\(h = 0.36\)). With \(O_i = 33{,}125\), \(\hat{F}_i = 1{,}125 / 18{,}000 = 0.0625\), the value expected for a child of first cousins, and \(h_i = 16{,}875 / 50{,}000 = 0.3375\). With \(O_i = 30{,}200\), \(\hat{F}_i = -1{,}800 / 18{,}000 = -0.1\) and \(h_i = 0.396\).

How precise is F?

Even for a person whose parents are unrelated and whose DNA is clean, \(\hat{F}_i\) is not exactly 0: whether the person is heterozygous at each SNP is a chance event. If the SNPs are independent, which is what LD pruning approximates, the spread of \(\hat{F}_i\) follows from the variance of a sum of independent yes-or-no outcomes:

\[ \mathrm{SD}(\hat{F}_i) \approx \frac{\sqrt{\sum_j H_j (1 - H_j)}}{\sum_j H_j} \]
\(H_j\)
\(2\hat{p}_j\hat{q}_j\), the probability that the person is heterozygous at SNP \(j\) when \(F_i = 0\).
\(\sum_j\)
Sum over the person's called SNPs.
\(\mathrm{SD}(\hat{F}_i)\)
Standard deviation of \(\hat{F}_i\) across people of the sample's population with \(F_i = 0\).

The number of heterozygous calls has variance \(\sum_j H_j(1 - H_j)\), and \(\hat{F}_i\) is that number divided by \(-\sum_j H_j\), plus a constant. With 50,000 SNPs and \(H_j = 0.36\), \(\mathrm{SD}(\hat{F}_i) \approx \sqrt{11{,}520}/18{,}000 \approx 0.006\). Two things follow. In a large, homogeneous, well-genotyped sample the peak of \(F\) around 0 is narrow, so a child of first cousins (\(F = 0.0625\)) stands out clearly. And with correlated SNPs the variance is larger than this formula says, which is one reason --het runs on the pruned SNPs: PLINK's documentation notes that the calculation does not take LD into account.4

The .het file, column by column

One line per person with a header line; six columns.5,8

FID, IID
Family ID and within-family ID, as in the .fam file.
O(HOM)
\(O_i\): observed number of homozygous genotypes.
E(HOM)
\(E_i\): expected number of homozygous genotypes. PLINK prints it with four significant digits, so a value of 10,000 or more appears in exponential notation: 2.523e+04 means about 25,230.
N(NM)
\(N_i\): number of non-missing, non-monomorphic autosomal genotypes.
F
\(\hat{F}_i = (O_i - E_i)/(N_i - E_i)\), also with four significant digits; nan for a person with no calls. PLINK computes it from the unrounded \(E_i\), so F recomputed from the printed columns can differ in the fourth digit.

The heterozygosity rate is not in the file; compute it as (N(NM) − O(HOM)) / N(NM). In R the column names contain parentheses, so they must be written in backquotes: `N(NM)`.

Genetic ancestry and F Core

\(E_i\) uses the whole sample's allele frequencies. That is right for a person drawn from the same population as most of the sample and can be wrong for others. Two cases show how.

A person from a different population. If the person's population has allele frequency \(p'_j\) at SNP \(j\) while the sample has \(p_j\), the person is heterozygous with probability \(2p'_jq'_j\), but \(E_i\) is computed from \(2p_jq_j\). Over many SNPs, \(\hat{F}_i\) then comes close to \(1 - \sum_j 2p'_jq'_j \big/ \sum_j 2p_jq_j\): positive if the person's population is less heterozygous at these SNPs than the sample's population, negative if it is more. Which one applies depends on the populations and on how the SNPs were chosen, so the tail such a person lands in is not a sign of an error. PLINK's documentation warns that with "a highly imbalanced ancestry distribution (e.g. >90% EUR but a few samples with ancestry primarily from other continents), you may need to process the rare-ancestry samples separately."4

A person whose parents come from two populations. They inherit one allele from each, so their heterozygosity is higher than the pooled frequencies predict:

\[ p_1 q_2 + p_2 q_1 = 2\bar{p}\bar{q} + \tfrac{1}{2}(p_1 - p_2)^2 \]
\(p_1, p_2\)
Frequency of allele 1 at a SNP in the mother's and the father's population; \(q_1 = 1 - p_1\), \(q_2 = 1 - p_2\).
\(p_1q_2 + p_2q_1\)
Probability that the child is heterozygous: allele 1 from the mother and allele 2 from the father, or the reverse.
\(\bar{p}, \bar{q}\)
The pooled frequencies, \(\bar{p} = (p_1 + p_2)/2\) and \(\bar{q} = 1 - \bar{p}\).

The child's expected heterozygosity exceeds the pooled Hardy–Weinberg value by half the squared frequency difference, at every SNP where the populations differ. Against frequencies pooled from both populations, such a person therefore gets a negative \(\hat{F}_i\), the same direction as contamination. This is the mirror image of the Wahlund effect, in which pooling people from two populations produces too few heterozygotes.

The practical makes the same point: F "relies on a specific dataset's allele-frequency reference", so "uncorrected population stratification will distort its distribution", and rules such as ±3 SD are best "evaluated within genetically comparable groups".6 In practice: look at heterozygosity after the principal components of step 6, compute it within groups of genetically similar people, or give --read-freq the allele frequencies of the person's reference population. Two rules, different people Core

The two statistics are closely tied. Dividing the definition of \(\hat{F}_i\) by \(N_i\):

\[ \hat{F}_i = 1 - \frac{h_i}{\bar{H}_i}, \qquad \bar{H}_i = \frac{N_i - E_i}{N_i} \]
\(h_i\)
Person \(i\)'s observed heterozygosity rate.
\(\bar{H}_i\)
The heterozygosity rate expected from the sample's allele frequencies, averaged over the person's called SNPs: the mean of \(2\hat{p}_j\hat{q}_j\).

When everyone is called at nearly the same SNPs, \(\bar{H}_i\) is nearly the same number \(\bar{H}\) for everyone, and \(\hat{F}_i\) is a decreasing straight-line function of \(h_i\): both statistics order people the same way, from most to least heterozygous. The rules differ in where they cut. Mean ± 3 SD of \(h\) corresponds to \(\lvert \hat{F}_i - \bar{F} \rvert > 3\,s_h / \bar{H}\), a cut that depends on the spread \(s_h\) of the sample; |F| > 0.1 is the same in every study. With \(\bar{H} = 0.36\), a homogeneous sample with \(s_h = 0.004\) gets a ±3 SD cut of about 0.033 in \(F\), three times stricter than 0.1, while a sample that mixes several ancestries, with \(s_h = 0.012\), gets 0.1.

Ancestry differencesWiden the SD; a small group can sit in a tailShift F for whole groups

Neither rule is correct in general; they answer different questions. ±3 SD asks "who is unusual in this sample?"; |F| > 0.1 asks "whose heterozygosity is far from what the allele frequencies predict?". The practical's question, how sensitive QC decisions are to the choice of metric and threshold, is answered by running both and looking at the people on which they disagree.6

Choose a threshold and act Core

What the practical does. It flags people more than 3 standard deviations from the mean heterozygosity rate, then shows that |F| > 0.1 (or |F| > 0.2 in some pipelines) flags different people, and notes that fixed cut-offs "are not universal" while dataset-specific rules are "ideally evaluated within genetically comparable groups". The exercise asks you to choose and justify a rule, and accepts that a well-justified analysis may remove no one.6

Explain before removing. For each flagged person, check the other evidence: a high missing-call rate (step 1) or relatedness to many people (step 4) points to contamination or poor DNA, and such samples are removed. A high \(F\) with normal missingness and long runs of homozygosity points to related parents; the genotypes are correct, so many studies keep these people. A position in the tail shared with other people of similar genetic ancestry (step 6 onward) is not an error either; keep them and handle ancestry in the analysis.

Robust limits. Because outliers inflate the standard deviation, some pipelines use the median and the median absolute deviation, or the interquartile range as Laurie et al. did, instead of the mean and SD.

Order of operations. This step reads the fileset after the missingness filters, because missing calls add noise. Read it with step 4: a contaminated sample creates false relatives, and removing it can resolve several flagged pairs at once. The removals are applied together at the end of the practical with --remove (the exercise).

Record. For every flagged person write down the family and individual ID, het_rate, F, the rule that flagged them and the likely cause; the exercise collects these decisions in its QC report.

Runs of homozygosity Optional

A child of related parents can inherit the same ancestral chromosome segment from both of them. Across such a segment every SNP is homozygous: a run of homozygosity (ROH). Short runs are common in everyone, because chance alone makes some stretches homozygous and shared ancient ancestry leaves short identical segments; long runs, several megabases or more, come from recent common ancestors of the parents. The share of the genome in long runs, \(F_{\mathrm{ROH}}\), estimates the inbreeding coefficient and, unlike \(\hat{F}\) from --het, shows where the homozygosity is and how long the segments are, which distinguishes recent from distant shared ancestry.10,11

bash · not part of the practical 
$ plink --bfile ${analysis_dir}/penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01 --homozyg \
      --out ${analysis_dir}/penncath.geno0.01.mind0.01.roh

By default PLINK reports runs of at least 100 SNPs and 1,000 kb, with at least one SNP per 50 kb on average, scanning windows of 50 SNPs that may contain one heterozygous and five missing calls.12 The reports list each run and each person's total length in runs. For a child of first cousins, about 1/16 of the autosomes, roughly 180 megabases, is expected to lie in runs inherited twice from the parents' shared grandparents. ROH also matter beyond QC: a run makes the person homozygous for any recessive variant inside it, and the total length of a person's runs has been studied in relation to many traits.11

What to look at.

  • Size. The file should have one line per person left after step 1, 1,380, plus the header.
  • N(NM). Close to the number of SNPs in .prune.in for everyone, minus each person's missing calls; a person with a much smaller value had many missing calls.
  • The shape of the histogram. One narrow peak is expected for a sample of one broad ancestry; a shoulder or a second peak points to a group of people whose ancestry, or genotyping batch, differs from the rest.

How the real data differ from the toy data. PennCATH has 1,380 people at this point and many more pruned SNPs than the toy data, so each person's \(F\) is estimated more precisely. Its genotypes come from the Affymetrix 6.0 array (The data); the review cited above called that array's genotypes plate by plate, a procedure in which a few poor samples can affect the calls of the others on their plate.1 The 1,401 patients were all recorded as white (The data), and the practical expects more than 99% of them to receive GrafAnc's European assignment in step 8, with a small number assigned to its Middle East and North Africa group.6 These are reference-based measures of genetic similarity, not identities, but they mean that ancestry should spread F only a little; the few people whose genetic ancestry differs from most of the sample are the ones to check before removing anyone from the tails. For comparison, real PLINK 1.9.0's .het file gives a mean heterozygosity rate of 0.2234 with a standard deviation of 0.0023, so the ±3 SD band runs from 0.2164 to 0.2303. Eleven people fall outside it, 9 above and 2 below, and nobody has |F| > 0.1: F runs only from −0.045 to 0.039. On these data the two rules flag entirely different people.

Pitfalls Core

Pitfall Removing people for their ancestry

A small group of people whose genetic ancestry differs from most of the sample can fall beyond ±3 SD, or beyond |F| = 0.1, because the sample's allele frequencies do not fit them. Removing them makes the study less diverse and fixes no error. Check genetic ancestry (steps 6–8) before removing people from the tails, and assess heterozygosity within genetically similar groups.

Pitfall Running --het on all SNPs, or on a tiny sample

Without pruning, correlated SNPs make \(F\) noisier and let a few long LD blocks dominate. With very few people the sample's allele frequencies are poor, and PLINK's documentation calls --read-freq "practically mandatory" in that case.4

Pitfall Reading the two tails the same way

High heterozygosity (negative F) is almost always technical; low heterozygosity (positive F) is often biological. A single rule that removes both tails without looking treats a child of first cousins like a contaminated sample.

Pitfall Forgetting the backquotes

In R, N(NM) - O(HOM) is read as calls to functions named N and O and fails; the column names must be written `N(NM)` and `O(HOM)`.

Key points
  • --het counts, per person, homozygous calls \(O\), expected homozygous calls \(E = \sum (1 - 2pq)\) and non-missing calls \(N\) at pruned autosomal SNPs, and reports \(F = (O - E)/(N - E)\), a method-of-moments estimate of the inbreeding coefficient.
  • The practical's heterozygosity rate is \((N - O)/N\); \(F = 1 - h/\bar{H}\), so the two order people the same way but the rules cut in different places.
  • Excess heterozygosity (negative F) points to contamination or poor DNA, which also raise missingness and create false relatives; a deficit (positive F) to related parents or poor DNA.
  • F depends on the allele frequencies used: people whose genetic ancestry differs from most of the sample can sit in either tail without any error.
  • Mean ± 3 SD adapts to the sample's spread; |F| > 0.1 is fixed. Run both, look at the people they disagree on, and explain each flagged person before removing them.

Check your understanding

A person has N(NM) = 40,000, O(HOM) = 26,000 and E(HOM) = 25,200. Compute het_rate and F. Is the person more or less heterozygous than expected?

het_rate = (40,000 − 26,000) / 40,000 = 0.35. F = (26,000 − 25,200) / (40,000 − 25,200) = 800 / 14,800 ≈ 0.054. The person has 800 more homozygous calls than expected, so they are less heterozygous than expected: 14,000 heterozygous calls against 14,800 expected. An F of about 0.05 is close to the 0.0625 expected for a child of first cousins; whether ±3 SD flags it depends on the sample's spread, while |F| > 0.1 does not.

Why does a contaminated sample get a negative F, and in which other two QC steps would you expect it to stand out?

Wherever the two people in the mixture have different genotypes, the measured signal lies between the clusters and is called heterozygous or not called. More heterozygous calls than the allele frequencies predict make \(O < E\), so F is negative. The no-calls raise the person's missing-call rate (step 1), and because a heterozygous call can never be an opposite homozygote, the sample shares at least one allele with everyone at those SNPs and appears related to many people (step 4).

In a sample of 2,000 people of similar genetic ancestry, the SD of het_rate is 0.003 and the mean expected heterozygosity is 0.35. Which F values correspond to the ±3 SD limits, and which rule is stricter?

From \(\hat{F} = 1 - h/\bar{H}\), a change of 3 SD in \(h\) is a change of \(3 \times 0.003 / 0.35 \approx 0.026\) in \(F\). So ±3 SD flags people whose F differs from the mean F by more than about 0.026, which is much stricter than |F| > 0.1. It would flag, for example, most children of first cousins (F ≈ 0.0625) and mild contamination that |F| > 0.1 misses.

A study is 95% of European genetic ancestry and 5% of African genetic ancestry. Why might the second group's F values be shifted, and what would you do?

PLINK computes the expected homozygous count from the whole sample's allele frequencies, which here are mostly those of the European-ancestry majority. For people whose genetic ancestry differs, those frequencies predict their heterozygosity badly, so their F values shift as a group (the direction depends on the frequency differences and on how diverse their populations are at the array's SNPs). PLINK's documentation warns that such rare-ancestry samples may need to be processed separately. Compute heterozygosity within genetically similar groups, or with --read-freq and suitable reference frequencies, and do not remove people just because their group sits in a tail.

Show that a child whose parents come from two populations with allele frequencies 0.2 and 0.6 at a SNP is heterozygous more often than Hardy–Weinberg proportions for the pooled frequency predict.

The child is heterozygous with probability \(p_1q_2 + p_2q_1 = 0.2 \times 0.4 + 0.6 \times 0.8 = 0.08 + 0.48 = 0.56\). The pooled frequency is \(\bar{p} = 0.4\), so the Hardy–Weinberg value is \(2 \times 0.4 \times 0.6 = 0.48\). The difference, 0.08, equals \(\tfrac{1}{2}(0.2 - 0.6)^2 = 0.08\). Over many such SNPs the child gets a negative F against pooled frequencies, the same direction as contamination.

Why does --het use the pruned SNPs from step 3 rather than all SNPs?

The estimate treats each SNP as independent information. SNPs in strong LD carry the same information several times, so a few LD blocks would count many times, F would be noisier than the formula for its precision suggests, and regions with long-range LD would weigh too much. PLINK's documentation says these calculations do not take LD into account and recommends pruning first.
