Three traditions, one map, one course

Genetic epidemiology joined three traditions, Mendelian and statistical genetics, clinical genetics and molecular genetics, with the methods of epidemiology. Its map has a row of foundations (how variation is inherited and distributed), a row of study designs that link variation to disease, and social factors that shape both. This course follows the GWAS row, from the design of a study to the analyses that come after it, and starts with the quality of the data.

  • The workshop. Nine Wednesday sessions from September to December 2026, each a lecture and a practical, with the readings, slides and code on the workshop website.
  • Where it comes from. Each tradition brought its own data and statistics; Neel (1984) tied the field to multifactorial causation, and slide 39 adds that the same history includes eugenics.
  • The map. Every box has its own question, design, data and statistic: pedigrees and LOD scores for linkage, a genotype matrix and one regression per variant for GWAS, sequences and gene-level tests for rare variants.
  • The course. Every session builds on GWAS; the family-based and molecular boxes are left out (slide 45), and each session highlights the boxes it uses (slides 46–54).
  • From the map to data. Slide 106 turns to the practical: before any association test, the genotypes are checked and cleaned.

Where genetic epidemiology comes from Core

Slide 26 states the theme, “Genetic epidemiology is transdisciplinary”, and slide 27 lists the fields: “The history of genetic epidemiology is a tapestry of observational science, statistical developments, animal and plant breeding experiments, molecular experiments, medicine, epidemiology, technology, and…”. The slide credits the review by Duggal and colleagues (2019), which traces the field from studies of familial aggregation to genomic sequencing.8 Slide 28 groups the people behind this history into three traditions that meet in genetic epidemiology. Each tradition asked a different question and brought its own data and methods:

Garrod showed in 1902 that alkaptonuria, a rare disorder in which urine darkens on standing, is inherited as a recessive trait: the first human disease explained by Mendel’s laws.13 He called such diseases inborn errors of metabolism, and argued that the same chemical individuality explains why people react differently to drugs and foods, the idea later named pharmacogenetics. Neel studied hemoglobinopathies, inherited disorders of hemoglobin: in 1949 he showed that sickle cell anemia is the homozygous form of the sickle cell trait.14 He also led genetic studies of the children of atomic-bomb survivors in Hiroshima and Nagasaki (“radiation” on the slide).Newborn screening for inborn errors and the fetal-hemoglobin story of page 1.

Two quotations on slide 28 come from James Neel’s editorial in the first issue of the journal Genetic Epidemiology (1984).18 The first says why the three traditions needed epidemiology:

“Understanding the function of much that is being revealed will not yield to classical Mendelian genetics but will require the epidemiological approach.”

The second says that genetic epidemiology is inseparable from “the concept of multifactorial causation”: most common diseases arise from many genetic variants and many environmental exposures, each changing risk a little. A single-gene disease can be found by following one trait through a pedigree; a multifactorial disease needs what epidemiology brings, large samples from defined populations, measured exposures and covariates, and attention to confounding. A GWAS is that combination: the regression of epidemiology, applied to each of millions of genetic variants in tens of thousands of people.

Try itA timeline of genetic epidemiology, by tradition

What to notice. The statistical tools came first: correlation, the chi-square test, variance components and path analysis were all in place by 1921, decades before anyone knew that genes are DNA. The molecular lane fills up in the 1940s and 1950s and again after 1990, and each burst changed what genetic epidemiology could measure: markers made linkage studies of human families possible (Huntington disease, 1983; BRCA1, 1990), and arrays and reference panels made GWAS possible (2005–2007). Turn on only the social context lane: eugenics starts in the same decades, and with some of the same people, as the statistics.

“And…”: the rest of the sentence on slide 39

Slide 39 repeats the “tapestry” sentence and completes it in red: the history of genetic epidemiology also includes “prevailing ideas explaining and justifying social hierarchy.” The statistical tradition of slide 28 is the clearest case. Galton coined the word eugenics in 1883 for improving a population by controlling who has children; Pearson and Fisher, whose correlation, chi-square test, variance and maximum likelihood every GWAS still uses, were also advocates of eugenics, and Fisher succeeded Pearson as the Galton Professor of Eugenics at University College London.19 The methods themselves are mathematics and can be checked on their own terms; what the history shaped is which questions were asked, which groups were compared, and how differences between groups were explained. Page 3 follows this thread through slides 40–43, from eugenics to the misuse of genetics research today.

The field map: eight boxes and a frame Core

Slide 29 draws genetic epidemiology as eight boxes in two rows, with a ninth box, social factors, joined to all of them by a bracket. The top row holds the foundations: what is known about how variation is inherited (molecular and Mendelian genetics), whether a trait is influenced by genes at all (familial aggregation and heritability), and how variation is distributed among human populations (population genetics). The bottom row holds the study designs that link genetic variation to disease, roughly in the order they became possible: linkage studies, GWAS, post-GWAS studies and rare variant association studies. Arrows join the boxes within each row and run between the rows in both directions: each design draws on the foundations, and its findings feed back into them.

For a data scientist the boxes differ above all in their data and their test statistic. The table gives, for each box, the question it answers, the design and data it uses, its main statistic and an example; the slide’s own caption is under each name.

The reading draws the line between the GWAS box and the rare-variant box the same way the slide does, by the kind of variant and the technology: GWAS genotype preselected variants with arrays, while whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing aim to capture all variation. “Strictly speaking, both WES and WGS studies are also GWAS, although in the literature ‘GWAS’ mostly refers to genome-wide studies of common variants and is sometimes considered separate from WGS and WES studies.”4

Pitfall “Agnostic” does not mean free of assumptions

Linkage studies and GWAS are called agnostic because they test the whole genome instead of a candidate gene chosen in advance. Every GWAS still rests on choices: who is in the sample, how the trait is defined and measured, which covariates are adjusted for, which genetic model is fitted (usually additive) and which variants are on the array or in the imputation panel. These choices are where the social-factors box acts on the others, and where many of the later QC and analysis steps of this lecture come from.

Check your understanding

A study recruits 300 families in which at least two siblings have early-onset colorectal cancer and genotypes about 400 markers spread over the genome. Another genotypes 600,000 variants in 20,000 unrelated cases and 20,000 controls. Which box of the map does each belong to, and which statistic does each report?

The first is a linkage study: affected relatives share stretches of the genome around a disease variant more often than chance predicts, and the statistic is the LOD score, the base-10 logarithm of the likelihood ratio of linkage against no linkage, with 3 or more as evidence. It works best for rare variants with large effects, such as those in Lynch syndrome genes.

The second is a GWAS: one logistic regression per variant on case status, adjusted for ancestry, with genome-wide significance at a p-value below 5 × 10−8. It works best for common variants with small effects.

Slide 28 joins three traditions. For each, name one kind of data it contributed and one place where that data or method appears in the Session 1 practical.

Mendelian and statistical genetics contributed pedigrees, segregation ratios and population genetics; the practical tests each variant for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (page 16) and checks relatedness between samples (page 18).

Clinical genetics contributed the study of patients with defined diagnoses; PennCATH is a hospital-based study of coronary artery disease in people who had coronary angiography (page 11). Molecular genetics contributed DNA markers and genotyping technology; the practical starts from array genotypes in PLINK files (page 12) and uses the 1000 Genomes reference panel (page 21).

What does Neel mean by “multifactorial causation”, and why does it call for “the epidemiological approach” rather than the methods of classical Mendelian genetics?

Multifactorial causation means that a disease arises from many genetic variants and environmental exposures, each changing risk by a small amount, rather than from one variant that nearly determines the disease. Following one trait through a pedigree, the classical Mendelian method, cannot detect effects that small.

Small effects need large samples from defined populations, measurement of exposures and covariates, and control of confounding: the tools of epidemiology. A GWAS applies them variant by variant, and studies of gene–environment interaction (Session 3) apply them to genes and exposures together.

Slide 45 grays out “Familial aggregation, heritability”, yet Session 5 covers heritability. How can both be true?

The gray box refers to family-based estimates: twin and family studies compare the similarity of relatives of different kinds, for example identical and fraternal twins, to estimate heritability.

Session 5 estimates SNP heritability from GWAS data on unrelated people, the share of trait variance explained by the measured variants together, with methods such as GCTA and LD score regression. It belongs to the Post-GWAS box, which the course covers. SNP heritability is usually lower than twin heritability, because arrays do not capture all genetic variation; the gap is part of the “missing heritability” question.

Which box is highlighted in all nine sessions, which boxes are never highlighted, and which session is the only one to highlight social factors?

GWAS is highlighted in every session (slides 46–54). Mendelian genetics and familial aggregation and heritability never appear. Linkage studies appear only once, in gray, as the predecessor of rare variant studies (Session 7); molecular genetics appears in gray in Session 3 and is highlighted in Session 9, functional genomics.

Social factors are highlighted only in Session 3 (population descriptors and gene–environment interaction), although slides 30–43 argue that they shape every box.

A blog post cites GWAS results to argue that differences in an outcome between two countries are genetic. Which part of the social-factors sentence on slides 29–38 applies, and what does slide 39 add?

The last clause applies: social contexts influence “how those results are interpreted”. A GWAS finds variants associated with a trait within the populations studied; differences between countries also reflect environments, history and who was sampled, and allele frequencies differ between populations for reasons unrelated to the trait. The earlier clauses apply too: which populations were studied, and how the trait was measured.

Slide 39 adds that using genetics to explain and justify social hierarchy is part of the field’s own history, from Galton’s eugenics onward, so arguments of this kind are an old misuse rather than a new finding. Page 3 discusses this.
