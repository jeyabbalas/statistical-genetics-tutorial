Social context: what is studied, in whom, and how results are read

On the field map of slide 29, the box for social factors carries one sentence: “Social and environmental contexts influence genetic associations, including what gets studied, how, in which population, and how those results are interpreted.” Slides 30, 36 and 38 highlight its three parts in turn, and an example follows each: a GWAS of margarine use in UK Biobank, the ancestry of GWAS participants, and the history of eugenics. This page decodes those slides and works through the data and the statistics behind them.

Slides 31–35, 37, 40–43 ≈ 50 min Shows up in the data as: questionnaire answers that record behavior; allele frequencies and trait means that both differ between regions; the ancestry categories of the people in a sample
Mental model

Four places where social context enters a GWAS

A GWAS result is a statement about a sample, a measurement and a model. Social context decides who is in the sample and what the questionnaire records, it can create associations through behavior or through differences between places, and it shapes how a result is read and used.

  • A GWAS of a choice. Alleles at APOE and LPA are associated with using margarine instead of butter, most likely because they raise cholesterol or heart disease risk and people at risk are advised to change their diet.
  • Places differ. When allele frequencies and a trait both differ between regions, SNPs with no effect become associated with the trait, and the excess grows with sample size.
  • Who is in GWAS. Most GWAS participants have been in the European category of the GWAS Catalog, which limits both discovery and the accuracy of predictions in other groups.
  • How results are read. The statistics of genetic epidemiology grew up alongside eugenics, and genetics papers are still misused to argue for social hierarchy.

Margarine versus butter: a GWAS of a choice Core

At the assessment center, UK Biobank asked its participants on a touchscreen, “What type of spread do you mainly use?”, with the hint “If you use more than one type of spread, please select the one that you use the most.”1 The answers are stored as Data-Field 1428, “Spread type”, for 498,506 participants, under Touchscreen › Lifestyle and environment › Diet.2

A GWAS needs a numeric trait, so an analysis of this field first turns the answers into one: for example, 1 for margarine (code 3), 0 for butter (code 1) or for never or rarely using spread (code 0), and missing for “Do not know” and “Prefer not to answer”. Each SNP is then tested with a logistic or linear regression of that 0/1 trait on the genotype, adjusted for age, sex and genetic principal components. The coding is already a choice: whether people who rarely use spread count as 0 changes who is compared with whom. One answer to the second question, “Flora Pro-Active or Benecol”, names spreads sold to lower cholesterol, a first hint of why a cholesterol gene could matter.

The associations are real: the alleles do change the probability of answering “margarine”. But neither gene is known to act on taste or food preference. The path from genotype to answer runs through biology (cholesterol, heart disease) and then through social steps: a blood test, a diagnosis, a doctor’s advice, a decision about what to buy. Lecture 0 gives the biology of the two genes in Association is not causation.

Slide 35 asks that such mechanisms be “concrete & testable”. Writing the proposed chain as a path model makes it testable. If an allele changes margarine use only through LDL cholesterol, its effect on margarine use is a product of two effects:

\[ \beta_{\mathrm{GY}} = \beta_{\mathrm{GL}}\, \beta_{\mathrm{LY}} \]
\(\beta_{\mathrm{GY}}\)
Effect of the variant on the trait: the difference in the probability of answering “margarine”, per copy of the allele. This is what the GWAS estimates.
\(\beta_{\mathrm{GL}}\)
Effect of the variant on LDL cholesterol, in mmol/L per copy of the allele, estimated in a GWAS of LDL cholesterol.
\(\beta_{\mathrm{LY}}\)
Effect of LDL cholesterol on the probability of answering “margarine”, per mmol/L, through diagnosis, treatment and advice. If the chain is right, it is the same for every variant.

Along a chain of causes, effects multiply: one more copy of the allele shifts LDL by \(\beta_{\mathrm{GL}}\), and each unit of LDL shifts the trait by \(\beta_{\mathrm{LY}}\). This is Sewall Wright’s path analysis, and the ratio \(\beta_{\mathrm{GY}} / \beta_{\mathrm{GL}}\) is the Wald ratio of Mendelian randomization. The model makes predictions that can fail. Every variant that changes LDL cholesterol, at APOE or at other genes such as LDLR or PCSK9, should change margarine use in proportion to its effect on LDL. With hypothetical numbers: if a variant raises LDL by 0.1 mmol/L per allele and each mmol/L raises the probability of choosing margarine by 5 percentage points, the variant raises it by 0.5 points per allele, and a variant with twice the LDL effect should show twice the margarine effect. And the association should be concentrated in people who have had a cholesterol test or a diagnosis, and absent in people who have never been told their cholesterol.

Slide 34 draws the first lesson, “Social context matters”: the same allele would probably show little or no association with spread type where cholesterol is rarely measured or dietary advice is different. Slide 35 adds the second, “Hypothesized biological mechanisms should be concrete & testable”, with a cartoon by Sidney Harris that returns on slide 54 (page 2): between a red G (genotype) and a red Y (trait), two scientists stand at a blackboard whose derivation includes the step “Then a miracle occurs”, and one says, “I think you should be more explicit here in step two.” A claimed mechanism that cannot be written as a chain of measurable steps, with predictions like those above, contains such a step.

Story GWAS of what people eat

A GWAS of 85 single foods and 85 dietary patterns from the UK Biobank food-frequency questionnaires found 814 associated loci. Mendelian randomization suggested that the top healthy dietary pattern, driven by eating wholemeal rather than white bread, is causally influenced by factors correlated with education, but is not strongly causal for coronary artery disease or type 2 diabetes.4 Like spread type, a diet trait mixes biology with income, education, health advice and habit, and its genetic associations can run through any of them.

Pitfall “A gene for margarine”

A genome-wide significant association with a behavior is not evidence of a gene that controls the behavior. It says only that the allele changes the probability of the answer, by some path. Before naming a mechanism, ask which steps lie between genotype and answer, which of them are social, and what measurable prediction would show the proposed chain is wrong.

Regions, allele frequencies and false associations Core

Social context can also create associations with no causal path at all. Within one country, allele frequencies differ slightly between regions, because people have tended to have children with partners from nearby, and diet, income, health care and many traits differ between regions too. If the regions of a sample differ in both, every SNP whose frequency differs between them becomes associated with the trait. This is population stratification, a form of confounding introduced in Lecture 0 and treated on page 9 for populations from different continents. Here it acts within one country, where the frequency differences are tiny and the samples are huge.

UK Biobank shows both halves of the problem. Single SNPs and polygenic scores are associated with where participants were born, and the routine adjustment for assessment center and genetic principal components does not account for all of this geographic structure; major health outcomes are geographically structured too, and the coincidence can bias associations.5 In about 450,000 people from Great Britain, 21 of 33 traits showed geographic clustering of their associated alleles after controlling for ancestry. Alleles associated with educational attainment clustered most, with the alleles associated with less education more common in areas of lower socioeconomic status such as coal-mining areas, probably reflecting migration driven by socioeconomic status.6

How much does such structure inflate the test statistics? Take two regions of equal size, a trait whose mean differs between them by \(\delta\) standard deviations for reasons that have nothing to do with the SNPs, and SNPs whose frequencies have drifted apart by \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\). For a SNP with no effect on the trait, the association test statistic then has mean approximately

\[ \E[\chi^2] \approx 1 + \frac{n\, F_{\mathrm{ST}}\, \delta^2}{4 + \delta^2} \]
\(\chi^2\)
The association test statistic of one SNP with no effect on the trait: the squared z- or t-statistic of the regression slope, with 1 degree of freedom. Its mean is 1 when there is no confounding.
\(n\)
Number of people, half in each region.
\(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\)
How far allele frequencies differ between the two regions: for a SNP of average frequency \(p\), the expected squared difference in frequency is \(2 F_{\mathrm{ST}}\, p(1-p)\). It is about 0.004 between two European samples (page 7); the widget below starts at half that.
\(\delta\)
Difference in the mean of the trait between the regions, in units of its standard deviation within a region, caused by anything except the SNPs: diet, income, health care, how the trait was measured.

The genotype and the trait are correlated only through region, so their squared correlation is the product of how strongly each tracks region: about \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) for the genotype and \(\delta^2 / (4 + \delta^2)\) for the trait. The test statistic is about \(n\) times the squared correlation, plus the 1 that chance contributes. Either factor at 0 removes the excess. With \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) and \(\delta\) fixed, the excess grows in proportion to \(n\), so a structure too weak to matter in 5,000 people matters in a biobank. With hypothetical values \(n = 400{,}000\), \(F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.0005\) and \(\delta = 0.1\): \(1 + 400{,}000 \times 0.0005 \times 0.01 / 4.01 \approx 1.50\), so the statistics of SNPs with no effect are inflated by half on average, and SNPs whose frequencies differ more than average can cross genome-wide significance. The formula also reproduces the numbers of page 9’s simulator: \(n = 1{,}000\), \(F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.02\) and \(\delta = 0.5\) give \(1 + 1{,}000 \times 0.02 \times 0.25 / 4.25 \approx 2.2\).

The genomic inflation factor \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) summarizes this inflation from the data: the median of the observed \(\chi^2\) statistics divided by 0.455, the median of a \(\chi^2\) distribution with 1 degree of freedom.7 The widget simulates the two-region model, drawing each region’s allele frequencies from the Balding–Nichols model,8 and tests 1,000 SNPs that have no effect on the trait, with and without region as a covariate.

In real data it rarely is: genetic principal components stand in for it, and in UK Biobank they do not capture all the geographic structure.

Pitfall Adjusting for principal components is not a guarantee

Principal components capture the main axes of genetic variation in a sample. A confounder that follows a finer or a different pattern, such as birthplace within a region or the migration by socioeconomic status described above, can remain (Lecture 0 gives more examples in When PCs are not enough). A QQ plot that follows the diagonal is reassuring for the genome-wide average; it does not show that a particular association is free of confounding, which is one reason GWAS findings are replicated in other samples and checked within families.

Several counts tell the same story. In 2009, 96% of the participants in published GWAS were of European ancestry; by 2016 the share had fallen to 81%, with about 14% of Asian ancestry, 3% of African ancestry and less than 1% from all other groups together.10 A review of 3,639 GWAS published from 2005 to 2018 found 88% European ancestry in 2017, and that 72% of discoveries came from participants recruited in three countries: the United States, the United Kingdom and Iceland.11 Martin and colleagues counted 79% of all GWAS participants as of European descent, against 16% of the world’s population.9 The widget recomputes such shares from the GWAS Catalog’s ancestry file of 2 November 2026.12

In the data How the GWAS Catalog records ancestry

For every study the Catalog’s curators record each sample, initial (discovery) or replication, with the ancestry description that the authors used and a category from a fixed list: the category the authors named or, if they named none, the one that best matches their description. They rely on what the paper states, giving precedence to ancestry inferred with genomic methods such as PCA, and where possible they also record the country of recruitment. “Not reported” marks samples with neither ancestry nor recruitment information.13 A category therefore mixes self-identification, authors’ labels and genetic similarity: “European”, for example, includes people who self-report or are described as European, Caucasian or white, and people who cluster genetically with the 1000 Genomes samples from Europe. The categories describe samples, not distinct human groups. The widget merges them into nine groups close to those of slide 37; its code lists the merges.

The imbalance has measurable costs, set out in Lecture 0’s Diversity in genetic studies. Variants that are common in some populations and rare in others are found mainly where they are common: the PCSK9 nonsense variants that pointed to a new class of cholesterol drugs were found in African Americans (page 1). Fine-mapping depends on linkage disequilibrium, which differs between populations, so samples from several populations can separate variants that one population cannot (page 6). And polygenic scores built from GWAS of samples labeled European predicted 17 traits in UK Biobank on average 1.6-fold less accurately in the groups labeled Hispanic/Latino American and South Asian, 2.0-fold less accurately in the group labeled East Asian and 4.5-fold less accurately in the group labeled African.9

In the reading Uffelmann et al. 2021, on the ethics of GWAS

“Arguably, the primary ethical challenge facing GWAS today relates to issues of diversity and inclusion, ensuring that GWAS result in fair opportunities to promote health and well-being for all humans regardless of race, gender or geographical location.”14 The primer adds that this means making the samples and data of GWAS representative of the global human population and the genomics workforce diverse, and it points to the real-time tracking of diversity in GWAS by the GWAS Diversity Monitor.15

Essentialist theories of “race” treated humans as falling into a few distinct, internally uniform, ranked and unchanging groups, each with a fixed nature. The 2023 National Academies report on population descriptors calls this assumption typological thinking and recommends that genetics research avoid it;16 Lecture 0 tells how Blumenbach came to name Europeans “Caucasian” (Population descriptors). Francis Galton coined the word eugenics in 1883 and defined it as the study of “agencies under social control that may improve or impair the racial qualities of future generations”.17

The policies were real and large. In the United States, eugenicists were crucial to the passage of discriminatory immigration legislation, the Johnson–Reed Act of 1924, which completely excluded immigrants from Asia. Compulsory sterilization laws in 30 states led to the involuntary sterilization of at least 60,000 people by the 1970s.17 In 1927 the U.S. Supreme Court upheld Virginia’s sterilization law in Buck v. Bell, by 8 votes to 1.18 Nazi Germany’s campaign of forced sterilization had at least 400,000 victims, and in Germany, Austria and occupied territories the Nazis killed at least 70,000 adults and 5,200 children under the name of euthanasia.17 The statisticians whose methods every GWAS uses belong to this history: Fisher succeeded Karl Pearson as Galton Professor of Eugenics at University College London, and his promotion of eugenics has led several organizations to remove his name from awards and buildings.19

The misuse is current. The comment on slide 42 opens with a photograph of the memorial to the ten Black people killed by a shooter at a supermarket in Buffalo, New York, in May 2022. Its authors, Carlson and colleagues, describe being “appalled to see a figure from a paper co-authored by one of us (S.R.) displayed in a 180-page screed” that the shooter, an avowed white supremacist, used to justify the massacre, and they note that their own work on global genetic diversity was increasingly being invoked in online discussions of this kind. The pull quote on the slide gives their conclusion: “Geneticists must rethink how they conduct their research and how they communicate results.”20

For an analyst, the history turns into habits that can be checked in every paper and every analysis:

  • Say what the data show. A GWAS estimates associations within the sample studied. Differences in allele frequencies or in average polygenic scores between groups are not, by themselves, evidence that differences in a trait between those groups are genetic: environments differ too, and scores transfer poorly between populations.
  • Name groups by how they were defined. Self-identified race or ethnicity, country of recruitment and genetic similarity to a reference panel are different things (optional section below, and Lecture 0).
  • Avoid language that suggests fixed, ranked types. The reading adds that researchers “must not sensationalize or link their findings to pejorative stereotypes”.14

Consent and data sharing Optional

Social context also decides what may be done with the data. What participants agreed to sets the limits of every later analysis, and the workshop’s own description calls for sharing data, individual-level and summary results, “consistent with participants’ consent and privacy”.22 Two arrangements recur in GWAS:

  • Broad consent. UK Biobank participants agreed to the use of their data and samples for health-related research in general, not for one named study (Lecture 0).
  • Controlled access with data use limitations. Under the NIH Genomic Data Sharing Policy, which applies to NIH-funded research submitted from 25 January 2015, investigators deposit genomic data in a repository such as dbGaP, and other researchers apply for access to the controlled-access data.23 Each dataset, or each consent group within it, carries a data use limitation based on its participants’ informed consent: general research use; health/medical/biomedical research, which excludes the study of population origins or ancestry; research on one disease, for example diabetes and related conditions; or other, customized limits.24

A consent form does not settle every question. Between 1990 and 1992 more than 200 members of the Havasupai Tribe in Arizona gave blood for a study of genetic factors in diabetes. The written consent form described the purpose as studying “the causes of behavioral/medical disorders”, and the samples were later used in studies of schizophrenia, inbreeding and evolutionary genetics, to which tribal members objected. The tribe sued, and in April 2010 the university agreed to pay $700,000 to 41 tribal members and to return the samples.25 What the participants understood they had agreed to mattered as much as the wording of the form.

Key points
  • A GWAS of a questionnaire answer finds every path from genotype to answer: alleles at APOE and LPA raise margarine use, most likely because they raise cholesterol or heart disease risk and people at risk are advised to change their diet.
  • A proposed mechanism becomes testable when written as a chain of measurable effects: if margarine use follows LDL cholesterol, every LDL variant should affect it in proportion to its effect on LDL.
  • When allele frequencies and a trait both differ between regions, SNPs with no effect become associated with the trait. The mean test statistic is about \(1 + n F_{\mathrm{ST}} \delta^2 / (4 + \delta^2)\), so biobanks are sensitive to tiny differences, and principal components do not remove all of them.
  • Most GWAS participants have been in the European category of the GWAS Catalog (86% of those with a reported category from 2005 to 2026, counted per publication), which limits discovery, fine-mapping and the accuracy of polygenic scores elsewhere.
  • The statistics of the field grew up alongside eugenics, which led to forced sterilization and discriminatory immigration laws. Genetics papers are still misused, and careful descriptors and plain statements of what the data show are part of the analysis.

Check your understanding

A colleague reports a genome-wide significant SNP near APOE for “uses margarine” in UK Biobank and proposes that the variant changes taste perception. Give two testable predictions of the explanation on slides 32–33, and say what result would refute it.

If the association runs through LDL cholesterol and the advice that follows a diagnosis, then (1) other variants that change LDL cholesterol, at genes such as LDLR or PCSK9, should also be associated with margarine use, in proportion to their effects on LDL: the ratio of the margarine effect to the LDL effect should be about the same for all of them; and (2) the association should be stronger in people who have had a cholesterol test, a diagnosis or a statin prescription, and weak or absent in people who have never been told their cholesterol.

If LDL variants elsewhere in the genome showed no association with spread type, or the APOE association were just as strong in people never tested or treated, the chain would be refuted, and the taste hypothesis would deserve a direct test.

Why would the same APOE allele probably show no association with spread type in a population where cholesterol is rarely measured?

The allele raises LDL cholesterol everywhere, but the step from high LDL to margarine needs a measurement, a diagnosis and advice. Without them, people with high LDL have no reason to change spread, so \(\beta_{\mathrm{LY}}\) in the chain \(\beta_{\mathrm{GY}} = \beta_{\mathrm{GL}}\, \beta_{\mathrm{LY}}\) is near 0, and so is the association. The genetic effect on the trait depends on the social context: the first take-home message of slide 34.

A study of 100,000 people from two regions finds \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}} = 1.20\). The regions differ in mean trait by 0.2 standard deviations. Roughly what \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) between them would explain this, and what would you do next?

Setting \(1 + n F_{\mathrm{ST}} \delta^2 / (4 + \delta^2) = 1.20\) with \(n = 100{,}000\) and \(\delta = 0.2\) gives \(F_{\mathrm{ST}} = 0.20 \times 4.04 / (100{,}000 \times 0.04) \approx 0.0002\), treating \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) as close to the mean statistic. A twentieth of the \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\) between two European samples is enough at this sample size.

Next: add region, or genetic principal components that capture it, as covariates and check whether \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) falls; use LD score regression to separate confounding from many true small effects, which also inflate \(\lambda_{\mathrm{GC}}\) in large samples; and replicate the top findings in another sample or within families.

In the widget, why do the blue points (adjusted for region) stay on the diagonal however large \(F_{\mathrm{ST}}\), \(\delta\) and \(n\) become, and why is this more optimistic than real data?

In the simulation the only link between genotype and trait is region, and region is known exactly and included in the model. Within each region the SNPs have no association with the trait, so the adjusted statistics follow their null distribution.

In real data the confounder is not measured directly. Principal components or assessment center stand in for it, and they capture only the main patterns; finer structure, such as birthplace within a region or migration by socioeconomic status, can remain, as found in UK Biobank.

The widget gives very different totals and European shares for 2018 per study and per publication. Why, and which rule would you use to describe who takes part in GWAS?

Per study counts the participants of every study accession. In 2018 one paper entered 1,403 traits analyzed in the same UK Biobank participants as 1,403 studies; it alone accounts for four-fifths of that year’s per-study total of about 700 million, almost all in the European category. Per publication counts the largest sample in each category once per paper, which removes that repetition, though the same cohorts still appear in many papers.

Neither rule counts distinct people. To describe participation, the per-publication rule is closer to the question, and every rule shows a large European majority.

A news story says that a polygenic score for educational attainment is higher on average in one country than in another, “proving” a genetic difference in education. Using this page, give three reasons why the conclusion does not follow.

First, polygenic scores are built from GWAS of particular samples, mostly in the European category, and transfer poorly: accuracy drops several-fold in other ancestry groups, and average scores can differ between populations for reasons unrelated to the trait, because allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium differ.

Second, GWAS of social outcomes pick up geographic structure and migration by socioeconomic status, which principal components do not fully remove (the UK Biobank studies above). Third, education depends on schools, income and history, which differ between countries; a GWAS estimates associations within a sample and says nothing directly about why groups differ. Slides 40–42 show that claims of this kind have a long history of being used to justify social hierarchy.
