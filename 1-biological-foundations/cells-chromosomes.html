Part A · The biological system · Page 1 of 13

Cells, chromosomes, and the genome

UK Biobank genotypes, which record the DNA sequence each participant carries at selected positions, were measured on DNA taken from blood1. This page builds the physical picture behind such data: where DNA sits in the body, how it is divided into 46 chromosomes, how places on a chromosome are named, how the X and Y chromosomes differ, and which cells pass DNA on to children.

Core for Lecture 2 ≈ 25 min Shows up in the data as: chromosome codes 1–22, X, Y, XY, MT
Mental model

A zoom ladder, and two copies of 23 DNA molecules

Almost every cell that has a nucleus carries the same genome: 46 chromosomes, which are two copies of 23 different DNA molecules, one set inherited from the mother and one from the father.

  • Same genome in (almost) every cell. DNA from a blood sample gives the inherited DNA sequence of the whole body. The exceptions are changes that arise in body cells during life.
  • Two copies of each numbered chromosome. A person therefore carries two versions of the sequence at every position on chromosomes 1–22, which is why a genotype is coded 0, 1 or 2.
  • Chromosomes have numbers, arms and bands. A band name such as 12q24.12 and a coordinate such as chr12:111,803,962 both give a location.
  • X and Y are different. Males carry one X, so genotypes on the X need their own coding and quality checks.
  • Germline versus somatic. Only DNA in eggs and sperm passes to children; changes that arise in other body cells stay in the body that made them.

Cells and the nucleus Core

A cell is a compartment enclosed by a thin membrane and filled with water, salts and large molecules; it is the smallest part of the body that can grow and divide. Estimates for an adult body range from about 28 to 37 trillion cells (2.8–3.7 × 1013), depending on the study and on body size2,3,4. Cells that work together form a tissue, such as blood, liver or muscle. Cells also come in many cell types, such as liver cells, white blood cells and nerve cells, which look and behave differently even though they carry the same DNA (the last section explains how).

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the molecule that carries inherited information. It is a long chain built from four kinds of units, written A, C, G and T; the next page describes it in detail. DNA length is counted in base pairs (bp), one per position along the molecule: 1 kb is 1,000 bp, 1 Mb is one million bp and 1 Gb is one billion bp.

In human cells almost all DNA sits in the nucleus, a compartment inside the cell with its own membrane, typically 2–10 µm across5. Everything inside the cell membrane but outside the nucleus is the cytoplasm. The cytoplasm contains mitochondria (singular: mitochondrion), small compartments that produce most of the cell's usable chemical energy. Each mitochondrion holds its own small ring of DNA, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), which is present in hundreds to thousands of copies per cell6,7.

Not every cell has a nucleus. Red blood cells, 7–8 µm discs that carry oxygen, lose their nucleus as they mature8. They are the most numerous cells in the body, about 84% of all cells by count; together with platelets (cell fragments that help blood clot) they make almost 90% of the body's cells nucleus-free, leaving about 3 trillion cells with a nucleus2. So when geneticists say that "every cell" carries the genome, they mean every cell that has a nucleus.

In the data Why genotyping DNA comes from white blood cells

UK Biobank extracted DNA from the buffy coat, the thin layer of white blood cells and platelets that forms between plasma and red cells when a tube of blood is spun1. White blood cells keep their nucleus, so they supply the chromosomes; red cells supply none. About 3% of participants could not be genotyped because too little DNA was extracted9. The page From a blood sample to genotypes follows this step in detail.

The human genome by the numbers Core

A genome is the complete DNA of an organism. For humans it usually means the DNA in the nucleus: one set of 23 different chromosomes, plus the small mtDNA. Each chromosome is one long DNA molecule together with the proteins that fold it (proteins are large molecules made by cells; the page Genes and gene expression explains how). Chromosomes 1–22 are autosomes, present in the same form in both sexes; the X and Y chromosomes are the sex chromosomes.

Genotype positions are given relative to a reference genome, a standard sequence for one set of chromosomes. The current reference, GRCh38, has about 3.1 billion base pairs10. UK Biobank measured its genotypes with a genotyping array, a chip that tests about 800,000 chosen positions in each DNA sample, and reports their positions on the previous version, GRCh37; the same base usually has a different position number in the two versions9,11. The newer gap-free reference sequence T2T-CHM13 has 3.055 billion without the Y chromosome, and 3.117 billion with a complete Y added12,13. A cell with two sets therefore holds about 6 billion base pairs: from GRCh38 lengths, 6.06 billion in a cell with two X chromosomes and 5.96 billion in a cell with an X and a Y. At 0.34 nm per base pair, that is about 2 m of DNA in each nucleus14,15.

The autosomes are numbered roughly by size, longest first. The numbers were fixed in 1960 from chromosomes measured under the microscope, four decades before the DNA was sequenced16,17. The DNA sequence shows a few pairs out of order: chromosome 21 (46.7 Mb) is the shortest autosome, shorter than chromosome 22 (50.8 Mb)10,18; in GRCh38, chromosome 11 is slightly longer than 10, and 20 is longer than 19. Chromosome 1 is the longest, at 249.0 Mb. The X chromosome (156.0 Mb) is mid-sized. The Y is small (57.2 Mb in GRCh38), and GRCh38 lacks more than half of its sequence; the complete Y sequence published in 2023 is 62.5 Mb19.

In the data Every variant belongs to one chromosome

Genotype files list variants, positions in the genome where people's DNA sequences differ, and record the chromosome of each. PLINK, the software used in the Session 1 practical that accompanies Lecture 2, codes the human chromosomes as 1–22, then X = 23, Y = 24, XY = 25 (the pseudoautosomal regions explained below) and MT = 26 (mtDNA)23. UK Biobank supplies its array genotypes as one PLINK fileset per chromosome, including X, Y, XY and MT, with positions on GRCh379,24. Its marker files list 18,857 array variants on X, 691 on Y, 1,357 in the XY regions and 265 on MT. The first line of each file shows the codes in the first column; the last two columns give the two versions of the sequence seen at that variant, its alleles (the third column, a genetic-map position, is left at 0):

In a genotype matrix (rows are people, columns are variants), every column therefore belongs to exactly one chromosome, and most analyses are run one chromosome at a time.

Two sets: diploid body cells, haploid eggs and sperm Core

Body cells are diploid: they carry two complete sets of chromosomes, 2 × 23 = 46. One set came from the mother and one from the father. The two copies of the same chromosome, for example the maternal and the paternal chromosome 12, are homologous chromosomes: they have nearly the same length and the same genes in the same order, but their sequences differ at some positions.

Eggs and sperm, the gametes, are haploid: they carry one set of 23 chromosomes. They are made by meiosis, a pair of cell divisions that halves the chromosome number (described on the page Inheritance). At fertilization an egg and a sperm fuse into a zygote, a single diploid cell with 46 chromosomes. The zygote then divides again and again by mitosis, in which a cell copies all of its DNA and splits into two cells with identical chromosomes. Every body cell descends from the zygote in this way, so every body cell that has a nucleus carries the same two sets.

This is where genotype data come from. At a position where people's DNA differs, each person carries two versions of the sequence, one on each homologous chromosome. The versions are called alleles, and the pair a person carries is their genotype. Counting how many copies of one chosen allele a person carries gives the codes 0, 1 or 2 used in genotype files. The page Genetic variation develops this.

Karyotypes and the anatomy of a chromosome Core

A karyotype is a person's set of chromosomes, and also the picture used to show it: chromosomes photographed in a dividing cell, then arranged in pairs by number. A typical karyotype is written 46,XX or 46,XY. Chromosomes appear as separate, compact bodies like this only during cell division; the next section shows what they look like the rest of the time.

Each chromosome has a centromere, a narrowed region where the fibers that separate chromosomes during cell division attach25. The centromere divides the chromosome into two arms: the short arm, called p, and the long arm, called q. The two ends of each chromosome are telomeres, long runs of the repeated sequence TTAGGG26 that become slightly shorter each time a cell divides27. UK Biobank measured telomere length in the white blood cells of about 474,000 participants28.

Staining chromosomes with Giemsa dye (G-banding) produces a fixed pattern of light and dark cytogenetic bands; light bands are richer in genes than dark ones29. A standard drawing of the band pattern is called an ideogram. A band name has four parts: the chromosome, the arm, a region number and a band number, sometimes followed by a decimal point and sub-band digits. Numbers increase with distance from the centromere30. The name is read digit by digit: 12q24.12 is "twelve q two four point one two".

Band names and base-pair coordinates are two ways of giving a location. The variant rs671, in the gene ALDH2, is a major cause of the alcohol flush reaction; Lecture 2 slide 68 writes it as chr12:111803962:G:A. It lies in band 12q24.12, at base pair 111,803,962 of chromosome 12 in GRCh3831,32,33. A band spans hundreds of thousands to millions of base pairs (12q24.12 spans 0.6 Mb), whereas a coordinate names one base pair.

Common confusion Homologous chromosomes are not sister chromatids

Homologous chromosomes are the maternal and the paternal version of a chromosome; their sequences differ at many positions. Sister chromatids are the two identical copies of one chromosome made when a cell copies its DNA before dividing; they stay joined at the centromere until the cell splits. In a karyotype, each chromosome you see is a pair of sister chromatids, and its homolog lies beside it.

How 2 m of DNA fits in a nucleus Optional

Each nucleus holds about 2 m of DNA. It fits because the DNA is wound onto proteins. Histones are small proteins that bind DNA. Eight histones form a core, and 147 bp of DNA wraps almost twice (about 1.7 times) around it; this unit is a nucleosome34,35,36. Neighboring nucleosomes are separated by 10–80 bp of linker DNA, which gives a string about 11 nm wide37,38. DNA together with its histones and other bound proteins is called chromatin.

In test-tube studies, strings of nucleosomes fold further into fibers about 30 nm wide; inside nuclei, most studies find less regular folding39. For most of a cell's life, between divisions, each chromosome is unwound but stays within its own region of the nucleus, a chromosome territory40. Before a cell divides, it copies its DNA and condenses each chromosome into two sister chromatids joined at the centromere, about 1.4 µm wide38. That condensed form is what a karyotype shows.

Packing also affects which genes can be used: chromatin is less tightly packed where DNA is being copied or where genes are in use, and chemical marks on histones differ between genes that are in use and genes that are not29,41. The page Genes and gene expression returns to this.

Sex chromosomes: X, Y and the pseudoautosomal regions Core

Typically, females have two X chromosomes (XX) and males have one X and one Y (XY)42. The X chromosome carries an estimated 900 to 1,400 protein-coding genes with many different roles in the body46. The Y chromosome carries SRY, the gene that directs development as male43. Because a male has a single X, he has only one copy of most X-chromosome genes: he is hemizygous for them, and at each X variant outside the regions described next he carries one allele, not two.

Short stretches at both tips of X and Y have the same sequence on both chromosomes. These pseudoautosomal regions are PAR1, at the tips of the short arms (2.77 Mb), and PAR2, at the tips of the long arms (0.33 Mb)44. They pair and exchange DNA between X and Y when sperm are made, so variants in them are not inherited in a strictly sex-linked way, and men carry two copies of them, as for an autosome43,45.

Female cells have two X chromosomes but use mainly one. Early in embryonic development each cell silences one of its two X chromosomes, chosen at random, and all cells descending from it keep the same choice. This is X-chromosome inactivation46. The silenced X is coated by RNA (a molecule copied from a gene's DNA sequence) made from the gene XIST, and it condenses into a compact body29. As a result, female tissues are made of patches of cells that use the mother's X and patches that use the father's X. About 15% of X genes escape inactivation to some degree47.

Genotyping measures DNA, and both X chromosomes are present in the DNA of every female cell, so X-inactivation does not change genotype calls; it matters for measurements of gene activity. Hemizygosity, on the other hand, matters directly: outside the PARs, a male should have essentially no heterozygous calls on the X, that is, calls with two different alleles. Quality-control pipelines use this to check each sample's sex.

In the data Sex checks and the sex-chromosome fields in UK Biobank

PLINK's --check-sex computes an inbreeding coefficient F for each sample from its X-chromosome genotypes; F measures how far the sample's heterozygous calls fall below the number expected for two X copies (F near 1 means almost none). By default F < 0.2 gives a female call and F > 0.8 a male call, after the PARs have been split off with --split-x48. UK Biobank's genotype calling treated chromosomes 1–22 and the XY regions as diploid (two copies), Y and MT as haploid (one copy), and X as diploid in females and haploid in males49.

UK Biobank provides Field 31 (sex taken from the NHS central registry at recruitment, in some cases updated by the participant), Field 22001 (genetic sex, inferred from the genotypes) and Field 22019 (sex-chromosome aneuploidy, meaning an unusual number of sex chromosomes such as XXY). Bycroft et al. found 378 participants whose genetic sex differed from the recorded sex and 652 with a likely sex-chromosome aneuploidy11,50. A mismatch flags a sample for checking: possible explanations include a sample mix-up, an aneuploidy, or a difference between recorded sex and genetic sex.

Germline and somatic cells, and mosaicism Core

The germline is the line of cells that produces eggs or sperm; only DNA in the germline passes to children51. All other cells of the body are somatic cells52.

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence. A somatic mutation arises in a body cell after fertilization. It is copied into every descendant of that cell and into no other cell, and it is not passed to children53. A mutation present in an egg or a sperm, in contrast, is in the zygote and therefore in every cell of the child.

When the cells of one body do not all have the same genome, the person shows mosaicism: a mixture of cells that differ by a variant, or by a gained or lost chromosome or chromosome segment54. Mosaicism is easy to find in blood. Blood cells are made continuously by hematopoietic stem cells, cells that divide both to renew themselves and to produce every type of blood cell. An estimated 50,000–200,000 of them are making white blood cells at any one time55, and each gains about 17 new mutations per year of life56. If one stem cell acquires a mutation that lets its descendants outgrow their neighbors, those descendants, a clone, make up a growing fraction of the blood. This is clonal hematopoiesis.

Clonal hematopoiesis becomes common with age. In DNA sequencing data from 17,182 people, clonal mutations in genes known from blood cancers were rare before age 40 but were found in 9.5% of people aged 70–79, 11.7% of those aged 80–89 and 18.4% of those aged 90–108; people with such a clone had a higher risk of blood cancer and of coronary heart disease57. In men, the most common clonal change in blood is mosaic loss of the Y chromosome: about 20% of the men in UK Biobank have detectable loss of Y58.

The fraction of DNA copies in a sample that carry a variant is its variant allele fraction (VAF). An inherited variant on one of the two copies has a VAF of 50% in every tissue. A somatic variant carried by a clone of 8 out of 40 white blood cells has a VAF of 10% in blood and is absent from other tissues. Tumors are the extreme case: cancer cells carry many somatic mutations, which are found by comparing tumor DNA with normal DNA from the same person29.

In the data Mosaicism in UK Biobank array data

Mosaic changes can be detected in the same genotyping-array data used for association studies, from the raw signal intensities measured for each allele. Loh et al. found 8,342 mosaic chromosomal alterations (gained, lost or otherwise altered chromosome segments, 50 kb to 249 Mb long, present in a fraction of cells) in blood DNA from 151,202 UK Biobank participants59; Thompson et al. measured loss of Y in 205,011 men58. Standard genotype calling assumes 0, 1 or 2 copies of an allele in every cell, so mosaic changes need separate methods.

One genome, many cell types Core

If nearly all cells carry the same DNA, why does a liver cell differ from a white blood cell? Because cells use different subsets of their genes. To use a gene, a cell copies the gene's DNA sequence into RNA and, for protein-coding genes, uses that RNA to make a protein; this is gene expression, the topic of the next pages. The Human Protein Atlas, for example, classifies the albumin gene ALB as liver-enriched, the insulin gene INS as pancreas-enriched and the β-globin gene HBB as enriched in bone marrow, where red blood cells are made, while the beta-actin gene ACTB is used across tissues60.

This matters later in the course. Studies of gene expression, such as expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) studies and transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) in Session 5, measure RNA in a particular tissue, and a variant can change a gene's activity only in tissues that use the gene. Blood DNA gives the genotype of every tissue; blood RNA describes only blood.

Key points
  • Almost every cell with a nucleus carries the same genome: 46 chromosomes, which are two copies of 23 DNA molecules, one set from each parent. Red blood cells have no nucleus, so genotyping uses DNA from white blood cells.
  • GRCh38 has about 3.1 billion bp per set of chromosomes (about 6 billion per body cell). Chromosome 1 is longest (249.0 Mb), 21 shortest (46.7 Mb); X is 156.0 Mb and Y 57.2 Mb; mtDNA is 16,569 bp and inherited from the mother.
  • Two homologous copies of each autosome mean that a person carries two alleles at each variant, so a genotype is coded as 0, 1 or 2 copies of one chosen allele.
  • Locations are given as band names (12q24.12: chromosome, arm, region, band, sub-band) or as coordinates (chr12:111,803,962 in GRCh38).
  • Males are hemizygous for the X outside PAR1 and PAR2, so they should have no heterozygous X calls there; sex checks rely on this. PLINK codes X, Y, XY and MT as 23–26.
  • Somatic mutations arise during life and are not inherited. Clones carrying them, such as in clonal hematopoiesis or mosaic loss of Y, become common with age and can be seen in blood DNA.
  • Cell types share one genome but use different genes, so tissue matters for expression studies.

Check your understanding

A participant's DNA was extracted from blood. Why can it be used to study a variant that acts in the liver?

Because every cell with a nucleus descends from the same zygote and carries the same inherited genome; the white blood cells in the sample carry the same inherited alleles as the liver cells. The exceptions are somatic changes, which can differ between tissues.

How many copies of chromosome 12 does a typical body cell have, and where did each come from?

Two: one from the mother (through the egg) and one from the father (through the sperm). They are homologous chromosomes, so they carry the same genes but differ at some positions.

Read the band name 8q24.21 aloud and say what each part means.

"Eight q two four point two one": chromosome 8, long arm (q), region 2, band 4, sub-band 21. The region and band numbers increase with distance from the centromere.

Chromosome 21 is shorter than chromosome 22. Why is it numbered 21?

The numbers were assigned in 1960 from sizes measured under the microscope. Chromosome 21 later turned out to be slightly shorter than chromosome 22 (46.7 Mb versus 50.8 Mb in GRCh38), but the numbering was kept.

A sample recorded as male has many heterozygous calls at X SNPs outside the pseudoautosomal regions. What could explain this?

A male has one X, so outside the PARs he should have essentially no heterozygous calls. Possible explanations are a sample mix-up (the DNA is from a female), contamination with another person's DNA, or a sex-chromosome aneuploidy such as XXY.

A variant is found in 6% of the DNA copies from a person's blood, but not in a cheek swab and not in either parent. What kind of variant is it most likely to be?

A somatic variant carried by a clone of blood cells (clonal hematopoiesis), not an inherited one. An inherited heterozygous variant would have a VAF near 50% in every tissue and would come from a parent; here about 12% of blood cells carry it on one copy (6% of copies).
