UK Biobank: who is in it and how they were recruited

UK Biobank is the data set you will use most in this course. It is a prospective cohort study: a group of people who were measured once at the start and whose health has been followed ever since. About half a million adults in England, Scotland and Wales were measured in depth between 2006 and 2010, gave blood and urine, and agreed to be followed through their health records. This page explains how the study was built, so that you know whom each row of the data represents and what each column can and cannot tell you.

Mental model

A prospective cohort: measure once in depth, store samples, follow for decades

UK Biobank measured about 500,000 people aged 40–69 once, in depth, in 2006–2010, stored their blood and urine, and has followed their health ever since through national records; every later visit, questionnaire or assay of the stored samples adds new columns to the same rows.

  • Design. A prospective cohort: exposures were measured before most outcomes happened, so new cases can be counted. The PennCATH data in the Session 1 practical, which accompanies Lecture 2, come from a case–control design instead.
  • Recruitment. 9.2 million invitation letters, 503,317 participants (5.45%), 22 assessment centres, 2006–2010.
  • The visit. About 2.5 hours of consent, questionnaires, interview, measurements and samples. It fills instance 0 of every baseline Data-Field.
  • Samples and follow-up. About 15 million stored aliquots, later visits and linked hospital, cancer and death records keep adding data: genotypes, sequences, metabolites and proteins from the samples, images from the imaging visits, diagnoses from the records.
  • Who is in it. Participants are healthier and wealthier than the people they were drawn from. Prevalence and incidence are not representative; many associations still generalize.

In the genotype–phenotype data matrix (second site-wide model), this page is about the rows: who they are, how they were chosen, and when each phenotype column was measured.

Epidemiology in five ideas Core

Epidemiology is the study of how health-related states are distributed in populations and what determines them, applied to controlling health problems. Genetic epidemiology adds genetic variation to the list of determinants. Five ideas from epidemiology explain most of how UK Biobank was designed and how its data should be analyzed.

Lecture 2 Slide 11 · What epidemiology is

In Lecture 2 (slide 11) you will see two definitions. Epidemiology is “the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states in specified populations, and the application of this study to control health problems”; genetic epidemiology studies genetic factors “in families and in populations” and their interplay “with social and environmental factors”. The words map onto the ideas below: a specified population (idea 1), determinants or exposures and health-related states or outcomes (idea 2). Listen for “specified”: UK Biobank is a specified group of 503,317 volunteers, not the UK population.

1. Target population, sampling frame, sample

The target population is the group a study wants to learn about, for example UK adults aged 40–69. The sample is the people actually studied. Between them sits the sampling frame: the list from which people are invited. UK Biobank’s frame was the registration records of the National Health Service (NHS), the publicly funded health service, for people aged 40–69 who lived within about 25 miles (40 km) of an assessment centre.2 About 98% of the UK population is registered with the NHS, so the frame covered almost everyone in those areas.1 The sample is the 5.45% of invitees who said yes. Results describe the target population only to the extent that the sample resembles it. (In genetics, “population” has a second meaning, a group of people who mostly have children with one another; see Populations and samples.)

2. Exposure and outcome

An exposure is any factor whose effect is being studied: a behavior such as smoking, a measurement such as LDL cholesterol, a treatment, or a genotype. An outcome is the health-related state the exposure might affect: a diagnosis, a death, a measured trait. In a GWAS each variant is in turn the exposure and the phenotype is the outcome. Lecture 2 uses both words for Mendelian randomization (slide 22), and “outcome” again for TWAS (slide 25).

3. Three study designs

Study designs differ in how they choose people and in when exposure and outcome are measured relative to each other:

  • A cross-sectional study measures exposure and outcome at the same time. It shows how common a condition is now, but not which came first.
  • A case–control study selects people because they have the outcome (cases) or do not (controls) and compares their past exposures. It is efficient for rare diseases, because every case is used, but the design fixes the share of cases, so it cannot estimate how common the disease is. Its natural effect measure is the odds ratio.
  • A cohort study enrolls people without regard to the outcome, measures their exposures, and counts outcomes as they occur. In a prospective cohort study the exposures are measured first, at the baseline assessment, and outcomes are recorded afterwards, during follow-up. In a retrospective cohort study both are reconstructed from records that already exist.
Lecture 2 Slide 8 · The practical uses case–control data

In Lecture 2 (slide 8) you will see the Session 1 practical: quality control of GWAS data with PLINK. Its data come from the PennCATH study of coronary artery disease at the University of Pennsylvania Medical Center: 1,401 people genotyped at 861,473 markers.28,29 PennCATH recruited 3,850 people in 1998–2003 and chose, for genome-wide genotyping, a case–control sample from among them: people the study describes as of European ancestry, with severe coronary artery disease on angiography (an X-ray image of the heart’s arteries) as cases, and people with normal angiograms as controls.28 Listen for what follows from that design: case status is a column fixed by the study, and some quality-control checks, such as comparing missing genotypes between cases and controls, only make sense in case–control data. In UK Biobank, a cohort, who becomes a case is decided by follow-up.

Common confusion A cohort is cross-sectional at baseline

Everything measured at the baseline visit was measured at the same moment. An association between two baseline variables, for example diet and a diagnosis the participant already had, is cross-sectional and can run in either direction: people told before the visit that they had high cholesterol may already have changed their diet. Only outcomes that occur after the visit give a cohort its time order. Lecture 2’s margarine example (see the visit section) is a case of this.

4. Prevalence and incidence

Prevalence is the proportion of people who have a condition at a given time. Incidence counts new cases among people who did not have the condition. The incidence rate divides new cases by person-time, the total time people were followed while at risk; cumulative incidence (risk) divides new cases by the number of people at risk at the start of a period.

prevalence = people with the condition now ÷ people in the group now
incidence rate = new cases ÷ person-years at risk
risk over t years = new cases within t years ÷ people at risk at the start

Illustrative numbers If 1,000 people without heart disease are followed for 10 years and 30 have a first heart attack, the 10-year risk is 30 / 1,000 = 3%. Each of the 30 stops contributing person-time at their event, and so does anyone who dies of something else or leaves the study, so the person-time is a little under 10,000 person-years and the rate a little over 3 per 1,000 person-years.

In UK Biobank, conditions recorded before a participant’s baseline visit are prevalent; conditions first recorded after it are incident. Most of the cohort’s diseases had not yet started at baseline, so for incident cases the exposures were measured before the disease began. The follow-up section shows where these records come from; Measuring phenotypes shows how to turn them into case definitions.

5. Confounding and selection bias

Confounding happens when a third variable causes both the exposure and the outcome. In observational data, statin users can have more heart attacks than non-users even though statins lower risk, because high LDL cholesterol leads both to statin prescriptions and to heart attacks (left panel below). Adjusting for the confounder removes this bias. In genetic association studies the classic confounder is ancestry (population stratification).

Selection bias arises when the people analyzed were selected in a way related to both the exposure and the outcome. A common form is collider bias. If smoking and poor health both make people less likely to take part, then smokers who did take part are healthier than smokers in general, and the smoking–health association among participants is distorted (right panel). Taking part is the collider: a variable caused by both. Every analysis of volunteers conditions on it. A larger sample removes neither bias; it only makes the biased estimate more precise.31

Who was recruited and how Core

UK Biobank recruited adults aged 40–69 through the NHS and measured each of them once, in depth, at a local assessment centre. Invitation letters went to 9,238,453 people whose names and addresses came from NHS registers; 503,317 of them came to a centre, gave consent and were assessed: a participation rate of 5.45%.2 UK Biobank did not re-contact people who did not reply, a decision taken to save cost and time, and this partly explains the low rate.3

Where and when

Recruitment began with a pilot at Stockport, near Manchester, from March to June 2006; the main phase ran from April 2007 to October 2010.13,18 Twenty-two assessment centres were used, in England, Scotland and Wales, none in Northern Ireland. Each was open for between a few weeks and about a year and a half, and several ran at the same time; each saw around 100 participants a day, six days a week.1,18 Of the 501,938 participants in the Showcase, 89% were seen in England, 7% in Scotland and 4% in Wales (Data-Field 54).14

Ages, sexes, places

The target ages were 40–69. In the data, ages at recruitment run from 37 to 73, with a mean of 56.5 years (Data-Field 21022), slightly wider than the target range.15 54% of participants are women. At recruitment, 24% were aged 40–49, 34% were 50–59 and 42% were 60–69. About 86% lived in urban areas, and more than 41,000 were born outside the UK.12

Why ages 40–69?

The investigators called the age range “a pragmatic compromise”: participants had to be old enough for enough health outcomes to occur during follow-up, yet young enough for the first assessment to happen before illness had changed their exposures.1 They estimated that 5,000–10,000 cases of a condition would be needed to reliably detect odds ratios of about 1.3–1.5, and projected how quickly the cohort would accumulate them (figure).1

What participants agreed to

The visit began with electronic signed consent.1 Every participant gave broad informed consent: agreement, after being told the study’s purpose and procedures, to the use of their anonymized data and samples “for any health-related research”, to being re-contacted for further studies, and to UK Biobank’s access to their health-related records.1,25 At the end of the visit they received a printout of standard measurements such as blood pressure and body mass index, but no other individual results; potentially serious findings are the exception, for example findings on imaging scans, which are reported to the participant and their family doctor.1,25 Participants can withdraw at any time (Ethics and governance).

In the data Five fields almost every analysis starts with
  • Field 53 “Date of attending assessment centre”: one date per visit; for the baseline visit (instance 0) the dates run from 13 March 2006 to 1 October 2010.13
  • Field 54 “UK Biobank assessment centre”, coded with Data-Coding 10, for example 11010 = Leeds and 10003 = Stockport (pilot). Consider centre as a covariate: it captures both the local population and local measurement conditions.14
  • Field 21022 “Age at recruitment”, in whole years, derived from date of birth and the date of the first visit.15
  • Field 31 “Sex”, taken from the NHS registry at recruitment and in some cases updated by the participant, so it mixes registered and self-reported sex. Quality control compares it with genetic sex (Field 22001; next page).16
  • Field 22189 “Townsend deprivation index at recruitment”: the deprivation score of the census area of the participant’s postcode (defined in the representativeness section). It replaced the retired Field 189.17

On the Research Analysis Platform the column for field 53 at instance 0 is named p53_i0; What the data looks like explains the naming scheme.

A visit to the assessment centre Core

A baseline visit took about two and a half hours on average.1 Participants moved through a fixed sequence of stations. At reception each was given a USB key holding their participant ID, name, date of birth and sex, which identified them at every station and carried data between stations.9 To make the sequence concrete, here is one visit seen through a hypothetical participant.

Hypothetical participant One morning in spring 2010

H is a 61-year-old woman who lives in Sheffield. She is not a real participant, but each step of her visit follows UK Biobank’s protocols.6,8,9 UK Biobank found her through NHS registration records, because she is in the right age range and lives near the Sheffield centre, open from August 2009 to July 2010.18 She accepts the invitation letter and books a visit.

At reception her arrival is logged under a 9-digit participant ID and she receives her USB key. At a touchscreen she reads the consent statements and signs on a signature pad. She then answers the touchscreen questionnaire, about 30 minutes of questions on her work, home, smoking, alcohol, diet, sleep, family history and health, followed by a hearing test and short cognitive tests. A trained interviewer asks about her past illnesses, operations and current medications, including the statin her doctor prescribed, and measures her blood pressure twice. She has her eyes examined; her grip strength, waist, hip, height, sitting height, weight and body composition measured; an ultrasound scan of both heels; and a test of how much air she can blow out of her lungs (spirometry). Her answers to screening questions allow her to do a cycle fitness test with an electrocardiogram (ECG). Finally, blood is drawn into several tubes, she gives urine and saliva samples, and she leaves with a printout of her measurements and a travel-expenses claim form.

Everything recorded that morning becomes instance 0 of the baseline Data-Fields in her row of the data. The statin, the diet answers and any diagnosis she reported were all recorded on the same day.

The questionnaire and interview became the self-reported part of the data. Participants filled in the touchscreen questionnaire themselves (staff helped on request), and interviewers did not know its answers, apart from a few linked answers that decided which interview questions to ask.8 Illnesses that participants were unsure how to name were described to the interviewer, a trained nurse, who placed them in a coding tree; free-text entries were later reviewed by a doctor (Data-Field 20002).20 Measuring phenotypes covers what these fields contain and how reliable they are.

In the data Missing by design

Measurements added partway through recruitment are missing for everyone seen before they were introduced, for reasons that have nothing to do with the participant. In the 2015 summary, pulse-wave velocity had been recorded for about 170,000 people and the eye examination and cycle fitness test for about 100,000 each.1 Whether a value exists therefore depends on the date and centre of the visit (Fields 53 and 54). Check this before treating missing values as random, and before comparing people with and without a measurement.

The samples Core

Each participant gave about 45–50 ml of blood (UK Biobank’s documents give both “about 45 ml” and “around 50ml”) and a urine sample; from August 2009 participants were also asked for 2.5 ml of saliva, and 120,003 gave one.5,11,20 Blood was drawn without fasting; staff recorded the hours since the participant last ate or drank anything other than water (Data-Field 74, “Fasting time”).9

Each blood tube contains an additive that decides what can be extracted later. EDTA tubes stop the blood from clotting; spun in a centrifuge, they separate into plasma (the liquid part of the blood), a thin layer of white blood cells and platelets (the buffy coat, the source of DNA) and red blood cells. The serum tube lets the blood clot, leaving serum (plasma without its clotting proteins); the lithium-heparin tube gives plasma; the ACD tube keeps whole blood for possible future cell lines; and the RNA tube, used from August 2009, preserves RNA.5,7,9

Samples were kept at 2–8 °C (the ACD tube at room temperature), packed with cool packs and temperature loggers, and collected by courier at 8:30 pm, Monday to Saturday, for delivery to UK Biobank’s central laboratory in Stockport by 7:30 the next morning.9 Pilot studies had shown that a wide range of assays still worked on blood and urine kept at 4 °C for up to 36 hours. A full blood count was run on the fresh 4 ml EDTA tube, typically within 24 hours of the draw.10,33

In the laboratory, automated systems split each fraction into aliquots: small portions of 1.4 ml, each in its own tube, so that a later study can thaw one portion without touching the rest. They produced about 25,000 aliquots a day, about 15 million in total; on average, a sample went from needle to freezer in 24 ± 2.5 hours.10

The aliquots are stored at two sites. In Stockport, about 10 million samples are kept in an automated store at −80 °C, where a robot running on a track can retrieve about 250,000 samples a year; about 6 million backup copies are kept in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C at a separate site in southern England.11 More than 90% of the stored material has not yet been used.11 This is what lets UK Biobank add new columns for the same people years later: array genotypes (released 2015–2017), blood biochemistry (2019), NMR metabolomics (about 250 small molecules and lipid measures in plasma; 2021–2025) and proteomics (about 2,900 proteins in a subset of 54,219 people; 2023) were all measured on samples collected in 2006–2010.4,34,37,38 How DNA is extracted from the buffy coat and turned into genotypes is the subject of the next page, From a blood sample to genotypes.

In the data Sample handling leaves traces
  • Blood was not fasting. Glucose, triglycerides and many metabolites depend on the time since the last meal, recorded in Field 74; consider it as a covariate for such traits.9
  • Assays were run years after collection, in batches and on several instruments, so a biomarker value also carries information about when and how it was measured. Measuring phenotypes describes the quality procedures and the fields that record them.
  • Stored material is finite: every assay uses up aliquots, and some fractions, such as the buffy coat, have only one or two per participant (table above).10

Following participants over time Core

Most participants never returned to a UK Biobank centre. Their health is followed through record linkage: UK Biobank joins its own data with national health records about the same people, using identifying details that it holds encrypted and never gives to researchers.1,26 These are electronic health records: data created during routine care rather than for research, so what they contain depends on who sought care, where, and how it was coded. Four kinds are linked:23,32

  • Hospital inpatient records. Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) Admitted Patient Care in England, from 1997; the Patient Episode Database for Wales, from 1991 (with ICD-10 diagnosis codes from 1999); and the Scottish Morbidity Records, from 1981. Each hospital stay carries diagnosis codes from the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10; older Scottish records use ICD-9) and procedure codes (OPCS-4).
  • Death registrations for all participants, from NHS England and National Records of Scotland: date of death (Field 40000) and underlying and contributory causes in ICD-10 (Fields 40001, 40002).
  • Cancer registries, which record every diagnosed cancer, including cancers back to the early 1970s, with date, site and tumor type (Fields 40005, 40006).
  • Primary care records from general practitioners (GPs, family doctors): diagnoses, symptoms, prescriptions and test results for about 45% of participants (about 230,000 people), up to 2016 or 2017. A UK Government notice of 10 February 2026 allows coded GP data for all participants in England to be shared with UK Biobank; researchers will get them only once UK Biobank’s automated output-checking system is in place.

New visits and new kinds of data

  • Repeat assessment (instance 1). In 2012–13, 20,323 participants repeated the baseline assessment at a centre in Stockport (“Cheadle (revisit)” in Data-Coding 10).13,14 Its purpose is to measure how much a single measurement varies within a person, so that analyses can correct for regression dilution: random measurement error and day-to-day fluctuation bias the association with a single baseline measurement toward zero.1
  • Imaging (instances 2 and 3). In 2014 UK Biobank began an imaging study: a visit of about 5 hours at one of four dedicated centres, with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain, heart and abdomen, a whole-body DXA scan (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) of bone and body composition, ultrasound of the carotid arteries in the neck and an ECG, after which participants repeat most baseline measurements.21,27 The target of 100,000 people was reached in 2025, and 102,521 now have an imaging visit. A repeat imaging visit, from 2019, aims to re-scan 60,000 people by 2029; 21,469 have had one so far.13,27
  • Online questionnaires. About 330,000 participants with an email address receive online questionnaires (table below).24
  • Activity monitors. Between 2013 and 2015, 236,519 participants were invited by email to wear an accelerometer, a sensor that records acceleration 100 times a second, on the wrist for 7 days; 103,712 data sets came back and 96,600 were usable.22
  • COVID-19. During the pandemic UK Biobank added test results (Field 40100) and vaccination records, and ran an antibody study in which about 20,000 participants and their adult children and grandchildren sent monthly self-collected samples for at least six months from mid-2020. GP records released for about 400,000 participants under a temporary legal notice can no longer be requested, because the notice has expired.39
  • New assays of stored samples. Genotypes, exome and genome sequences, biochemistry, metabolomics and proteomics, shown in the mental-model figure and covered on the next two pages.

Who is (and is not) in UK Biobank Core

People who volunteer differ from people who decline. Fry and colleagues compared UK Biobank participants with everyone who was invited, using the age, sex, deprivation and region known for 94.8% of invitees, and with national surveys and the census.2 Women, older people and people living in less deprived areas were more likely to take part (panel A below).

Deprivation here is the Townsend deprivation index of a person’s home area. It combines four census measures: unemployment, households without a car, households not owner-occupied, and overcrowded households. Each measure (two of them after a log transform) is converted to a z-score across areas and the four z-scores are added, so higher scores mean more deprivation.30 In UK Biobank the median score is −2.14 and the mean −1.29 (Field 22189).17 According to UK Biobank, 86,000 participants live in the most deprived third of the country; an even spread would put about 168,000 there.12

Participants were also healthier and wealthier than the general population of the same age: fewer were obese or smoked, fewer reported cardiovascular disease, and more owned their home outright (panel B). By ages 70–74, all-cause mortality was 46.2% lower than in the general population for men and 55.5% lower for women, and total cancer incidence 11.8% and 18.1% lower.2 This pattern is called healthy volunteer bias. Fry and colleagues concluded that “UK Biobank is not representative of the sampling population”.2

By self-reported ethnic background, the cohort is close to the census for its age range: 94.6% of participants reported a White background, against 94.5% of people of the same ages in the 2001 census (and 91.3% in the 2011 census).2 As a result, relatively few participants reported other backgrounds: about 22,000 reported an ethnic background originating outside Europe.4 Ancestry and descriptors in UK Biobank explains the self-reported and genetic descriptors in the data.

What this means for analyses

  • Do not read prevalence or incidence in UK Biobank as national rates. Both are too low for the UK population of the same age, because participants are healthier.2
  • Associations often carry over. Batty and colleagues compared UK Biobank (response rate 5.5%) with 18 pooled cohorts from the Health Survey for England and the Scottish Health Survey (mean response 68%). For smoking and cardiovascular death, the age- and sex-adjusted hazard ratio, the ratio of the death rates in ever-smokers and never-smokers, was 2.04 in UK Biobank and 1.99 in the surveys (panel C). They concluded that “risk factor associations in the UK Biobank seem to be generalisable”.3 This kind of generalizability does not require a representative sample.
  • But selection can bias some associations. Because taking part depends on health, education and behavior, restricting an analysis to participants can create collider bias (idea 5): two factors that both affect participation can become associated among participants even if they are unrelated in the population.31
Common confusion A large n does not fix selection

With 500,000 people, confidence intervals are very narrow. A narrow interval around a biased estimate is still biased: neither selection bias nor confounding shrinks as the sample grows.

Ethics and governance Core

UK Biobank has approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee and research tissue bank approval from the Human Tissue Authority, so approved researchers do not need separate ethics approval unless they want to re-contact participants.1,4

  • Consent. Broad consent, given at the visit (What participants agreed to).
  • De-identification. Researchers never receive identifying information; UK Biobank holds it encrypted. Each project gets its own participant identifiers (eid), so data sets from different projects cannot be joined. Rare occupation codes are withheld, brain images are “defaced”, and location is given only on request, as a 1 km grid square or a small census area.26,41
  • Approved research only. Access is for “health-related research in the public interest”. Researchers register and apply, their institution signs a Material Transfer Agreement, they undertake “not to attempt to identify any participant”, and they return their results to UK Biobank.1,40
  • Withdrawal. Participants can choose no further contact (existing data and samples are still used and health records still linked), no further access (no new information is collected, existing data and samples can still be used), or no further use (samples are destroyed and their data are no longer available).25 UK Biobank periodically sends the eids of withdrawn participants, and their records must be removed from analyses.41
  • Where analyses happen. Since July 2024, data for new projects have been available only on the cloud-based UK Biobank Research Analysis Platform, with rare exceptions; individual-level exome and genome sequences cannot be downloaded at all.26,40 Details are in What the data looks like.

Access status, checked 21 October 2026. On 23 April 2026, at the request of the UK government, UK Biobank suspended all access to the Research Analysis Platform, after de-identified participant data downloaded from the platform by approved researchers had been offered for sale online. The platform is to reopen only once a system for checking what researchers download is in place.26 In the latest copies of UK Biobank’s pages that could be checked (11 October 2026), the platform was “currently closed whilst we implement necessary changes” and new applications were paused.40 The status may have changed since then: see the access section of What the data looks like and UK Biobank’s own page.

Key points
  • Design: UK Biobank is a prospective cohort, measured once at baseline (2006–2010) and followed through health records. Associations between baseline variables are cross-sectional; incident outcomes come after the visit. The PennCATH practical data are case–control.
  • Recruitment: 9,238,453 invited, 503,317 took part (5.45%), at 22 assessment centres in England, Scotland and Wales; target ages 40–69 (37–73 in the data); 54% women.
  • The visit: about 2.5 hours of consent, touchscreen questionnaire, interview, measurements and samples. Measurements added in 2009 are missing by design for people seen earlier.
  • Samples and follow-up: about 15 million aliquots stored at −80 °C and in liquid nitrogen; linked hospital, death and cancer records for everyone, GP records for about 45%; instances 0–3 index the visits.
  • Who is in it: participants are healthier and better off than the population they were drawn from. Do not read prevalence or incidence as national rates; many associations generalize, but selection can still bias some (collider bias).
  • Ethics: broad consent, de-identified data, approved health research only, and withdrawn participants must be removed.

Check your understanding

A student computes the share of UK Biobank participants with type 2 diabetes at baseline and reports it as the UK prevalence of diabetes. What is wrong?

Two things. The number is the prevalence among volunteers aged about 40–69, not among all UK adults. And the volunteers are healthier than people of the same age who declined (healthy volunteer bias), so the prevalence is likely too low even for that age group. A national estimate would need a representative sample or reweighting.

Why can a case–control study, such as the PennCATH data in the practical, not estimate how common coronary artery disease is, while a cohort can estimate its incidence?

In a case–control study the investigators choose how many cases and how many controls to include, so the share of cases reflects the design, not the population. A cohort enrolls people without regard to the outcome and counts new cases over the person-time of follow-up, which gives an incidence rate for the cohort (though, for UK Biobank, not for the whole UK).

A participant has values in p21001_i0 and p21001_i2 but not in p21001_i1. What happened?

Their BMI was measured at the baseline visit (instance 0) and at an imaging visit (instance 2). They were not among the 20,323 people who attended the 2012–13 repeat assessment (instance 1). The gap is missing by design, not a failed measurement.

Eye measurements exist for only about 100,000 participants. Is a missing eye measurement a sign of anything about the participant’s health?

Mostly not. Eye measurements were added from August 2009, so everyone seen earlier has none. Missingness depends on the date and centre of the visit (Fields 53 and 54), which you should check before treating the missing values as random.

Batty et al. found almost the same hazard ratio for smoking and cardiovascular death in UK Biobank as in representative surveys. Does this show that UK Biobank is representative?

No. UK Biobank is not representative: its prevalence, incidence and mortality differ from those of the general population. The result shows that some exposure–outcome associations are similar despite that. It does not guarantee that every association is unbiased, because conditioning on participation can create collider bias.

A participant in England, seen in 2008, had a heart attack in 1995 and another in 2012. Which events can the linked hospital records show, and are they prevalent or incident?

English hospital records (HES) start in 1997, so the 1995 heart attack is not in them; it can be known only from the interview (self-report) or, for the 45% with linked GP records, from primary care. It is a prevalent condition at baseline. The 2012 heart attack, after the 2008 visit, is an incident event, recorded in HES.
