Genetic variation: how genomes differ

Two people's genomes are about 99.9% identical. The remaining differences, a few million positions per person, are what genetic studies measure. This page defines a variant, an allele and a genotype precisely, shows how a genotype becomes the number 0, 1 or 2 in a data matrix, and explains what variants can do to genes and proteins.

Core for Lecture 2 ≈ 40 min Shows up in the data as: VCF REF/ALT, .bim A1/A2, genotype entries 0/1/2
Mental model

A variant is an address plus alleles; a genotype is a count

A variant is a position where DNA differs between people, written as an address plus alleles: (build, chromosome, position, reference allele, alternative allele). Each person has two copies of every autosomal address, so their genotype is a pair of alleles, which analyses record as 0, 1 or 2 copies of one chosen allele.

  • Variants begin as mutations. A child carries about 70 new ones; the other 4–5 million sites where a person differs from the reference were inherited from ancestors.
  • Variants come in all sizes, from one base (SNVs) to whole chromosomes. Single-base variants are by far the most numerous and are what genotyping arrays measure.
  • Two copies give three genotypes at a two-allele variant: homozygous (0 or 2) or heterozygous (1). This count is the entry of the genotype matrix.
  • Allele labels answer different questions (reference genome, evolution, population frequency, the analysis, the trait). Always state which allele a frequency or an effect refers to.
  • What a variant does depends on where it falls. Coding changes alter the protein (synonymous, missense, nonsense, frameshift); most trait-associated variants lie outside coding sequence.

Where variation comes from Core

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence. Many new mutations arise when DNA is copied before a cell divides and a copying mistake is not repaired; others come from damage by radiation or chemicals2. A mutation in a somatic cell stays in that cell and its descendants. Only a mutation in the germline, the cells that give rise to eggs and sperm, can pass to children (see page 1).

A de novo mutation is a variant present in a child but in neither parent: it arose in a parent's egg or sperm lineage. Sequencing the whole genomes of 1,548 Icelandic families (mother, father and child) found on average 70.3 de novo mutations per child, a rate of about 1.29 × 10⁻⁸ per base pair per generation3. The two numbers agree: a cell carries about 6 billion base pairs (two copies of each chromosome)20, and 1.29 × 10⁻⁸ × 6 × 10⁹ ≈ 77, close to the observed 70 (the rate is estimated only from the parts of the genome where new mutations can be detected reliably).

About 80% of de novo mutations arise in the father. Their number grows with parental age: each extra year of the father's age at conception adds about 1.5 mutations, and each extra year of the mother's age about 0.43.

Compare those 70 new mutations with the 4–5 million positions at which a typical genome differs from the reference genome (next section). Almost every variant you carry was inherited, most of them from ancestors who lived many generations ago. A variant that is common today arose once as a mutation in one person and spread through that person's descendants. This shared history is why nearby variants are correlated in a population (linkage disequilibrium, page 7) and why allele frequencies differ between populations (page 6, page 8).

How different are two genomes? Core

Take one copy of each chromosome from each of two people, line them up base by base, and they differ at roughly 1 position in every 1,250–1,3305. That is about 0.1% (more precisely about 0.08%), the source of the familiar statement that humans are "99.9% identical", a figure that counts single-base differences only. Measured against the reference genome, a typical person's genome differs at 4.1–5.0 million sites4.

More than 99.9% of those variant sites are single-base differences and short insertions or deletions. Larger structural variants are far fewer, about 2,100–2,500 per genome, but because each one is long, together they cover about 20 million bases, more than all the single-base differences combined4. Genomes in the 1000 Genomes African (AFR) group carry the most variant sites (about 4.3 million single-base variants, against about 3.5–3.6 million in each of the other four groups), because genetic diversity is highest in African populations (page 8).

Across many people the catalog of known variants is enormous. The 1000 Genomes Project found 88 million variants in 2,504 people4; gnomAD v4 lists about 787 million single-nucleotide variants and 123 million indels from 807,162 people, including 416,555 UK Biobank exomes7; and the dbSNP database has issued about 1.2 billion variant identifiers6. Most of these variants are rare, so each person carries only a small fraction of them (page 6).

Types of variants Core

Variants are classified by what changed and by how many bases are involved.

  • Single nucleotide variant (SNV): one base replaced by another, for example G → A. An SNV whose less common allele is not rare in a population (by convention, at least 1%) is called a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), pronounced "snip"12,13. In practice "SNP" is often used for any SNV, including on genotyping arrays.
  • Indel: a short insertion or deletion of one or more bases (by convention fewer than 50)8. In a gene's coding sequence, an indel whose length is not a multiple of 3 shifts the reading frame (see What variants do).
  • Short tandem repeat (STR, microsatellite): a motif of about 1–6 bases repeated back to back, such as CAGCAGCAG; alleles differ in the number of repeats2. In the HTT gene a CAG repeat is normally present 10–35 times; people with Huntington's disease have 36 to more than 120 copies (36–39 copies may or may not cause disease, 40 or more almost always do)9.
  • Copy number variant (CNV): a segment of DNA that is deleted or duplicated, so people carry different numbers of copies of it (usually defined as longer than 50 bp; classically at least 1 kb)8. Example: in the LPA gene a protein-coding segment called the kringle IV type 2 repeat varies so much in copy number that it produces more than 40 sizes of the apolipoprotein(a) protein; people with small sizes have median lipoprotein(a) concentrations up to five times higher10.
  • Structural variant (SV): any rearrangement affecting more than about 50 bp: large deletions, duplications and insertions (the CNVs), inversions (a segment flipped end to end) and translocations (a segment moved to another chromosome)8.
  • Aneuploidy: a whole chromosome gained or lost, so cells have a number of chromosomes other than 462. Most cases of Down syndrome are caused by trisomy 21: three copies of chromosome 21 instead of two11.

Which technology sees which type matters for analysis. Genotyping arrays measure pre-chosen SNVs and some short indels. Short-read sequencing finds SNVs and short indels well, but misses many larger, repetitive or complex variants; structural variants and long repeats often need long reads or specialized methods8,10. For example, several functional SNVs lie inside the repeated LPA segment, where conventional sequencing and genotyping cannot reach them10. The GWAS you will run on UK Biobank array and imputed data test SNVs and short indels almost exclusively (see From a blood sample to genotypes).

Common confusion Mutation, variant, polymorphism, SNP, SNV

Mutation is the event (a change in DNA) and, in older usage, a rare and often harmful variant. Variant is the neutral word for any difference from a reference sequence, whatever its frequency or effect. Polymorphism traditionally means a variant with a frequency above 1%; SNP is the single-base case, and SNV covers single-base variants of any frequency. Because "mutation" suggested harm and "polymorphism" suggested harmlessness, clinical guidelines recommend "variant" plus a classification (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, benign)12. Lecture 2 uses "SNP" for any single-base variant on an array.

Loci, alleles and genotypes Core

A locus (plural loci) is a specific position or stretch of the genome, identified by its chromosome and coordinates. It can be a single base or a whole gene. An allele is one of the alternative versions of the DNA sequence at a locus: at rs671 the alleles are the bases G and A; at an STR they are different repeat counts; at the ABO gene they are the A, B and O versions of the gene.

Autosomes come in pairs, one copy from each parent, so each person has two alleles at every autosomal locus. The pair is the person's genotype at that locus. If the two alleles are the same, the person is homozygous (G/G or A/A); if they differ, heterozygous (G/A). Genotype data usually record the pair without saying which copy came from which parent, so G/A and A/G are the same genotype (which allele sits on which copy is called phase; see page 7). On the X chromosome, males have a single copy outside the pseudoautosomal regions and are hemizygous (page 1).

For a variant with two alleles there are three possible genotypes, and analyses code them as the number of copies of one chosen allele: 0, 1 or 2. This allele dosage (or additive) coding turns genetics into ordinary numeric data: the genotypes of n people at m variants form an n × m matrix of 0s, 1s and 2s. The coding is also a modeling choice. Using the count as a numeric predictor in a regression assumes that each extra copy of the allele shifts the trait by the same amount, the additive model of page 9. After genotype imputation, which predicts genotypes that were not measured directly (page 11), entries become expected counts between 0 and 2, such as 0.97.

In the data From genotype calls to 0/1/2

In a VCF file each genotype is written as a pair of allele indices: 0 is the REF allele and 1 the first ALT allele, so 0/0, 0/1 and 1/1 mean 0, 1 and 2 copies of ALT, and ./. is a missing call16. PLINK 1.9's --recode A writes one column per variant named after the allele it counts (for example rs671_A), with values 0, 1, 2 or NA; by default it counts the A1 allele15. Before using any genotype column, find out which allele it counts (next section).

More than two alleles

Most SNVs have exactly two alleles, because a second mutation at the same base is rare; such variants are biallelic (Lecture 2 says "diallelic"). Loci with three or more alleles are multiallelic. The ABO blood group gene has three common alleles, A, B and O, so there are six genotypes, which give four blood groups: A and B each produce a different molecule on red blood cells, AB people produce both, and O produces neither. The O allele is itself a variant: most O alleles carry a single-base deletion that shifts the reading frame, so they encode an inactive enzyme14. With K alleles there are K(K + 1)/2 possible genotypes.

One 0/1/2 column cannot hold a three-allele genotype: counting O alleles gives 0 for both A/A and A/B. Analyses therefore split a multiallelic site into several biallelic records, each with its own 0/1/2 column. Genotype files already store ABO this way, because its alleles are defined by several separate variants: the A and B alleles differ by a few single-base substitutions that change four amino acids, and the O allele carries a single-base deletion14. The GRCh38 reference sequence itself carries the O deletion (rs8176719), so in a VCF the A and B alleles appear at that site as an "insertion", the ALT allele14,20.

In the data Multiallelic records

A VCF line can list several ALT alleles (ALT = G,T), and a genotype such as 1/2 then carries one copy of each16. Most association software expects biallelic records, so pipelines usually split such sites first, for example with bcftools norm -m -, and each resulting record gets its own 0/1/2 column18.

Allele labels: which allele is which? Core

The two alleles of a SNP get different names depending on the question being asked. The same allele can be the alternative, derived, minor, effect and risk allele, or none of these. Mixing up the labels flips the sign of an effect estimate, and it is one of the most common errors when results from different studies or files are combined.

  • Reference / alternative (REF / ALT). The reference allele is the base found in the reference genome at that position, on its + strand; any other allele is an alternative allele. REF does not mean normal or healthy: the reference sequence came from a few anonymous donors, so at many sites it carries the less common allele (page 2).
  • Ancestral / derived. The ancestral allele is the state before the mutation, inferred by comparing human DNA with other primates such as the chimpanzee; the derived allele is the one that arose by mutation.
  • Major / minor. The more and less frequent allele in a particular sample or population. The frequency of the minor allele is the minor allele frequency, MAF (page 6). Which allele is minor can change from one population to another.
  • Effect / other. The effect allele (also called the tested, coded or counted allele) is the allele whose copies are counted as 0, 1, 2 in an analysis; the reported effect size is the change in the trait per copy of it. It is chosen by the analyst or by a software default.
  • Risk / protective. For a disease, the risk allele is the one associated with higher risk and the protective allele the one associated with lower risk. This depends on the outcome studied: the same allele can raise the risk of one disease and lower another.

The running examples show that every combination occurs. Never assume that REF is major, ancestral or harmless, or that ALT is the minor or the effect allele.

Common confusion Always say which allele

A frequency, a genotype count or an effect size means nothing without its allele. Write "A allele frequency 0.64" or "β per copy of the A allele", not "MAF 0.64" or "β for rs4988235". Before comparing two results, check that both refer to the same allele, on the same strand (page 2) and the same genome build.

In the data Which allele does this file count?
  • VCF: REF and ALT are always given on the + strand of the stated build16.
  • PLINK 1.9 .bim: column 5 is A1 ("usually minor") and column 6 is A2 ("usually major"). Unless you use --keep-allele-order, PLINK 1.9 makes the major allele in your data A2, so A1 may be REF or ALT and can differ between two datasets15.
  • PLINK 2 reads .bim column 5 as ALT and column 6 as REF, so a file written by PLINK 1.9 may have them swapped (--ref-allele sets them back). Its --glm regression still counts the minor allele unless you add omit-ref, and the A1 column of its output names the counted allele15.
  • REGENIE treats the last allele listed in a .bim or BGEN file as the reference unless you add --ref-first17.
  • Before meta-analysis or computing polygenic scores, align every input to the same effect allele (page 13).

Naming variants Core

The same variant has several names, each tied to a different coordinate system. The sickle cell variant shows all of them.

  • rsID. The dbSNP database gives each known variant a reference SNP identifier (rsID), such as rs334. The rsID does not change between genome builds, so it is the most portable name. It does not say which allele is meant: dbSNP lists several possible ALT alleles at rs334 (T>A, T>C, T>G), so write the allele too, for example rs334:T>A20.
  • chromosome:position:REF:ALT. Precise but build-specific: rs334 is chr11:5227002:T:A on GRCh38 and chr11:5248232:T:A on GRCh37. Alleles are on the + strand. Many files use IDs of this form.
  • HGVS nomenclature. The Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) format describes a change relative to a named reference sequence: g. for genomic, c. for coding DNA, numbered along one of the gene's transcripts (the RNA versions a gene can produce; page 3) with c.1 the A of the ATG start codon, and p. for protein (three-letter amino acid codes; parentheses mean "predicted from DNA"; Ter or * is a stop codon)19.
  • Legacy names. Older names often use other numbering systems; see the table below.

What variants do Core

A variant's possible effect depends on where it falls relative to genes. Annotation tools such as Ensembl's Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) assign each variant a consequence for each transcript it overlaps, using standard terms such as missense_variant20. Most variants have no detectable effect on any trait, and whether a particular one matters has to be shown with data.

Changes in coding sequence

Inside the coding sequence, the genetic code (page 3) decides the effect of a change.

  • Synonymous: the codon changes but still encodes the same amino acid (CAT and CAC both encode histidine). The protein is unchanged, although a few synonymous variants alter splicing.
  • Missense (non-synonymous): one amino acid is replaced by another (GAG to GTG turns glutamic acid into valine in sickle cell disease). The effect ranges from none to severe, depending on the amino acid and its place in the protein.
  • Nonsense (stop-gained): a codon becomes a stop codon (GAG to TAG), so the protein is cut short.
  • Frameshift: an insertion or deletion whose length is not a multiple of 3 shifts the reading frame, so every codon after it is read differently until a new stop codon appears by chance.
  • Start-lost: the ATG start codon is changed, so translation cannot begin there.
  • In-frame insertion or deletion: a multiple of 3 bases added or removed, which adds or removes amino acids but keeps the rest of the protein.
  • Splice-site: a change in the short sequences at exon–intron boundaries (usually GT at the start of an intron and AG at its end) that mark where introns are cut out. It can make the cell skip an exon or keep an intron.

A typical genome carries 10,000–12,000 sites with amino-acid-changing variants and 149–182 with protein-truncating variants4. The example below follows one missense change from DNA to disease.

Sickle cell disease is the classic example of a single missense change with a large effect. People with two copies of the sickle allele have the disease: their sickle-shaped red cells block small blood vessels and are destroyed early22. People with one copy (sickle cell trait) are almost 90% protected against severe malaria23, which is why the allele is common where malaria has been common (frequency 0.10 in the 1000 Genomes African group, and absent from its European and East Asian groups20; see page 6).

Every preset is a real variant recorded in dbSNP or ClinVar. The HGVS names follow the 3′ rule: in a run of identical bases, a deletion is assigned to the last one.

Changes outside coding sequence

Protein-coding sequence makes up only about 1.1–1.2% of the genome35, so most variants are non-coding. A non-coding variant can still matter if it changes a regulatory element: a promoter or enhancer that controls how much a gene is transcribed, an untranslated region (UTR) that affects the mRNA, or a splice signal. Several running examples work this way:

  • rs4988235 lies in an intron of MCM6, about 14 kb upstream of LCT, the lactase gene; its A allele marks lactase persistence, the ability of adults to keep digesting milk sugar26.
  • rs1427407 lies in an enhancer of BCL11A that is active in red blood cell precursors. Its T allele weakens a transcription-factor binding site, which lowers BCL11A expression and raises fetal hemoglobin27 (page 3).
  • rs2814778 changes a binding site for the transcription factor GATA1 in the ACKR1 promoter, so red blood cells make no Duffy protein (the "Duffy-null" type). This protects against Plasmodium vivax malaria and lowers blood neutrophil counts28.

An intergenic variant lies between genes. Most have no known function, but some sit in enhancers that act on genes far away.

Most associations found by genome-wide association studies (GWAS, which test millions of variants one at a time for association with a trait; page 9) are non-coding. Of 465 trait-associated SNPs from GWAS published through 2008, 45% were intronic, 43% intergenic and only 9% changed an amino acid24. A later analysis put about 93% of trait-associated variants in non-coding DNA and found that most of them lie in, or are tightly correlated with variants in, regions of open chromatin (DNase I hypersensitive sites), where regulatory elements are usually found25. Keep one caution in mind: an associated SNP is often not itself the cause. It may only be correlated with a nearby causal variant through linkage disequilibrium (page 7).

Loss of function and gain of function

A loss-of-function (LoF) variant reduces or abolishes the activity of a gene's product: nonsense, frameshift, start-lost and essential splice-site variants usually do, and some missense variants do too. A gain-of-function variant increases activity or gives the product a new property. PCSK9 shows both directions in one gene. Missense variants that probably increase PCSK9 activity cause inherited high LDL cholesterol (the cholesterol carried by low-density lipoprotein particles, a cause of coronary artery disease)30. Two nonsense variants, Y142X and C679X, which together have a frequency of 2% in African Americans, lower LDL cholesterol by about 40%30. The running example R46L (rs11591147) is associated with 15% lower LDL cholesterol and 47% lower coronary heart disease risk31. These findings led to PCSK9-inhibiting drugs (page 9).

Losing one of two working copies is often tolerated, because the other copy still makes the product; each of us carries, on one copy, some variants that would be lethal on both36. There are exceptions. ALDH2 is built from four copies of the same protein chain, and one copy of the rs671 A allele (ALDH2*2) is enough to cause the alcohol-flush reaction29. How many copies are needed to change a trait is the subject of dominance and recessiveness (page 5).

A variant's effect can also depend on the environment. α1-Antitrypsin inhibits elastase, an enzyme that can damage the lining of the lungs; its Z variant (Glu342→Lys) predisposes people to emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and smoking makes the effect worse36. Some harmful variants stay common because they also bring an advantage: the sickle allele and the variants that cause glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency both give resistance to malaria36 (see natural selection, page 6).

In the data Consequence annotations choose variants for rare-variant tests

Rare variants are too rare to test one at a time, so gene-based "burden" tests (Session 7) add up the rare variants in each gene, usually only those annotated as loss-of-function or damaging missense. These annotations come from tools such as VEP and depend on the transcript used; the HGVS names on this page use each gene's MANE Select transcript: one representative transcript per gene, annotated identically by NCBI and Ensembl and intended as the standard for clinical reporting (for HBB, NM_000518.5)20,38. Population frequencies used to define "rare" often come from gnomAD, which itself includes 416,555 UK Biobank exomes7.

Five public resources answer most questions about a single variant. Each accepts an rsID or a chromosome position.

For rs334, for example, dbSNP lists its alleles and positions, gnomAD (v4.1 genomes) shows that the A allele has a frequency of about 4% in its African/African American group and below 1% in every other group, VEP calls it missense_variant (p.Glu7Val), and ClinVar holds clinical classifications for sickle cell disease20,21.

Key points
  • Variants start as mutations. Each child has about 70 de novo mutations, mostly from the father, while a typical genome differs from the reference at 4–5 million inherited sites.
  • Variant types range from single bases (SNVs; SNPs when common) through indels, STRs, CNVs and other structural variants to whole-chromosome aneuploidy. SNVs are the most numerous and are what arrays and GWAS mostly test.
  • A variant is an address plus alleles. A person's genotype is the pair of alleles on their two copies (homozygous or heterozygous), coded 0/1/2 as copies of one chosen allele; this is the entry of the genotype matrix. Multiallelic loci such as ABO need more than one column.
  • REF/ALT, ancestral/derived, major/minor, effect/other and risk/protective are different questions about the same alleles. Always name the allele behind a frequency or an effect, and check strand and build.
  • The same variant has several names (rsID, chr:pos:REF:ALT, HGVS g./c./p., legacy names), and genes on the − strand give complementary alleles in c. notation.
  • Coding variants can be synonymous, missense, nonsense, frameshift or start-lost; most variants, and most GWAS signals, are non-coding and act, if at all, through gene regulation.

Check your understanding

A VCF line reads 12 111803962 rs671 G A … GT 0/1. What are this person's alleles, is the person homozygous or heterozygous, and what is the 0/1/2 value if you count ALT alleles?

The alleles are G (REF, index 0) and A (ALT, index 1). The person is heterozygous, and the count of ALT alleles is 1.

In gnomAD's non-Finnish European group, allele A of rs4988235 has frequency 0.64. Which allele is minor there? With default settings, which allele will PLINK 1.9 make A1, and how will an A/A person be coded if A1 is counted?

G is the minor allele (frequency 0.36), so PLINK 1.9 makes G the A1 allele by default. An A/A person carries no copies of G and is coded 0, even though A is the ALT allele. Counting ALT instead would code the same person 2.

One paper calls an MTHFR variant "C677T", a clinical report writes c.665C>T, and a VCF shows chromosome 1, position 11,796,321, G>A. Can these be the same variant?

Yes. "677" comes from an older numbering of the MTHFR transcript; c.665 is the position in the current reference transcript. MTHFR lies on the − strand, so the coding-strand C>T appears as G>A on the + strand used in VCF files (GRCh38).

Classify these HBB changes: (a) c.9T>C, which turns CAT into CAC; (b) c.19G>T, which turns GAG into TAG; (c) c.20del; (d) deleting c.22–c.24.

(a) Synonymous: both codons encode histidine. (b) Nonsense: TAG is a stop codon, p.(Glu7Ter). (c) Frameshift: one base is lost, so every later codon changes, p.(Glu7GlyfsTer13). (d) In-frame deletion: three bases (one codon) are lost, p.(Glu8del). Try each in the widget.

A child has about 70 de novo mutations but differs from the reference genome at about 4–5 million sites. What does that say about where most of a person's variants come from?

More than 99.99% were inherited. They arose as mutations in ancestors, often many generations ago, and are shared with many other people. This is why variants have population frequencies and why nearby variants are correlated (linkage disequilibrium).

Most GWAS signals are intronic or intergenic. Give two reasons why such a variant can still be associated with a trait.

(1) It may change a regulatory element, such as an enhancer, promoter or splice signal, and so alter how much of a gene's product is made (examples: rs4988235, rs1427407, rs2814778). (2) It may have no effect itself but be correlated, through linkage disequilibrium, with a nearby causal variant.
