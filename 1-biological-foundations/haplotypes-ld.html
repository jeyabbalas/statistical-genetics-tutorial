Populations II: haplotypes and linkage disequilibrium

Alleles that sit close together on a chromosome are inherited together, so across a population they are correlated. This correlation is called linkage disequilibrium (LD). LD is why an array of under a million SNPs can stand in for tens of millions of variants, why GWAS results appear as peaks of many SNPs instead of single points, and why the most significant SNP in a peak is often not the variant that causes the association.

Core for Lecture 2 ≈ 35 min Shows up in the data as: correlated neighboring columns of the genotype matrix
Mental model

Chromosomes are mosaics of ancestral segments; LD is correlation between columns

Every chromosome copy in a population is a mosaic of segments inherited from a limited set of ancestral haplotypes. Alleles on the same segment are inherited together, so nearby columns of the haplotype matrix, and of the genotype matrix built from it, are correlated. This correlation is linkage disequilibrium. Recombination cuts the segments shorter every generation, so LD fades with distance, and recombination hotspots split it into blocks.

  • Haplotypes and phase. A haplotype is the row of alleles along one chromosome copy. Genotype data add each person's two rows together, so which alleles share a copy (the phase) has to be inferred.
  • Where LD comes from. A new mutation starts out on one haplotype, perfectly associated with the alleles around it. Recombination erodes the association by a factor (1 − θ) per generation.
  • How LD is measured. From a 2 × 2 table of haplotype counts: D, D′ and r². r² is the squared correlation between two columns of the 0/1 haplotype matrix.
  • Where LD is strong. Inside blocks between recombination hotspots, and over longer distances in populations with a smaller long-term size, such as populations descended from the groups that left Africa.
  • Why GWAS cares. LD lets arrays and imputation cover common variants, turns one causal variant into a peak of associated SNPs, sets the number of effectively independent tests behind 5 × 10⁻⁸, and drives pruning, clumping and LD score regression.

Haplotypes and phase Core

Each person carries two copies of every autosome, one inherited from each parent. A haplotype is the sequence of alleles found along one of these copies at a set of loci. For two biallelic SNPs, one with alleles A and a and one with alleles G and g, each chromosome copy carries one of four two-SNP haplotypes: AG, Ag, aG or ag. Lecture 2 uses exactly this notation (slides 114–124), and so does this page.

In data terms, a region measured in n people gives a haplotype matrix with 2n rows (two chromosome copies per person) and one column per SNP. Each entry is 1 if that copy carries the counted allele and 0 if it does not. The genotype matrix you will analyze is what you get by adding each person's two rows: every entry becomes an allele dosage of 0, 1 or 2. The addition throws away one piece of information: which alleles sat together on the same copy.

That piece of information is the phase (haplotype phase): the assignment of a person's alleles at heterozygous sites to their two chromosome copies. Phase matters only when a person is heterozygous at two or more sites. Someone who is A/a at the first SNP and G/g at the second has dosages 1 and 1, and these fit two different haplotype pairs: AG with ag, or Ag with aG (figure below). Genotyping arrays measure each SNP separately, so they report genotypes, not haplotypes (Lecture 2, slide 114).

Phasing means inferring each person's two haplotypes from data. There are three routes:

  • Relatives. In a trio (two parents and their child), an allele the child cannot have received from one parent must have come from the other, so most of the child's heterozygous sites can be assigned to the maternal or paternal copy. The International HapMap Project genotyped 30 parent–offspring trios in each of two of its samples for this reason1.
  • Statistical phasing. Haplotypes are shared among people (the mosaic in the mental model), so the most plausible pair for one person is a pair built from haplotypes that are common in everyone else. Lecture 2 (slide 114) describes the classic method, the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm: start from a guess of the haplotype frequencies, compute each person's expected haplotype counts given their genotypes (assuming their two haplotypes were paired at random), re-estimate the frequencies from those counts, and repeat until nothing changes. Modern tools such as SHAPEIT, Eagle and Beagle use hidden Markov models in which each haplotype is built as a mosaic of other haplotypes in the sample, and they scale to hundreds of thousands of people2,3.
  • Long sequencing reads. One DNA molecule carries the alleles of one chromosome copy, so a single sequencing read that covers two heterozygous sites shows their phase directly. With PacBio HiFi reads of about 13.5 kb, 99.64% of the heterozygous autosomal variants in one genome were phased into blocks with an N50 of 206 kb (half of the phased sequence was in blocks at least that long)4.

How LD arises and how recombination breaks it down Core

Two loci are in linkage disequilibrium (LD) when, across the chromosome copies of a population, the allele a copy carries at one locus is associated with the allele it carries at the other: knowing one changes the probabilities for the other. For haplotype frequencies this means P(AG) ≠ P(A) × P(G). When the two sides are equal, the loci are in linkage equilibrium (Lecture 2, slide 119).

LD starts with mutation. A new mutation happens on a single chromosome copy, so the new allele first appears on one particular haplotype, next to whatever alleles that copy carried at the neighboring SNPs. At first every copy of the new allele carries that same background, so the new allele is perfectly associated with the alleles around it (slides 109–110).

Recombination breaks the association down. In each meiosis, crossovers join part of one parental chromosome copy to part of the other (see the inheritance page). A crossover between the new allele and a neighboring SNP produces a copy that carries the new allele on a different background. The chance of this per generation is the recombination fraction θ between the two loci: close to 0 for adjacent sites and 0.5 for loci on different chromosomes. Under random mating, the LD coefficient D (defined in the next section) shrinks by a factor (1 − θ) in each generation8, so after t generations

Dt = (1 − θ)t D0

Crossovers occur at an average rate of about 1 centimorgan per megabase (1.13 cM/Mb in the deCODE genetic map), that is, about a 1% chance per meiosis for loci 1 Mb apart9. So two SNPs 10 kb apart (θ ≈ 10⁻⁴) keep most of their LD for thousands of generations, two SNPs 1 Mb apart (θ ≈ 0.01) lose most of it within a few hundred generations, and unlinked loci (θ = 0.5) lose half of it every generation (figure below).

The result is the picture on slide 112 and in the mental model: today's chromosome copies are mosaics of segments from ancestral haplotypes. Alleles at very close SNPs usually lie on the same segment and still reflect single ancestral haplotypes; alleles far apart have been separated by many past recombinations. The statistical models behind phasing and imputation describe each haplotype in exactly this way, as an imperfect mosaic of the other haplotypes in the sample2. The widget later on this page simulates its population this way, too.

Mutation and recombination are not the only forces. Slide 85 adds selection and demographics, and slide 112 adds population size8,10:

  • Genetic drift. In a population of finite size, haplotype frequencies change at random from one generation to the next, and this creates LD between linked loci. Drift is stronger in smaller populations. Balancing drift against recombination gives a classic approximation, first derived by Sved (1971), for the expected r² (a measure defined in the next section) between two loci: E[r²] ≈ 1 / (1 + 4Nec), where c is the recombination fraction and Ne is the effective population size, the size of an idealized, randomly mating population that would show the same amount of drift11. For example, 4Nec = 4 gives an expected r² of 0.2, and 4Nec = 40 gives about 0.02. LD therefore reaches further when Ne is small or the loci are close.
  • Bottlenecks and founder effects. A sharp drop in population size is drift in an extreme form, and it increases LD8.
  • Natural selection. When an allele rises in frequency quickly because it is favored, recombination has little time to act, so the allele stays on a long stretch of its original haplotype. The lactase-persistence alleles near LCT, including rs4988235, mark a haplotype that is common (about 77%) in the samples the authors describe as "northern European-derived populations" and extends largely unbroken for more than 1 Mb12.
  • Admixture and population structure. Mixing people from populations with different allele frequencies creates LD, even between loci on different chromosomes. LD decays by a factor (1 − c) per generation, so between unlinked loci (c = 0.5) it halves every generation, but between linked loci it fades slowly: recently admixed populations can show "substantial LD over centimorgan distances"10,8.
Common confusion LD is not the same thing as genetic linkage

Genetic linkage is about families: two loci are linked when they are close enough on a chromosome that their alleles tend to be passed on together from parent to child (θ < 0.5). LD is about populations: alleles at two loci are associated across the chromosome copies of a population. Linkage is needed for LD to last, but not for LD to exist: admixture or population structure can associate alleles at unlinked loci. That is why Lecture 2 (slide 115) says that the standard LD measures are really measures of gametic disequilibrium, an association between alleles carried on the same gamete, whatever its cause. In practice LD is computed only for loci on the same chromosome, where the two terms coincide.

Measuring LD: D, D′ and r² Core

All the standard LD measures start from the four haplotype frequencies of two biallelic SNPs, arranged in a 2 × 2 table (Lecture 2, slide 118). Write pA and pa = 1 − pA for the allele frequencies at the first SNP, pG and pg = 1 − pG for the second, and pAG, pAg, paG and pag for the haplotype frequencies. If the two SNPs were independent, each haplotype frequency would be the product of its two allele frequencies; for example, pAG = pApG.

D: departure from independence

The LD coefficient D (written d or δ on slides 118–120) measures how far the table is from independence:

D = pAG − pApG = pAGpag − pAgpaG

D = 0 means no LD. The sign of D only reflects which alleles you happened to call A and G, and the range D can take depends on the allele frequencies, so raw values of D are hard to compare between pairs of SNPs (slide 120). One identity helps: D = pApa [P(G | A) − P(G | a)]. So D is proportional to how much more often G occurs on copies that carry A than on copies that carry a.

D′: D relative to its largest possible value

D′ (D prime) divides D by the most extreme value it could take, given the allele frequencies:

D′ = D / Dmax, with Dmax = min(pApg, papG) if D > 0, or min(pApG, papg) if D < 0

|D′| runs from 0 to 1. |D′| = 1 exactly when at least one of the four haplotypes is absent8; this is the "tip" on slide 124. Lecture 2 (slides 121–122) reads |D′| = 1 as no evidence of historical recombination between the two SNPs. Two cautions. First, |D′| estimates are biased upward in small samples and when an allele is rare: a rare haplotype can be missing from a sample by chance, which forces |D′| = 1 whatever the true LD13,14. Second, |D′| = 1 says nothing about how well one SNP predicts the other.

r²: the squared correlation

r² scales D by the allele frequencies at both SNPs:

r² = D² / (pA pa pG pg)

Code each chromosome copy as X = 1 if it carries A (0 if a) and Y = 1 if it carries G (0 if g). Then Cov(X, Y) = pAG − pApG = D, Var(X) = pApa and Var(Y) = pGpg. So r = D / √(pApapGpg) is exactly the Pearson correlation between two columns of the haplotype matrix (slide 121). r² runs from 0 to 1. It equals 1 ("perfect LD") only when just two haplotypes exist, AG and ag (or Ag and aG); then each SNP's alleles predict the other's without error, and the two SNPs must have the same minor allele frequency (slide 122).

The two measures answer different questions. |D′| asks whether all four haplotypes exist, which reflects the history of recombination between the SNPs. r² asks how well one SNP's alleles predict the other's, which is what matters for association studies. Slide 122 puts it as "directly related to power for association": if a marker SNP has correlation r with a causal SNP, a test at the marker has about the power of a test at the causal SNP with r² times the sample size, so matching the power requires about N/r² people10. A marker with r² = 0.8 needs about 25% more people (1/0.8 = 1.25); a marker with r² = 0.2 needs five times as many.

Worked example: Lecture 2, slide 124

The table inferred by EM above has 16 chromosome copies: 8 AG, 2 Ag, 0 aG and 6 ag. So pA = 10/16 = 0.625, pG = 8/16 = 0.5 and pAG = 8/16 = 0.5.

  • D = 0.5 − 0.625 × 0.5 = 0.1875. Slide 124 computes the same number from counts: (1/16)² × (8 × 6 − 2 × 0) = 48/256.
  • D is positive, so Dmax = min(pApg, papG) = min(0.3125, 0.1875) = 0.1875, and D′ = 0.1875 / 0.1875 = 1. The aG haplotype is missing, which forces |D′| = 1.
  • r² = 0.1875² / (0.625 × 0.375 × 0.5 × 0.5) = 0.03515625 / 0.05859375 = 0.6, so r ≈ 0.775.

So this pair is in "complete" LD by D′ but only moderately correlated by r². A copy that carries a always carries g, but a copy that carries A carries G only 8 times out of 10: P(G | A) = 0.8 and P(G | a) = 0, and indeed D = 0.625 × 0.375 × (0.8 − 0) = 0.1875.

Because r² = 1 requires equal allele frequencies, SNPs with different frequencies cannot be strongly correlated, even when |D′| = 1. For minor allele frequencies m1 ≤ m2, the largest possible r² is m1(1 − m2) / [(1 − m1)m2], reached when every copy that carries the rarer minor allele also carries the other minor allele. A SNP with minor allele frequency 0.05 can therefore reach at most r² ≈ 0.08 with a SNP of frequency 0.4 (figure below). This is what slide 85 means by "high r² between two SNPs requires both have similar MAFs", and it is one reason common SNPs are poor proxies for rare variants.

In the data Computing LD with PLINK

plink --bfile mydata --r2 reports, for nearby pairs of variants, the squared correlation between their two columns of allele counts (0, 1, 2) across people. It does not need phase: under random mating, each dosage is the sum of two independent 0/1 haplotype codes, so in the population the correlation between two dosage columns equals the haplotype r. By default PLINK 1.9 reports only pairs that lie within a window of 10 variants and 1,000 kb and have r² of at least 0.2; --ld with two variant IDs instead estimates the four haplotype frequencies by maximum likelihood and prints haplotype-based r² and D′ for that pair15. LD varies along the genome and between populations Core

Averaged over many pairs of SNPs, LD falls with distance; plotting the average against distance gives an LD decay curve. Lecture 2 shows two versions: r against distance for five chromosomal regions, published in 2001 (slide 86), and mean D′ and r² against distance in the HapMap samples (slide 95). In a US sample described as "of north-European descent", Reich et al. found that LD typically extends about 60 kb from common alleles, and markedly less far in a sample of Yoruba from Nigeria (under 5 kb by their measure, the distance at which the average |D′| falls below 0.5)16.

Individual pairs scatter widely around these averages, because recombination is far from even along the genome. Most crossovers fall in recombination hotspots, stretches typically 1–2 kb long17. The HapMap Project identified 32,996 hotspots, which together account for about 60% of recombination in about 6% of the sequence18. Between hotspots, recombination is rare, so SNPs keep their ancestral combinations. An LD block (haplotype block) is a run of SNPs with strong pairwise LD and only a few common haplotypes, bounded by hotspots or other sites of past recombination. In one early study, three to five common haplotypes typically captured about 90% of the chromosomes in a block19; Lesk describes the same pattern as 100-kb regions that show "fewer than five combinations of SNPs" out of the 2100 possible20.

On an r² heatmap, blocks appear as dark triangles along the diagonal. Two SNPs a few kilobases apart on opposite sides of a hotspot can be almost uncorrelated, while two SNPs tens of kilobases apart inside one block can be almost perfectly correlated. This is why GWAS tools look up measured LD between SNPs instead of relying on distance.

LD also differs between populations (slides 94–96), with a consistent pattern: LD extends over shorter distances in samples from African populations than in samples from other populations.

  • In Gabriel et al.'s samples, half of the genome lay in blocks of at least 22 kb in the Yoruba and African American samples, and of at least 44 kb in the European and Asian samples19.
  • In ten 500-kb regions where HapMap genotyped nearly every common SNP, choosing SNPs until every common SNP had r² ≥ 0.8 with at least one chosen SNP took about one SNP every 2 kb in YRI (Yoruba in Ibadan, Nigeria), compared with one every 5 kb in CEU (Utah residents with northern and western European ancestry) and CHB+JPT (Han Chinese in Beijing and Japanese in Tokyo)1.
  • Across the 26 populations of the 1000 Genomes Project, LD decays fastest with distance in African populations and slowest in East Asian populations21.

The usual explanation is population history. Humans originated in Africa, and the populations that later spread out of Africa went through bottlenecks (see the ancestry page). Populations in Africa have had a longer history at a larger effective population size: more generations of recombination have cut the ancestral segments short, and weaker drift has rebuilt less LD. Lesk puts it this way: the older the population, or more precisely the larger the number of generations, the greater the chance of recombination20. In Sved's approximation above, a larger Ne lowers the expected r² at every distance.

In the data Choose an LD reference that matches your sample

Many tools need LD between SNPs but not individual genotypes, so they use an LD reference panel: phased haplotypes or precomputed LD values from a reference sample. LDlink, for example, uses the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 data, 2,504 people from 26 populations22,21. Pan-UK Biobank provides LD matrices computed within UK Biobank for six genetic ancestry groups23. Because LD differs between populations, the reference should be genetically similar to the people in your GWAS. A mismatched reference gives wrong r² values for regional plots, clumping and fine-mapping.

LD in GWAS: tagging, imputation and association peaks Core

Slide 85 lists why LD matters for genetic studies. This section and the next follow that list and add the other places where LD shapes a GWAS, each with the Lecture 2 session where it comes back.

Lecture 2 Slide 85 · Linkage disequilibrium: what and why

In Lecture 2 (slide 85) you will see LD described as "the correlation between genetic markers in a population", "typically measured using r²". Its mechanisms are "mutation and recombination in meiosis", "selection" and "demographics" (the section on how LD arises). The reasons to study it map onto this page: "to assess the number of independent tests in the genome" (5 × 10⁻⁸, next section), "to identify a minimum number of variants that retain coverage of genetic variation" (tag SNPs), "to impute markers that are not directly genotyped" (imputation) and "to help understand the origins and biological properties of genetic variation" (blocks and population differences).

Tag SNPs: measuring a few variants stands in for many

Because nearby SNPs are correlated, genotyping one SNP gives information about its neighbors. A tag SNP is a SNP chosen because it is in high LD with SNPs that are not genotyped (a common criterion is r² ≥ 0.8), so that it can stand in for them. Genotyping arrays are designed this way. HapMap estimated that about 1.09 million well-chosen SNPs were needed to capture all common HapMap SNPs at r² ≥ 0.8 in YRI, but slightly more than 500,000 in CEU or CHB+JPT. An array with about 550,000 SNPs captured an estimated 88% of common SNPs at r² ≥ 0.8 in CEU and 55% in YRI, and the authors noted that such estimates are optimistic in practice18. The UK Biobank Axiom array has 825,927 markers6.

Imputation: filling in what the array did not measure

Genotype imputation goes further. It predicts genotypes at variants that were not measured by comparing each person's phased haplotypes, at the array SNPs, with a reference panel of sequenced haplotypes. Where a person's haplotype matches a reference haplotype at the array SNPs, the alleles of that reference haplotype at the other variants are the likely ones (figure below). Real imputation software models each haplotype as a mosaic of many reference haplotypes and reports a probability for each genotype, summarized as a dosage between 0 and 2. UK Biobank imputed its array data to 93,095,623 autosomal variants, mainly with the Haplotype Reference Consortium panel of 64,976 haplotypes6. The samples-to-genotypes page covers imputation quality and file formats.

Association signals are peaks of correlated SNPs

Suppose one variant in a region affects a trait; call it the causal variant. Every SNP in LD with it will also be associated with the trait, because its genotype column is correlated with the causal column. With a single causal variant, the expected excess of a marker's association statistic (its χ² minus 1, the value expected under no association) is about r² times the excess at the causal variant, where r² is between the marker and the causal variant. This is the N/r² rule again. The result is a peak: a cluster of SNPs whose −log10 p values rise and fall with their LD to the causal variant.

A regional association plot, often drawn with the LocusZoom software, shows this directly: −log10 p against position for every SNP in a window, each point colored by its r² with a reference SNP, together with the local recombination rate and the genes in the region24. The reference SNP is usually the lead variant (lead SNP, or index SNP): the SNP with the smallest p value in the region.

The lead SNP is not necessarily the causal variant. Inside a block, many SNPs have almost the same correlation with the trait, and sampling noise decides which one comes out on top. The causal variant may not even be on the array or in the imputed data. Narrowing a peak down to a short list of candidate causal variants is called fine-mapping. Statistical fine-mapping methods use the association statistics and the LD matrix of the region to estimate, for each variant, the probability that it is causal. They report a credible set: a small set of variants that contains a causal variant with a stated probability, often 95%25. Fine-mapping is Session 4 (Lecture 2, slide 49). How this simulation works

The region is 200 kb with 60 SNPs at random positions and three recombination hotspots at random places. The population descends from a small number of ancestral haplotypes (10 for the longer-range LD population, 24 for the shorter-range one) with unequal frequencies. Between two hotspots, the SNP alleles are placed on the branches of a random genealogy of these ancestral haplotypes, so SNPs in the same block are correlated. Each present-day chromosome copy is built from left to right by copying one ancestral haplotype and, between consecutive SNPs, switching to a randomly drawn one with a probability that is small in general and large at hotspots (a simplified version of the copying model of Li and Stephens2); each copied allele is flipped with probability 0.003. The shorter-range population has more ancestral haplotypes and more switching, standing in for a longer history of recombination.

People are formed by pairing consecutive chromosome copies (random pairing, so Hardy–Weinberg proportions hold), which gives genotypes 0, 1 or 2. The trait is y = β·gcausal + ε with ε ~ Normal(0, 1). Each SNP is tested with a simple linear regression of y on its genotype, with a two-sided t-test on N − 2 degrees of freedom. The r² and D′ values come from the 2 × 2 haplotype counts in the simulated copies, where phase is known. The random number generator is seeded, so the first view is the same for everyone.

LD in GWAS: the number of tests, pruning, clumping and LD scores Core

The effective number of tests, and 5 × 10⁻⁸

A GWAS tests millions of variants, so it needs a correction for multiple testing. A Bonferroni correction divides α = 0.05 by the number of tests, but tests of SNPs in LD are correlated, so the relevant count is the effective number of independent tests: the number of independent tests that would give the same family-wise false-positive rate (the probability of at least one false positive among all the tests). In 1996, Risch and Merikangas considered testing 10⁶ alleles (both alleles of five SNPs in each of 100,000 genes) and proposed α = 5 × 10⁻⁸ for "1,000,000 independent association tests", adding that this number was likely "far larger than necessary if one allows for linkage disequilibrium"26. Estimates based on measured LD later arrived at about the same number:

  • HapMap found that testing all common variation in a 500-kb region is equivalent to about 150 independent tests in CEU and CHB+JPT and about 350 in YRI1. Scaled to the whole genome (about 6,200 such regions), that is roughly 0.9 million tests in CEU (our arithmetic).
  • Pe'er et al. estimated "a million independent tests genomewide in Europeans, and twice that number in Africans"27.
  • Dudbridge and Gusnanto estimated a genome-wide threshold of about 7.2 × 10⁻⁸ from UK data28.

The convention p < 5 × 10⁻⁸, called genome-wide significance, is therefore a statement about LD, estimated mostly in samples labeled European (such as HapMap CEU and UK data). In samples with less LD, the same set of variants corresponds to more independent tests (Session 2).

LD pruning and clumping: one SNP per correlated group

Many analyses need SNPs that are roughly independent of each other. Two procedures pick them (figure below):

  • LD pruning ignores association results. It slides a window along the genome and, whenever two SNPs in the window have r² above a threshold, removes one of them. A widely used setting is plink --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2: windows of 50 SNPs, moved 5 SNPs at a time, with an r² threshold of 0.229,15. Pruned SNPs are used for principal component analysis of ancestry and for estimating relatedness (the ancestry and inheritance pages), where blocks of correlated SNPs would otherwise count the same signal many times. Regions of long-range LD, such as the MHC described below, are usually removed as well29,30.
  • LD clumping uses association results. It starts from the most significant SNP, puts every nearby SNP in LD with it into that SNP's clump, then repeats with the most significant SNP not yet assigned. By default, PLINK's --clump starts clumps only from SNPs with p ≤ 10⁻⁴ and adds SNPs within 250 kb that have r² > 0.5 with the index SNP31. Clumping turns a list of significant SNPs into a list of roughly independent loci, and it is the classic way to choose SNPs for a polygenic score (Session 6).

LD scores: separating polygenic signal from confounding

The LD score of SNP j is ℓj = Σk r²jk, the sum of its r² with every SNP k within 1 cM, itself included (r²jj = 1)32. A SNP with a high LD score tags more of the genome. If a trait is influenced by many variants, SNPs that tag more variation pick up more of the true signal, so their χ² statistics rise with the LD score; confounding such as population stratification raises χ² roughly equally whatever a SNP's LD score. LD score regression uses this difference. It fits

E[χ²j] = N h² ℓj / M + N a + 1

where N is the sample size, M the number of SNPs, h² the SNP-heritability and a a term for confounding. The slope estimates heritability, and an intercept above 1 points to confounding32. LD score regression returns in Session 5.

LD differs across ancestries, and so do GWAS results

Because LD patterns differ between populations, so do the consequences above.

  • Fine-mapping resolution. Shorter-range LD in samples from African populations (for example the 1000 Genomes AFR samples) means fewer SNPs are swept into each peak. In simulations of five type 2 diabetes loci, adding samples that the authors label "African ancestry" to a meta-analysis gave "a marked improvement in fine-mapping resolution"25,33.
  • Polygenic scores. A score built from lead SNPs found in one population works less well in others, partly because the lead SNPs tag the causal variants with different r² under different LD patterns, and partly because allele frequencies and effect sizes differ. Averaged over 17 traits in UK Biobank, Martin et al. reported polygenic score accuracy 4.5-fold lower in the people they label "Africans" than in those labeled European34.
  • LD reference panels. LD measured in one population gives wrong answers for another (see the callout on LD references above).

In the data How LD shows up in GWAS output

GWAS summary statistics have one row per tested variant, so a single association signal appears as many neighboring rows with small p values. Before reporting loci, analysts clump them, and regional plots show each signal's LD pattern. LocusZoom colors points by r² with the reference SNP in five bins, from dark blue (r² below 0.2) through light blue, green and orange to red (0.8 and above), marks the reference SNP with a purple diamond, and shows variants without LD information in gray35. The plots on this page use a single color ramp instead, light for low r² and dark for high r².

A special region: the MHC on chromosome 6 Optional

The major histocompatibility complex (MHC) is a region of about 4 Mb on the short arm of chromosome 6, at band 6p21.3, containing about 260 genes36. Its best-known genes are the HLA (human leukocyte antigen) genes. HLA proteins carry short pieces of proteins (peptides) to the cell surface, where immune cells inspect them; this is how the immune system distinguishes the body's own proteins from foreign ones20. The region has three parts: class I (including HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C), class III, and class II (including HLA-DR, HLA-DQ and HLA-DP), shown in the figure below.

For statistical genetics, three features matter:

  • Extreme polymorphism. The IPD-IMGT/HLA database lists 46,146 HLA alleles (release 3.66), including 11,328 for HLA-B alone37.
  • Long-range LD. MHC haplotypes can stay intact over several megabases36. The MHC is one of 24 regions of long-range LD, each longer than 2 Mb, that can confound genome scans in admixed populations30, and it is usually removed before principal component analysis29.
  • Many disease associations. The MHC is associated with more diseases, mainly autoimmune and infectious, than any other region of the genome; examples include type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis and systemic lupus erythematosus36. It also matters for drug safety: carriers of the allele HLA-B*57:01 are at high risk of a hypersensitivity reaction to the HIV drug abacavir, and in a randomized trial, screening for the allele before treatment reduced immunologically confirmed hypersensitivity from 2.7% to 0%38.

Because of this long-range LD (in one detailed map, LD extended up to 1 Mb from the center of an HLA gene39), association signals in the MHC can be very wide, so they are often analyzed by imputing the HLA alleles themselves.

In the data HLA alleles in UK Biobank

UK Biobank provides imputed HLA alleles for 11 classical HLA genes (HLA-A, -B, -C, -DRB1, -DRB3, -DRB4, -DRB5, -DQA1, -DQB1, -DPA1 and -DPB1) as data-field 22182, "HLA imputation values". They were imputed with the HLA*IMP:02 software at four-digit resolution6,40.

Key points
  • Haplotypes and phase. A haplotype is the row of alleles along one chromosome copy. Genotype data add each person's two rows, so phase must be inferred: from relatives, statistically from population data (EM; SHAPEIT, Eagle, Beagle), or from long reads.
  • Origins. A new allele starts out on one haplotype; recombination erodes its association with neighboring alleles by a factor (1 − θ) per generation. Drift, bottlenecks, selection and admixture also shape LD.
  • Measures. D = pAG − pApG. D′ = D / Dmax is ±1 whenever a haplotype is missing. r² = D² / (pApapGpg) is the squared correlation of two 0/1 columns; a proxy with r² needs about N/r² people for the same power. High r² requires similar allele frequencies.
  • Patterns. LD decays with distance, but hotspots split it into blocks. LD extends over shorter distances in samples from African populations than in samples from other populations, so reference panels must match the sample.
  • GWAS. LD lets arrays and imputation cover common variants, turns one causal variant into a peak whose lead SNP may not be causal (fine-mapping, Session 4), sets the effective number of tests behind 5 × 10⁻⁸ (Session 2), and underlies pruning, clumping (Session 6) and LD score regression (Session 5).

Check your understanding

A person is heterozygous at two nearby SNPs (A/a and G/g). Why can't a genotyping array tell you their haplotypes, and what can?

The array measures each SNP separately and reports allele counts, 1 and 1, which fit both AG + ag and Ag + aG. Phase can come from relatives (for example, a trio), from statistical phasing that uses the haplotypes common in the population (EM or modern tools such as SHAPEIT), or from a sequencing read long enough to cover both SNPs.

Slide 124 asks: if any of the four haplotype counts is 0, why is |D′| = 1?

Take aG = 0. Then paG = papG − D = 0, so D = papG > 0. Every copy with G also carries A, so pG ≤ pA, which makes papG ≤ pApg. Hence Dmax = min(pApg, papG) = papG = D, and D′ = 1. The other cells work the same way: a missing Ag gives D′ = 1, and a missing AG or ag gives D′ = −1.

Two SNPs have minor allele frequencies 0.05 and 0.40. Can one be a good proxy for the other in a GWAS?

No. The largest possible r² is m1(1 − m2) / [(1 − m1)m2] = 0.05 × 0.60 / (0.95 × 0.40) ≈ 0.08, even if |D′| = 1. A test at one SNP then has roughly the power of a test at the other with only about 8% of the sample.

In a GWAS peak, the most significant SNP has r² ≥ 0.9 with twelve other SNPs. Which one is causal?

The association results alone cannot say. All thirteen SNPs have nearly the same correlation with the trait, and sampling noise decides which one has the smallest p value; the causal variant may even be untyped. Fine-mapping (Session 4) combines the association statistics with the LD matrix of the region, and sometimes with functional annotation or with samples whose LD differs, to give each variant a probability of being causal and to report a credible set.

Why is the genome-wide threshold 5 × 10⁻⁸ and not 0.05 divided by the number of variants tested (for example, 0.05 / 90 million after imputation)?

Tests of SNPs in LD are correlated. What controls the family-wise error rate is the effective number of independent tests, estimated at about one million for common variants in samples labeled European, which gives 0.05 / 10⁶ = 5 × 10⁻⁸. A Bonferroni correction for 90 million tests would be far too strict. In samples with less LD, such as samples from African populations, the effective number is larger (Pe'er et al. estimated about twice as large), so a stricter threshold is appropriate.

Why do analysts prune SNPs before PCA, but clump them after a GWAS?

Pruning ignores the trait: it thins SNPs by LD alone, so that principal components and relatedness estimates are not dominated by blocks of correlated SNPs or by long-range LD regions such as the MHC. Clumping uses the p values: it keeps the most significant SNP of each group of correlated SNPs, giving one index SNP per association signal for reporting loci or building polygenic scores.
