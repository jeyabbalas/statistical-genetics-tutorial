Biological foundations for statistical genetics

Statistical genetics analyzes a table: one row per person, one column per position in the genome where people differ, plus columns for traits such as cholesterol level or heart disease. This lecture explains where every part of that table comes from: the biology that creates genetic differences between people, and the measurement processes that turn people into rows of data. It prepares you for Lecture 2, an introduction to genome-wide association studies, and for working with UK Biobank data.

Mental model 1 · The zoom ladder

From molecule to matrix

Genotype–phenotype data are the end of a chain. A cell carries chromosomes; each chromosome is one DNA molecule; genes are segments of that DNA; variants are positions where people's DNA differs; families pass variants from parents to children; populations differ in how common each variant is and in which variants travel together; traits depend on variants and environment; and a biobank measures many people to produce a data matrix.

  • Each level explains one feature of the data matrix in mental model 2: for example, two copies of each chromosome are why every genotype is 0, 1 or 2.
  • Part A (pages 1–9) climbs the ladder through the biology. Part B (pages 10–13) shows how UK Biobank measured it. Click a box to go to its page.
Mental model 2 · The data matrix

Anatomy of the genotype–phenotype data matrix

Every analysis in this course starts from three blocks joined by a participant ID: a genotype matrix G (people × variants, entries 0, 1 or 2), covariates C, and a phenotype y. Each numbered feature below is a piece of biology or of measurement that you will learn on one of the pages.

  1. Rows are people. Each row is one participant, identified by a participant ID (in UK Biobank, the eid). Who ends up in the rows depends on how people were recruited. → who was recruited (p. 10) · the participant table (p. 13)
  2. Columns are variants, positions in the genome where people differ, ordered by their position along a chromosome. A column is named by its chromosome, position, and alleles, or by an rsID. → coordinates (p. 2) · naming variants (p. 4)
  3. Each entry counts copies of one allele: 0, 1 or 2. People carry two copies of each autosome, one from each parent, so they carry zero, one or two copies of any given allele. → two sets of chromosomes (p. 1) · genotypes as counts (p. 4)
  4. Column mean ÷ 2 is an allele frequency: the fraction of all chromosome copies in the sample that carry the counted allele. Under random mating, the counts of 0, 1 and 2 in a column follow Hardy–Weinberg proportions, which is used to check data quality. → counting alleles · Hardy–Weinberg (p. 6)
  5. Neighboring columns are correlated (green box). Alleles at nearby positions are inherited together on the same stretch of chromosome, so their columns rise and fall together. This correlation is linkage disequilibrium (LD); it is why a GWAS finds regions rather than single variants. → how LD arises · GWAS peaks (p. 7)
  6. Groups of rows differ (purple box). People whose ancestors lived in different places differ in allele frequency at many variants at once, and relatives share long identical stretches of DNA. Principal components such as PC1 capture this structure. → PCA (p. 8) · relatedness (p. 5)
  7. NA is a failed measurement: the genotyping assay could not decide the genotype for this person at this variant. The fraction of missing calls per person and per variant is one of the first quality checks. → genotyping arrays · quality control (p. 11)
  8. Fractional values are predicted, not measured (dashed orange columns). Imputation predicts genotypes at variants that were not on the genotyping array, using LD with the variants that were measured; the result is an expected allele count (a dosage) between 0 and 2. → imputation (p. 11)
  9. Covariates C are variables included in every model, such as age, sex and principal components, mainly to prevent confounding. → the analysis-ready matrix (p. 13) · confounding by ancestry (p. 8)
  10. The phenotype y is the measured trait, here LDL cholesterol. A genome-wide association study (GWAS) tests each column of G for association with y, one variant at a time, adjusting for C, and writes one row of results per variant. → measuring phenotypes (p. 12) · what a GWAS does (p. 9) · GWAS output (p. 13)

What Lecture 2 assumes you know

Lecture 2 moves fast through the ideas below. If you can explain each one in your own words, you are ready. The Lecture 2 decoder maps every slide to the matching explanation here.

  • Chromosomes come in pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes plus the sex chromosomes; a cytoband such as 8q24 names a region. Lecture 2 slides 59–60. Read: Two sets: diploid body cells, haploid eggs and sperm (p. 1); Karyotypes and the anatomy of a chromosome (p. 1).
  • DNA is two complementary strands of bases; one copy of the genome is about 3.1 billion base pairs, and positions are given on a reference genome build. Lecture 2 slides 57–58. Read: Base pairing and the double helix (p. 2); The human genome by the numbers (p. 1); The reference genome and genome builds (p. 2).
  • Changes in coding and non-coding DNA can alter (or not alter) what a gene does. Lecture 2 slides 61–63. Read: Gene regulation: when, where and how much (p. 3); What variants do (p. 4).
  • A SNP has (almost always) two alleles; reference, alternative, minor and effect alleles are different labels, and the strand matters. Lecture 2 slides 66–69. Read: Allele labels: which allele is which? (p. 4); Strands in SNP data (p. 2).
  • Genotype counts give genotype and allele frequencies; heterozygosity is the share of heterozygous people. Lecture 2 slides 70–79. Read: Counting genotypes and alleles (p. 6); Heterozygosity (p. 6).
  • Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium predicts genotype frequencies from allele frequencies and is used to detect genotyping errors. Lecture 2 slides 80–83. Read: Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (p. 6); Testing HWE, and what deviations mean (p. 6).
  • Linkage disequilibrium is the correlation between alleles at nearby variants; it is measured by D′ and r² and decays with distance. Lecture 2 slides 84–86, 107–124. Read: How LD arises and how recombination breaks it down (p. 7); Measuring LD: D, D′ and r² (p. 7).
  • Allele frequencies and LD differ between populations, and there is more genetic diversity within African populations than elsewhere. Lecture 2 slides 87–97. Read: Why allele frequencies differ between populations (p. 6); LD varies along the genome and between populations (p. 7); Human population history in brief (p. 8).
  • STRUCTURE and principal components summarize genetic similarity between people; they describe data, not fixed groups. Lecture 2 slides 98–105. Read: Measuring genetic similarity with principal component analysis (p. 8); Model-based estimates: STRUCTURE, ADMIXTURE and GrafAnc (p. 8).
  • Epidemiology studies the distribution and determinants of health in populations, using designs such as cohort and case–control studies. Lecture 2 slide 11. Read: Epidemiology in five ideas (p. 10).
  • The practical's quality-control steps: missingness, Hardy–Weinberg, heterozygosity, relatedness (IBD) and PCA. Lecture 2 slide 8. Read: Quality control: checking rows and columns against biology (p. 11); Relatives in genotype data: PLINK, KING and UK Biobank (p. 5); Measuring genetic similarity with principal component analysis (p. 8).
